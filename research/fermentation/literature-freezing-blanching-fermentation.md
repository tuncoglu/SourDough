# Literature search: Freezing/thawing and blanching before lactic acid fermentation of vegetables

Scope: cabbage/sauerkraut, kimchi cabbage, cucumbers, carrots, beets, radish.
Every number below was retrieved from a page I actually fetched (full text, publisher abstract, or
index-record abstract). Evidence tier is stated per item. "Snippet-only" = search-result text only.

---

## (b) FREEZING / THAWING BEFORE FERMENTATION

### Headline result
I found **no peer-reviewed study** that measured the effect of freezing/thawing a vegetable
**before** brining or salting on subsequent lactic acid fermentation rate, pH at 24/48 h, or
titratable acidity, for any of the target vegetables. The entire peer-reviewed kimchi freezing
literature tests freezing the **finished or already-salted** product, and it consistently reports
that freezing **suppresses** LAB and **arrests/stalls** fermentation (used for shelf-life), not
that it accelerates it. See "Could not find" section.

### (b1) Frozen storage arrests fermentation (pH/TA/RS flat for 4 weeks) — STRONG
- **Numbers (Korean white kimchi, 4 weeks frozen, 4 phase-transition regimes):**
  - pH 6.12 at day 0 → 6.05–6.18 across all treatments over 4 weeks; **not significant** (p > 0.05).
  - Titratable acidity 0.14 % (as lactic acid) at day 0 → 0.11–0.15 %; **not significant**.
  - Reducing sugar 31.28 mg/g → 30.78–33.87 mg/g; **not significant**.
  - Authors' conclusion: "frozen storage inhibits LAB growth, thereby delaying fermentation."
  - Tg′ = −34.19 °C; Tm′ = −15.50 °C.
- **Source:** Kang M, Kim E, Chung H-J, Park SH (2025). *Food Chemistry: X* 29:102610.
  doi:10.1016/j.fochx.2025.102610. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12213297/
  (full text fetched)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed; commercial kimchi from a Gwangju production facility; real vegetable;
  n = 9 (3 replicates × 3 measurements); one-way + two-way ANOVA, Tukey HSD; funded by World
  Institute of Kimchi / Korean Ministry of Science & ICT (government, no industry funder detected).
- **Confidence: strong** (measured values).

### (b2) Quantified freeze damage: ice-crystal size and drip loss — STRONG
Same paper as (b1), full text fetched:
- Ice-crystal **diameter**: 0.77 ± 0.10 mm (glassy, −45 °C, no fluctuation) → 1.09 ± 0.19 mm
  (glassy + fluctuation) → 1.38 ± 0.50 mm (rubbery, −25 °C) → **3.88 ± 1.37 mm** (partially
  thawed, −10 °C).
- Ice-crystal **area**: 0.33 ± 0.05 mm² → 1.15 ± 0.36 → 3.39 ± 0.97 → **10.86 ± 3.06 mm²**.
- Ice-crystal **number**: 73.80 → 40.60 → 24.80 → 14.40 (recrystallisation coalesces crystals).
- **Drip loss**: 23.83 ± 1.35 % immediately after freezing (i.e. ~24 % of mass lost on the first
  thaw); after 4 weeks: 28.08 % (glassy, no fluctuation), 37.59 % (glassy + fluctuation),
  44.85 % (rubbery), **51.82 %** (partially thawed).
- **Mechanism supported:** growth of large ice crystals → cell-wall/membrane rupture → drip loss
  and nutrient leakage. Authors: "It increases as a result of cell wall destruction from the
  resizing and redistribution of ice during frozen storage, accompanied by nutrient leakage."
- **Confidence: strong** for the mechanism/numbers; note this is post-fermentation kimchi, not raw
  cabbage (measured values).

### (b3) Freezing the pre-fermentation salted cabbage: thawing loss and hardness — MODERATE
- **Numbers:** abstract reports that the combination of **centrifugal dehydration + glucose**
  (CD-SG) was most effective at "minimizing thawing loss, preserving hardness, and reducing
  structural damage caused by ice crystals"; glucose outperformed trehalose as cryoprotectant;
  LAB survival improved; higher antioxidant activity and total phenolics retained; SEM showed
  reduced pore size and preserved cell structure. **No numeric table retrieved (abstract only).**
- **Source:** Kim HE, Choi YJ, Lee M-J, Kim M, Park SJ, Choi J-Y, Kang M, Park S-H, Cho J-Y,
  Lee M-A (2025). "Effects of cryoprotectants and dehydration on physicochemical properties of
  salted kimchi cabbage during frozen storage." *LWT – Food Science and Technology* 238.
  Europe PMC record IND609397284:
  https://europepmc.org/article/AGR/IND609397284
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed; salted kimchi cabbage = the exact pre-fermentation intermediate;
  World Institute of Kimchi (government); n not visible in abstract.
- **Confidence: moderate** (abstract-level numbers, no replication detail).

### (b4) Freezing temperature governs thawing loss, hardness and LAB survival — MODERATE
- **Numbers:** kimchi frozen at **−18, −40, −80 °C for 4 months**, thawed at 2 °C to core 0 °C.
  - Thawing loss was **maximum at −18 °C**, with "increased cell membrane disruption, thus
    considerably decreasing the hardness."
  - Total aerobic count and total LAB "sharply decreased" at −18 °C relative to other treatments.
  - −40 °C and −80 °C better resisted changes in pH, TA, reducing sugar, antioxidant activity,
    total phenols. Authors conclude −80 °C is ideal for long-term kimchi storage.
  - **No numeric values retrieved (abstract only).**
- **Source:** Park D, Kim M-J, Lee MJ, Park SJ, Choi J-Y, Choi M-J, Park SH (2024).
  "Physicochemical Property Analyses of Deep-Frozen Kimchi Cabbage during Long-Term Storage."
  *Journal of Food Quality* 2024:1478159. doi:10.1155/2024/1478159
  https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/1478159 (publisher HTML/PDF returned HTTP 403 Cloudflare;
  abstract retrieved via Semantic Scholar Graph API record:
  https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1155/2024/1478159 )
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, gold OA (CC-BY); n not visible in abstract.
- **Confidence: moderate** (abstract-level, direction of effect clear, magnitudes not retrieved).

### (b5) Freeze–thaw destroys >2 log LAB and changes kimchi texture; plate freezing + trehalose mitigates — MODERATE
- **Numbers (verbatim from the English abstract):**
  - "When kimchi is frozen and thawed, the amount of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast is
    usually reduced by **more than 2 logs**, and its texture including its crispness and hardness
    are changed significantly."
  - With **plate freezing (−40 °C) + plate thawing (20 °C)**: LAB of white cabbage kimchi
    maintained **>10 % of initial** count; red cabbage kimchi maintained **more than the initial**
    amount.
  - **Trehalose pretreatment (19 °Brix soluble solids)** applied to salted Chinese cabbage
    maintained kimchi **hardness and crispness**.
  - Freezing methods tested: direct freezer −25 / −40 / −60 °C, plate freezer −40 °C. Thawing:
    radio frequency (RF), plate thawing, room-temperature thawing.
- **Source:** Kim Jinse, Whang Young, Jung Hyun Kyung, Song Jin Soon, Son Jae-Yong, Choi Dong-Su,
  Kim Yong-Hoon, Lee Soo Jang, Park Chun-Wan (2020). "Freeze-Thaw Study to Minimize the Changes of
  Lactic Acid Bacteria and Texture of Kimchi." *Korean Journal of Food Engineering* (산업식품공학)
  24(4):235–242. doi:10.13050/foodengprog.2020.24.4.235
  https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART002650263
- **Quality:** KCI-listed Korean journal, 9 authors all from RDA / National Institute of
  Agricultural Sciences (government); abstract only retrieved — no n, no replication detail, no
  pH/TA data.
- **Confidence: moderate** for the >2-log LAB loss and the textural change; **weak** for the
  mitigation magnitudes (percentages given without variance).

### (b6) COMMERCIAL PRACTICE (peer-reviewed confirmation that freezing is used industrially) — MODERATE
- "The growing global demand for kimchi has led to the adoption of **frozen distribution** as a
  strategy to extend shelf-life. However, freezing can cause quality deterioration through
  ice-crystal-related structural damage, resulting in **texture softening and reduced microbial
  viability**." Centrifugal dehydration was the primary factor reducing moisture content and
  thawing loss.
- **Source:** Choi Y-J, Kim HE, Lee MJ, Kim M, Park SJ, Choi JY, Kang M, Park SH, Lee M-A (2026).
  *Scientific Reports* 16:17454. doi:10.1038/s41598-026-48286-9
  https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC13237078 ; abstract via
  https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search (record PMID 41981063)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, CC-BY; World Institute of Kimchi; n not retrieved.
- **Confidence: moderate** (qualitative claim, no numbers retrieved).

### (b7) INDUSTRY PATENT: blanch + freeze + thaw + season kimchi (non-peer-reviewed) — patent claims
- **Process, verbatim from the granted claims:** trim cabbage, cut to 1–2 kg pieces → soak in
  **2–3 % salinity brine held at 85–95 °C for 1–2 min** (blanch + simultaneous pickle) → cool and
  wash in clean water until cabbage salt ≈ **1 %** → drain **30 min** → **freeze at ≤ −18 °C** →
  thaw → mix with kimchi seasoning.
- **Stated motivations:** slash pickling time and labour; let kimchi factories buy cabbage at
  harvest and store it frozen to ride out price swings; long shelf life; and — relevant here — the
  patent explicitly claims the hot-brine blanch **kills some of the microorganisms and therefore
  DELAYS ripening** ("일정의 미생물이 소멸되어 김치를 제조하였을 경우 숙성을 지연시키는 효과로
  김치의 유통기한을 늘릴 수 있으며"), and claims the thawed cabbage "does not go soft and the
  tissue is not destroyed" ("쉬 물러지지 않고, 육질이 파괴되지 않아 뛰어난 식감").
- **Source:** KR101350194B1, "Frozen cabbage kimchi manufacturing method" / 데친 배추의
  김치제조방법. Applicant 송영근; filed 2012-05-18; granted 2014-01-09; lapsed 2019, revived;
  annual fees paid to year 13. https://patents.google.com/patent/KR101350194B1/en
  (full text fetched). Related: KR102678562B1 (blanching cabbage 95–100 °C, 1–5 min in dried-kimchi
  manufacture) — https://patents.google.com/patent/KR102678562B1/ko (snippet only).
- **Label: non-peer-reviewed patent claims; no data, no controls, no replication.**
  Notably the claimed direction is **slower** ripening, not faster fermentation.

### (b9) Texture after freezing + fermentation (indirect, model matrix) — WEAK/MODERATE
- Kelp/cabbage sauerkraut (50 % sugar kelp + 50 % white cabbage, 2 % salt, starter cultures,
  ~22 °C): raw/frozen kelp reduced firmness **before** fermentation (229.5 ± 16.1 N vs 238.4 ± 14.2 N
  raw; Kramer shear) but freezing had **no impact after fermentation** (225.7 ± 15.1 vs 225.4 ± 15.0 N).
  Blanching (100 °C, 1 min) reduced firmness significantly both before (201.0 N) and after (188.5 N)
  fermentation. Time to pH < 4.0 was ~6 days for all cabbage sauerkraut and ~9 days for
  kelp-containing sauerkrauts (no per-treatment rate data reported).
- **Source:** Akomea-Frempong S, Skonberg DI, Camire ME, Perry JJ (2021). *Foods* 10(10):2258.
  doi:10.3390/foods10102258 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8535061/ (full text fetched)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, CC-BY; n = 3 fermentation jars per treatment; real vegetable matrix but
  **seaweed is the frozen component, not the cabbage**; University of Maine.
- **Confidence: weak-to-moderate** as an analogue for cabbage (matrix mismatch — explicitly flagged).

## (c) BLANCHING (HEAT PRETREATMENT)

### (c1) 80 °C brief blanch of whole cucumbers: ≥2-log microbe kill, NO firmness loss — STRONG
- **Numbers:**
  - Blanch treatments **15, 90, or 180 s in 80 °C water**, whole cucumbers, two lots, duplicate runs.
  - **90 s blanch** consistently achieved a **minimum 2-log reduction in cucumber microbiota** and a
    **predicted 5-log reduction of *Escherichia coli* O157:H7 up to 1.1 mm into the cucumber fruit.**
  - **Blanching had no impact on tissue firmness during refrigerated storage for 1 year
    (p > 0.098).**
  - No differences in flavour-active lipid oxidation products (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal and (E)-2-nonenal.
  - **Consumers (n = 110) were unable to differentiate** control vs 90 s blanched pickles stored
    **62 days**.
  - Exocarp colour and mesocarp opacity were **preserved** by the blanch → potentially longer shelf
    life.
- **Source:** LaFountain LJ, Johanningsmeier SD, Breidt F Jr, Stoforos GN, Price RE (2022).
  "Effects of a brief blanching process on quality, safety, and shelf life of refrigerated cucumber
  pickles." *Journal of Food Science* 87(4):1475–1488. doi:10.1111/1750-3841.16112
  (abstract fetched from AGRIS: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfad5d63b8185d9cadf559 )
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed; duplicate blanching runs on two cucumber lots; texture followed for
  1 year; consumer panel n = 110; USDA-ARS / North Carolina State University (government, no
  industry funder detected).
- **Important caveat for the brief:** this is a **refrigerated ACIDIFIED** pickle (acidified, stored
  at 4 °C, "typically produced without a thermal process"), **not a lactic fermentation**. Blanching
  here is a *safety hurdle*, not a fermentation accelerator. The paper reports **no** fermentation
  rate, pH-drop or titratable-acidity data.
- **Confidence: strong** for the microbial-reduction and firmness numbers; **not applicable** as
  direct evidence on fermentation rate.

### (c2) Low-temperature blanching (50–70 °C) makes cabbage/Chinese cabbage FIRMER via PME — STRONG
- **Numbers — firmness improvement vs blanched controls, with optimum temperature/time:**
  | Vegetable | Firmness multiplier | Optimum preheat |
  |---|---|---|
  | Chinese cabbage | **1.8 ×** | 55 °C, 45 min |
  | Cabbage | **1.6 ×** | 65 °C, 15 min |
  | Bok choy | 3.0 × | 65 °C, 45 min |
  | Carrots | 2.1 × | 60 °C, 15 min |
  | Green bell peppers | 1.36 × | 70 °C, 15 min |
  | Sugar snap peas | 1.7 × | 65 °C, 30 min |
  | Broccoli | 2.9 × | 60 °C, 15 min |
  - Temperature had a **more significant effect than time**. Thermal stability and optimal
    temperature for **pectin methylesterase (PME)** in homogenates were also measured; textural
    optimum is discussed in relation to PME activity.
- **Source:** Ni L, Lin D, Barrett DM (2005). "Pectin methylesterase catalyzed firming effects on
  low temperature blanched vegetables." *Journal of Food Engineering* 70(4):546–556.
  doi:10.1016/j.jfoodeng.2004.10.009
  (abstract fetched from AGRIS: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de3ea14c5aef494fdb3112 )
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed; eight vegetables; abstract does not state n (Barrett lab, UC Davis –
  established texture/PME group); no industry funder detectable.
- **Key implication:** the naive assumption "blanching → pectin degradation → soft" is **wrong in the
  50–70 °C window**: low-temperature blanching **firms** cabbage and Chinese cabbage by 1.6–1.8×.
  Above ~80–100 °C, PME is inactivated and thermal pectin depolymerisation dominates (softening) —
  see (c3).
- **Confidence: strong** (peer-reviewed, values in abstract, measured not extrapolated).

### (c3) High-temperature blanch does soften: 100 °C/1–3 min in a real cabbage sauerkraut — MODERATE
- Kelp/cabbage mix firmness (Kramer shear, N), blanched at **100 °C for 1 min** vs raw:
  - Before fermentation: blanched 201.0 ± 12.3 N vs raw 238.4 ± 14.2 N (p = 0.00, F = 152.86).
  - After fermentation: blanched 188.5 ± 13.7 N vs raw 225.4 ± 15.0 N (p = 0.00, F = 115.94).
  - The 100 % cabbage control lost firmness across fermentation (274.4 ± 10.6 N → 233.9 ± 15.1 N).
  - In a separate salad study, 100 °C blanching of kelp reduced firmness 280.2 ± 37.8 N (raw) →
    227.1 ± 57.4 N (1 min) → 182.3 ± 32.1 N (3 min).
  - Blanching at 100 °C also caused **no significant reduction** in aerobic plate count or fungi
    (APC ≤ 3 log CFU/g for all treatments) — i.e. a 100 °C blanch applied to **vacuum-packed** kelp
    did not deliver the microbial kill seen for cucumbers in (c1).
- **Source:** Akomea-Frempong et al. (2021), *Foods* 10(10):2258, full text fetched
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8535061/ ).
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, CC-BY, n = 3; real sauerkraut matrix; **but the blanched component is
  sugar kelp, not cabbage** — cabbage was only the raw control. Treat as an analogue, and as a
  demonstration that blanch temperature is decisive.
- **Confidence: moderate** for texture; **weak** as evidence about cabbage fermentation.

### (c4) Blanching + LAB inoculation of white cabbage (sprouts): LAB 0.97 → 8.47 log CFU/g — MODERATE
- **Numbers:** white cabbage sprouts (5–7 cm) processed as raw / blanched / *Lactiplantibacillus
  plantarum*-inoculated fermentation / spontaneous fermentation; dehydrated at 40 °C.
  - Inoculum fermentation of **blanched** cabbage sprouts (IF-BCS): LAB increased from
    **0.97 → 8.47 log CFU/g**.
  - IF-BCS had the highest mineral content: Ca 447 mg/100 g dw, Mg 204, Fe 9.3, Zn 5, Cu 0.5.
  - Anti-nutrient reduction in IF-BCS: phytate **42 %**, tannin **66 %**, oxalate **53 %**; raw
    (unblanched) fermentation reduced anti-nutrients by only **32–56 %**.
  - Authors conclude *L. plantarum*-led fermentation **coupled with blanching** is the most
    promising route.
- **Source:** Layla A, Syed QA, Zahoor T, Shahid M (2024). *International Microbiology* 27(3):
  753–764. doi:10.1007/s10123-023-00426-1 (abstract via Europe PMC
  https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search , record PMID 37700156)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed; real cabbage tissue; funder = HEC Pakistan PhD scholarship (no
  industry); **blanch temperature/time not given in the abstract**, no pH/TA data, no n.
  Matrix = sprouts, dehydrated — not brined sauerkraut.
- **Confidence: moderate** — supports "blanching + starter gives a cleaner, faster LAB take-off",
  but the numbers are LAB counts, not fermentation rate, and the blanch conditions are unknown.

### (c5) 60 °C blanch of onion loosens tissue and INCREASES lactic acid production (variety-dependent) — MODERATE
- **Numbers / findings:** 12 yellow onion (*Allium cepa* L.) varieties, raw vs **blanched at 60 °C**,
  then fermented with *Levilactobacillus brevis* ŁOCK 0944.
  - Varieties that showed **morphological changes after blanching (Hysky, Centro, Dormo)** showed
    **better growth of *L. brevis* and higher lactic acid production**.
  - Blanching **loosened the tissue structure**, reducing carbohydrate content in the blanched and
    fermented onions (Alonso, Centro, Dormo, Hypark).
  - Combined process reduced polyphenol content overall, but four varieties showed no statistically
    significant difference.
- **Source:** Grzelak-Błaszczyk K, Klewicki R, Ścieszka S, Piekarska-Radzik L, Sójka M, Jaszczak M,
  Klewicka E, Fotschki B, Juśkiewicz J (2025). *Molecules* 30(14):3002.
  doi:10.3390/molecules30143002 (abstract via Europe PMC, record PMID 40733267;
  open access https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC12300999 )
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, CC-BY; 12 varieties (i.e. replicated across genotypes); funder =
  Polish National Science Center (no industry). **Onion is not on the target vegetable list** —
  this is a mechanism analogue, not direct evidence for cabbage/cucumber/carrot/beet/radish.
- **Confidence: moderate** as a mechanistic analogue (measured values, wrong matrix).

### (c6) Whole-cucumber blanching doubles refrigerated-pickle shelf life (industry-facing, non-peer-reviewed)
- "Extended shelf life for NARPs [non-acidified refrigerated pickles] was achieved by **blanching
  fresh cucumbers** and with the use of ingredients (garlic oil, irradiated spices) that have
  reduced microbial cell counts compared to typical ingredients. Using this procedure, the **shelf
  life of NARPs was more than doubled**." Also: >80 LAB isolated from NARPs characterised as
  potential biocontrol agents; salt concentration limits *Lactococcus lactis* predominance.
- **Source:** Breidt F Jr (USDA-ARS / NC State), "Microbiology of Pickled Vegetables", Pickling
  Cucumber Improvement Committee Meeting abstract (2001), NC State Cucurbit Breeding site.
  https://cucurbitbreeding.wordpress.ncsu.edu/pickling-cucumber-improvement-committee-meeting-2001/microbiology-of-pickled-vegetables/
  (page fetched)
- **Label: non-peer-reviewed conference abstract**; no n, no replication, no fermentation-rate data.
- **Confidence: weak.**

### (c7) Hot-brine "blanching-pickle" of cucumber: fast salt penetration but SLOWER fermentation — WEAK
- **Findings (abstract, translated/English as published):** cucumbers brined in (A) boiled 10 % NaCl,
  (B) boiled 20 % NaCl, (C) 20 % NaCl cooled to 25 °C after boiling. "The cucumber preserved with
  **boiled solution and high salt concentration showed a slow fermentation rate**. The **rate of salt
  penetration during brining increased**." Cutting force (texture analyser) rose rapidly during
  initial brining. Method A (boiled 10 % NaCl) scored highest in most sensory attributes.
- **Source:** Park M-W, Park Y-K, Jang M-S (1994). "Changes of Physicochemical and Sensory
  Characteristics of Korean Pickled Cucumber with Different Preparation Methods." *Journal of the
  Korean Society of Food Science and Nutrition* 23(4):634–640.
  https://www.e-jkfn.org/journal/view.html?uid=1075&vmd=Full (abstract page fetched)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed Korean journal, 1994; **abstract only — no numbers, no n, no
  replication detail**; typographic errors in the published English abstract (e.g. "205 NaCl",
  "walt") reduce confidence.
- **Confidence: weak** — but it is the closest thing found to a direct test of hot pretreatment on
  both salt uptake and fermentation rate in cucumbers, and the direction is **faster salt uptake,
  slower fermentation**.

### (c8) Korean 1993: blanching + LAB inoculation of kimchi — WEAK (abstract only)
- **Findings (Korean abstract):** *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*-inoculated group showed pH decreasing
  sharply and then staying constant, whereas the *Bifidobacterium bifidum* group's pH decreased
  gradually; **heat treatment reduced viable cell counts**; during fermentation, malic, citric and
  fumaric acids **decreased in the control but increased in the culture and all heat-treated groups**;
  water-solubility in the *B. bifidum* group was low early and rose later.
- **Source:** Park H-O, Kim Y-K, Yoon S (1993). "The Effect of Blanching and Lactic Acid Bacterial
  Inoculation on the Quality of Kimchi" (열처리 및 젖산균 접종이 김치의 품질에 미치는 영향).
  *Korean Journal of Food Cookery Science* (한국조리과학회지) 9(2):61–66.
  https://koreantk.com/ktkp2014/thesis/thesis-view.view?ctrlNo=HJRGB8-1993-v9n2-61
  (record + abstract fetched; Korean-language)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed Korean journal; **abstract only, no numerical values, no n**;
  blanch temperature/time not stated.
- **Confidence: weak.**

### (c9) Cucumber-pickle firmness baseline (context for judging blanching/freezing texture data) — MODERATE
- Fresh cucumber pickle peak penetration force **~12 N** before fermentation, falling during
  fermentation. After 6 months: Ca-acetate ≈ CaCl₂ (~10 N) > NaCl control (~8 N) > KCl > MgCl₂
  (~7 N after 30 weeks) > AlCl₃. pH ≈ 3.6 marks the point at which texture and sensory quality
  become unacceptable; most pickles fell to pH 4.6 "within a few days".
- **Source:** Yousefi M, Arianfar A, Hakimzadeh V, Rafe A (2025). *Foods* 14(3):336.
  doi:10.3390/foods14030336 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11817513/ (full text fetched)
- **Quality:** peer-reviewed, CC-BY; n = 3 jars × 3 pickles per sampling date; 20 trained panellists;
  no external funding; Iran. **No blanching or freezing treatment in this study.**
- Also useful here: this paper's introduction states that "some investigations have also been carried
  out on salt reduction, **which requires blanching prior to fermentation to keep firmness**" — but
  the supporting reference was truncated in the retrieved text, so I could **not** verify it.
- **Confidence: moderate** for the pickle firmness numbers; the "blanching prior to fermentation for
  firmness" statement is **citation-only, unverified**.

## Cross-cutting note on direction of effect
Across everything retrieved, both pretreatments are used industrially for **control and shelf life**,
not for speeding fermentation: freezing is used to **stop/lock** kimchi fermentation (b1, b3–b6), and
blanching is used as a **microbial hurdle and firmness/shelf-life tool** (c1, c6) — with the patent in
(b7) explicitly claiming that the blanch **delays** ripening. The one retrieved case where a heat
pretreatment increased lactic acid output is blanching **onion** at 60 °C (c5), and the one retrieved
cucumber case shows hot brine **slowed** fermentation while **speeding** salt penetration (c7).
