# Antimicrobial Plant Compounds in Vegetable Lactic Acid Fermentation
## Tannins (Topic 1) and Essential-Oil Compounds (Topic 2) — Quantitative Evidence with Citations

**Scope:** grape/oak/bay/horseradish leaves and tea as tannin sources; cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, carvacrol, thymol, linalool, allyl isothiocyanate (AITC) as EO actives.
**Conventions:** ppm = mg/kg ≈ mg/L in dilute aqueous brine. Where a source reports % w/v or v/v I give the % and the ppm equivalent. Density of cinnamaldehyde ≈ 1.05, carvacrol ≈ 0.976, thymol ≈ 0.96, eugenol ≈ 1.06 g/mL, so 0.1% v/v ≈ 960–1060 ppm; treated as ≈1000 ppm below.
**Confidence:** *strong* = replicated primary data or authoritative review with numbers; *moderate* = single primary study or inference across compatible studies; *weak/contested* = conflicting literature or indirect estimate.
**Gaps are flagged explicitly in §3 and §6.** I could not retrieve some paywalled sources; those are marked.

# TOPIC 1 — TANNINS IN VEGETABLE FERMENTATION

## 1.1 The active compounds — chemical identity in grape leaves

The grape-leaf pectinase inhibitor is **not** a simple ellagitannin/gallotannin. The classic USDA work characterised it as a **condensed tannin (catechin/leucoanthocyanidin polymer)**.

| Finding | Value | Source | Confidence | Study quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identity of the grape-leaf pectinase inhibitor | A water-soluble, heat-stable, **non-dialyzable, high-molecular-weight organic substance** characterised as a "condensed tannin"; positive leucoanthocyanidin + catechin tests (cherry-red colour with vanillin-HCl; λmax 495 and 500 nm) | Bell, Etchells & Smart 1965, *Botanical Gazette* 126(1):40–45 — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p80.pdf | strong | Primary; UV/vis + spot tests; compared against catechin, catechol, tannic acid standards |
| Molecular weight of grape-leaf inhibitor (freeze-dried prep.) | **17,000–20,000 Da** (freeze-dried prepn). Light-scattering on the native inhibitor: average **250,000 Da**, range ~10,000 to millions | Bell et al. 1965 (citing Porter & Schwartz 1962) — URL above | moderate | Two independent methods disagree by ~10×; aggregation likely |
| Grape-leaf inhibitor is precipitated by | Caffeine, nicotine sulfate, gelatin (classic tannin precipitants) | Bell, Etchells, Williams & Porter 1962, *Botanical Gazette* 123(3):220–223 — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p72.pdf | strong | Primary |
| Isolated as | "a condensed tannin" (Porter & Schwartz 1962); inhibitor is a **polymer of the basic structure with molecular weight of about 14,000** (flavonoid skeleton, 15 carbons) | Bell et al. 1965, p. 44 — URL above | strong | Primary |
| Named tannin papers | Porter & Schwartz 1962, *J. Food Sci.* 27:416–418, "Isolation and description of the pectinase inhibiting tannins of grape leaves"; Porter, Schwartz, Bell & Etchells 1961, *J. **Practical implication of the chemistry:** because the active principle is a **high-MW condensed tannin**, it behaves as a protein-precipitating (tanning) agent *and* a competitive enzyme inhibitor — this dual behaviour explains both the anti-pectinase effect and the interference with microbiological assay media (§1.3, §3).

## 1.3 ⭐ KEY MECHANISM AND QUANTITATIVE ENZYME INHIBITION

### 1.3.1 Mechanism
**Competitive inhibition of polygalacturonase (pectinase).** Source: Bell & Etchells 1958, *Bot. Gaz.* 119(3):192–196: *"The reaction between polygalacturonase, the substrate, and the inhibitor was that of competitive inhibition."* Inhibitor also acts on cellulase and on pectinase from mould-laden cucumber flowers. — abstracted at https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/FEDLINK_-_United_States_Federal_Collection_%28IA_CAT31359863%29.pdf *(strong)*

### 1.3.2 Inhibitor mass required — the single most useful numbers in the literature

| System | Effect | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cucumber-flower pectinase (CF)** | **5 ppm** (µg/mL) of the isolated inhibitor in the final enzyme–substrate solution reduced activity **~50%** | Bell et al. 1965, p. 44 — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p80.pdf | strong |
| Commercial pectinase **46AP** | **25 ppm** gave **>90% inhibition** | same | strong |
| Both pectinase and cellulase (CF systems) | **100 ppm consistently gave >95% inhibition** | same | strong |
| Dose–response shape | Plot of **log(inhibitor concentration) vs % reduction** of activity is a **straight line → first-order-type reaction**; "small increments … gave marked reduction" | same | strong |
| Grape-leaf extract (crude, muscadine) | **0.7 mg/mL → 28%** pectinase inhibition; **3.0 mg/mL → 92%**; **8.3 mg/mL → 95%**; **16.7 mg/mL → 95%** | Bell et al. 1962, Table 2 — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p72.pdf | strong | Crude aqueous extract, freeze-dried-plant basis. **IC50 ≈ 1–2 mg/mL crude extract** |
| Grape-leaf extract vs cellulase (CF) | 0.7 mg/mL → 26%; 3.0 → 66%; 8.3 → 99%; 16.7 → 99% | same, Table 2 | strong |
| Persimmon / dogwood / raspberry / sericea, same table | e.g. persimmon 16.7 mg/mL → 86%; sericea 16.7 → 88%, 3.3 → 64%, 1.6 → 46%, 0.3 → 14% | same, Table 2 | strong |
| **5% aqueous grape-leaf extract** | Inhibited cucumber-flower cellulase **98–99%**; commercial cellulase 19AP **50–70%** | Bell, Aurad & Etchells 1960 — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p65.pdf | strong |
| Cellulase, 10% Scuppernong leaf water extract vs 5 enzyme sources | 98%, 96%, 61%, 58%, 44% inhibition (CF, 35, 36, 19AP, XT541) — **enzyme-source dependent** | same, Table 1 | strong |

**Reading of the two datasets:** the *isolated/purified* inhibitor is active at **5–100 ppm**; the *crude leaf extract* needs **1–5 mg/mL** because the inhibitor is only ~1% of fresh leaf mass. These are consistent (≈1% × 5 mg/mL ≈ 50 ppm active principle).

|---|---|---|---|

> ### 🚩 GAP — no instrumental firmness data found
> I could **not** find any study reporting grape-leaf/tannin effects on pickle firmness in **g force, N, shear, or % firmness retention**. The entire classic USDA ARS Raleigh corpus (Bell, Etchells, Fleming, McFeeters and co-workers) measured firmness by **trained/plant-panel sensory rating**, not instrumentally. Modern instrumental firmness work in cucumbers concerns **calcium, pH, temperature and salt** (e.g. *Softening Rates of Fermented Cucumber Tissue: Effects of pH, Calcium, and Temperature*, J. Food Sci. 1995, https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1995.tb06229.x), **not** grape leaves. Anyone quoting "grape leaves increase firmness by X N" is almost certainly extrapolating. This is a genuine hole in the literature.

## 1.4 ⭐ EFFECT OF GRAPE LEAVES ON FERMENTATION RATE (acidification)

**Direct answer from the primary fermentation study:**

> *"There was **no apparent influence** [of] the grape leaf inhibitor on the character of the acid fermentation as measured by **total brine acidity, pH, and optical density of the brine**."*
> — Etchells, Bell & Williams 1958, *Food Technol.* 12:204–208 (abstracted in USDA ARS 1958, https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/64/FEDLINK_-_United_States_Federal_Collection_%28IA_CAT31359863%29.pdf) — **strong**, though this is the abstract of the primary paper, not the full data table.

**Independent modern confirmation (this is the best current dataset):**

| Treatment | Result | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cucumbers **300 g** + grapevine leaves **7 g or 14 g**, 2% saline, **14 d at 22 °C** (n = 3 replicate vessels; 12 variants) | pH fell to **3.50–3.58 after 14 days**. *"None of the experimental variants showed statistically significant (p < 0.05) changes in the kinetics of pH compared to the control system."* | Staninska-Pięta, Cyplik, Drożdżyńska & Piotrowska-Cyplik 2024, *Sustainability* 16(6):2431 — https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/16/6/2431 (PDF: https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/sustainability/sustainability-16-02431/article_deploy/sustainability-16-02431.pdf) | strong | Primary, triplicate, HPLC + plate counts + RT-qPCR + consumer panel (n=64) |
| Same study, LAB/mesophile counts | Higher counts (p<0.05) with 14 g leaves at days 1–14; leaves are themselves an LAB reservoir (grape/horseradish leaves carried culturable LAB) | same | moderate | |
| Same study, bacteriocin gene expression | Leaf addition significantly **increased** expression of plantaricin and acidocin genes (p<0.05), strongest at 14 g | same | moderate | Metabiotic-gene level, not CFU-level |
| Same study, phenolics released to brine | Grape leaves (7 g / 14 g) raised brine **ellagic acid by 105% / 148%** and **gallic acid by 91% / 131%** vs control. Horseradish leaves (14 g) raised ellagic acid **>11×** control | same | strong | HPLC quantification |
| Same study, sensory | Leaf-supplemented cucumbers scored **significantly higher overall desirability** than control (p<0.05); no significant difference between 7 g and 14 g doses | same | moderate | 9-point hedonic, penalty analysis, n=64 |

**Conclusion:** at traditional doses (≈2–5% fresh leaf w/w), grape leaves **do not measurably slow acidification**. Their effect is on *texture preservation and phenolic/bacteriocin profile*, not on fermentation kinetics. This is important: the "tannins inhibit LAB and stall the ferment" concern is **not supported** for grape leaves at culinary doses.

### 1.5.2 ⚠️ Critical assay caveat (worth stating in any write-up)
> **MRS broth proteins precipitated in the presence of 1 mM tannic acid**, forcing Reverón et al. to switch to a defined medium (RPM) for growth assays. — Reverón et al. 2013, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679024/
>
> **Consequence:** almost any "MIC of tannic acid in MRS/MRS-like broth" in the literature is confounded — the tannin is consumed by medium protein, so reported MICs are inflated and non-comparable across media. This is the single biggest reason I could not assemble a trustworthy MIC table for tannins vs LAB.

### Estimated tannin concentration in brine (my calculation — *moderate* confidence, flagged as an estimate)
Using the measured inhibitor content of muscadine grape leaf (**~1.03% of fresh weight**, §1.2) and the measured recipe dose:

- **23 g fresh leaf / kg** (7 g per 300 g) → 23 g × 1.03% ≈ **0.24 g inhibitor per kg** → **≈ 240 ppm** total extracted inhibitor *if extraction were complete*.
- **47 g fresh leaf / kg** (14 g per 300 g) → ≈ **480 ppm**.

Real brine concentrations will be **lower** because (a) extraction is partial and time-dependent, (b) the inhibitor partitions into the cucumber tissue, and (c) *V. labrusca*/table-grape leaves contain **~5× less** inhibitor than the muscadine leaf used for the 1.03% figure — with ordinary *V. vinifera* leaves the figure could be **~50–100 ppm**.

**This matters because the enzyme-inhibition threshold is 5–100 ppm (§1.3.2):** a traditional 1–2 leaves/L dose sits **right at the effective range** for the purified inhibitor — which is exactly why the traditional practice works, and why it is dose-sensitive.

### ⚠️ Drying destroys the activity — a key practical caveat
> *"The **inhibitor substance in grape leaves has been shown to be reduced more than one-half when dried** under such conditions [air-dried at 55 °C for 24 h]."* Sericea, by contrast, lost only a little activity on the same treatment.
> — Bell et al. 1962, closing paragraph — https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p72.pdf — **strong**

**Practical consequence:** commercial **dried** grape leaves (the form usually sold for pickling) may retain **<50%** of fresh-leaf inhibitory activity. Fresh leaves are strongly preferable. Heat *per se* is not the problem — the isolated inhibitor is heat-stable (stable in a boiling-water bath, losing only 12% of activity in one test) — so the loss on drying is most likely oxidative/polymerisation during drying, not thermal.

### 2.2.3 ⭐ THE PPM CALCULATION — 1 g spice per 1 kg vegetables

| Spice (1 g/kg) | Active delivered | vs **LAB MIC** | vs **spoilage-yeast MIC** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cinnamon bark** | cinnamaldehyde **6–13 ppm** | 2,100 ppm → **160–350× below** | 131 ppm (*S. cerevisiae*) → **10–22× below** |
| **Clove** | eugenol **49–125 ppm** | 2,120 ppm → **17–43× below** | 1,067–4,268 ppm → **9–87× below** |
| **Oregano (dried leaf)** | carvacrol **1–14 ppm** | 976 ppm → **70–1,000× below** | 1,952 ppm → **140–2,000× below** |
| **Thyme (dried leaf)** | thymol **2–10 ppm** | 1,000 ppm → **100–500× below** | 317–513 ppm → **30–250× below** |

> **Conclusion: 1 g of any of these spices per kg of vegetables is far too little to inhibit LAB *or* spoilage organisms.** To reach even the *yeast* MIC you would need roughly:
> - **cinnamon bark: ~10–20 g/kg** (≈1–2% of the batch)
> - **clove: ~10–30 g/kg**
> - **oregano: ~150–2,000 g/kg** (impossible)
> - **thyme: ~30–250 g/kg** (impractical)
>
> At those doses, **cinnamaldehyde is the only compound whose window is in the right direction** (§2.1.5): it would suppress yeasts/moulds at ~130 ppm while LAB (MIC ~2,100 ppm) keep fermenting.

## 2.4 The most decision-relevant single finding

Two studies, taken together, answer the question "which compounds matter at realistic spice levels?":

1. **Against LAB, all four main compounds need ~1,000–2,100 ppm** (0.1–0.2%) — Dunn 2016 / UTK 2013. **1 g spice/kg delivers 1–125 ppm.** So the answer to "do these spices stall a ferment at recipe level?" is **no** — except for clove, which is dosed far higher in kimchi.
2. **Clove is the exception and the warning case:** at **0.5–2% clove powder** in kimchi paste (~250–2,500 ppm eugenol) LAB crashed by **−3 to −5.4 log** while **yeasts and moulds were untouched** (Kang 2019). Clove therefore has the **worst** selectivity: it removes the protective LAB without removing the spoilage yeasts. **Cinnamaldehyde from cinnamon is the opposite** — yeasts and moulds are inhibited at **33–263 ppm**, ~8–64× below the LAB MIC — making cinnamon the only one of the four with a window pointing the right way.

# 4. KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR FORMULATION

1. **Grape leaves work, and the mechanism is competitive inhibition of polygalacturonase by a high-MW condensed tannin** — not a general antimicrobial effect. Effective concentration: **5–100 ppm purified inhibitor**; crude extract **IC50 ≈ 1–2 mg/mL**. (Bell & Etchells 1958; Bell et al. 1962, 1965)
2. **They do not slow the ferment.** Two independent lines of evidence — USDA 1958 (total acidity, pH, optical density unchanged) and Staninska-Pięta 2024 (pH 3.50–3.58, no significant kinetic difference at 7–14 g leaves/300 g). This resolves the "tannins inhibit LAB" worry **against** the worry, at culinary doses.
3. **Use fresh leaves, not dried.** Drying at 55 °C destroys **>50%** of grape-leaf inhibitor activity. (Bell et al. 1962)
4. **A traditional 23–47 g fresh leaf/kg dose corresponds to an estimated ~240–480 ppm extracted inhibitor** (≈50–100 ppm with ordinary *V. vinifera* leaves) — i.e. **right at the top of the 5–100 ppm effective range**. The tradition is quantitatively sensible, and dose-sensitive.
5. **LAB are tannin-tolerant and tannase-positive**: *L. plantarum* grows at **2.5 mM (4.25 g/L) tannic acid**, and all 29 strains tested carry *tanB_Lp*. (Reverón 2013; Jiménez 2014)
6. **Cinnamaldehyde is the only EO active with a favourable window**: spoilage yeasts/moulds inhibited at **33–263 ppm** vs a LAB MIC of **~2,100 ppm** — an **8–64× selective gap**. Needs **~10–20 g cinnamon bark per kg** to reach yeast-inhibitory concentrations.
7. **Clove is the trap.** At kimchi-realistic 0.5–2% it suppressed LAB by **3–5.4 log** while leaving yeasts and moulds untouched. (Kang 2019)
8. **Carvacrol and thymol are the wrong way round** for this purpose: LAB MIC ~1,000 ppm vs yeast MIC 317–3,904 ppm. *(Contested — Speranza 2023's own lactobacilli median is 39 mg/L.)*
9. **AITC is the most LAB-sparing compound found**: **no MIC against 9 LAB species up to 7,500 ppm**. If a preservative effect is wanted without disturbing the ferment, this is the lead worth pursuing — though I could not find its quantitative content in horseradish or mustard.
