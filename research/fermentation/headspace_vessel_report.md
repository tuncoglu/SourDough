# Headspace and Vessel/Cover Methods in Vegetable Fermentation
## A quantitative source report with full citations

**Scope:** sauerkraut, kimchi, brined/fermented cucumbers.
**Compiled:** research-librarian pass using web search + direct retrieval of primary PDFs.
**Confidence key:** **STRONG** = peer-reviewed / official primary document, numbers read directly from the source. **MODERATE** = single study, small n, or numbers read from a scan/mirror with some transcription risk. **WEAK-OR-CONTESTED** = marketing claim, non-experimental assertion, or sources that disagree.

**Two structural findings that shape everything below:**
1. **No official source anywhere states headspace as a percent of vessel volume.** Every official recommendation found is a *linear depth* (inches/cm from brine or cabbage surface to the rim). Any "% headspace" figure circulating in the home-fermentation world is not traceable to these sources.
2. **There is no experiment that isolates headspace volume as a variable against pH/time** in sauerkraut, kimchi, or cucumber fermentation. I searched hard for this and found nothing. That is a genuine gap, not a search failure — details in §A2.

### Do not conflate: the USDA *canning* headspace doctrine is a different number

USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, Introduction, "Controlling headspace":
> "The unfilled space above the food in a jar and below its lid is termed headspace. Directions for canning specify leaving 1/4-inch for jams and jellies, 1/2-inch for fruits and tomatoes to be processed in boiling water, and from 1- to 1-1/4-inches in low acid foods to be processed in a pressure canner. This space is needed for expansion of food as jars are processed, and for forming vacuums in cooled jars."

The ½-inch figure that appears throughout sauerkraut and pickle recipes is the **post-fermentation canning headspace**, not the fermentation headspace.

### A real internal tension in the USDA guidance
USDA simultaneously (a) requires 4–5 inches of rim above the cabbage, and (b) declares "Fermenting sauerkraut in quart and half-gallon Mason jars is an acceptable practice." A quart Mason jar filled with cabbage leaves roughly 1–2 inches of rim. **USDA does not reconcile these two statements**, and it attaches the caveat "but may result in more spoilage losses" without quantifying it. No source found quantifies the spoilage-loss penalty.

## A2. Is there EXPERIMENTAL evidence that a large headspace slows acidification?

### Direct answer: NO.

I found **no experiment** that manipulates headspace volume (or headspace O₂) as an independent variable and measures pH/time in sauerkraut, kimchi, or cucumber fermentation. No dose–response, no headspace-volume gradient study, no headspace-O₂-vs-acidification-rate study. Searches covered "headspace volume," "headspace oxygen," "aerobic vs anaerobic sauerkraut," and the oxygen-availability/*Leuconostoc*→*Lactobacillus* succession literature. **This is a clear, valuable negative finding.**

Likewise: **the claim that large headspace increases mould/kahm risk is universally asserted by extension services but is not backed by any quantified dose–response study that I could find.** The mechanism is plausible and universally repeated; the quantification does not exist in the accessible literature.

### Adjacent evidence that partially bears on the question

**(a) The onggi paper explicitly names headspace volume as a confounder.** This is the closest thing in the literature to an acknowledgement that headspace volume matters:

> Kim S. & Hu D.L. (2023) "Onggi's permeability to carbon dioxide accelerates kimchi fermentation," *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 20(201):20230034. doi:10.1098/rsif.2023.0034
> https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsif.2023.0034

Discussion, verbatim:
> "For example, **if the container is large, the carbon dioxide may be diluted to a point where bacteria cannot detect any change. Such behaviour may provide an alternative explanation for the results observed.**"

The authors raise this as a threat to their own inference — i.e. headspace volume changes headspace CO₂ concentration, and they cannot rule out that this, rather than CO₂ signaling, drives the effect. **Confidence: STRONG** (peer-reviewed, authors' own stated limitation).

**(b) Headspace composition correlates with acidification but does not establish causation.**

> Kang J.H., Lee J.H., Min S., Min D.B. (2003) "Changes of Volatile Compounds, Lactic Acid Bacteria, pH, and Headspace Gases in Kimchi, a Traditional Korean Fermented Vegetable Product," *Journal of Food Science* 68(3):849–854. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.2003.tb08254.x

Kimchi stored at **5 °C**, from day 2 to day 27: **pH fell 4.3 → 3.8**, **headspace O₂ fell 14.3% → 1.3%**, **headspace CO₂ rose 27.7% → 45.3%**. LAB rose 3.1 → 4.5 (×10⁸ cfu/mL) over 17 d, then fell 40% by day 27.
**Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers (peer-reviewed; abstract retrieved from Crossref publisher deposit). **But this is a time-series correlation, not a headspace manipulation.**

**(c) Sauerkraut's own headspace is displaced by its own CO₂ faster than any vessel design matters.** Preuss et al. 1928 measured the barrel headspace going from air to ~98% CO₂ within 40–160 h (§A3/A4). In a 136 kg barrel, the fermentation itself purges the headspace. This is why one might expect headspace volume to matter little in large vessels and potentially a lot in small jars — but **that prediction has not been tested.**

**(d) Vessel comparison data exists but confounds headspace with cover material.** Satora & Strnad 2024 (full data in §B1) found glass jars with airlock lids acidified substantially faster than stoneware vessels covered with LDPE foil. Headspace is one of several confounded differences (material, cover, geometry, O₂ ingress rate). **It cannot be read as a headspace-volume result.**

## A3. The counter-consideration: CO₂ production, headspace CO₂, and why cucumbers get purged

### A3.1 CO₂ production during SAUERKRAUT fermentation — quantitative

**The definitive quantitative source, and it is from 1928:**

> Preuss L.M., Peterson W.H., Fred E.B. (1928) "Gas Production in the Making of Sauerkraut," *Industrial & Engineering Chemistry* 20(11):1187–1190. doi:10.1021/ie50227a021
> https://doi.org/10.1021/ie50227a021
> Full text mirror consulted: https://datapdf.com/gas-production-in-the-making-of-sauerkraut192c7bc2810c0b7a596dee83c9665ce9197121.html

**Apparatus:** a **58-gallon (219.8 L)** all-metal barrel, paraffin-coated, leak-tested with a manometer. **300 lb (136 kg)** shredded cabbage per experiment (~**306 lb / 138.8 kg** in Exp III), ~**2.5% NaCl**. Gas collected over mercury and analyzed on a Burrell gas apparatus.

**Headspace, stated explicitly:**
> "With the cabbage and stones in place, there still remained about **10 inches (25.4 cm) space in the barrel**."

(This is the only *measured* fermentation headspace figure I found in the vegetable-fermentation literature. It is 25.4 cm in a 219.8 L vessel.)

**Total gas produced and final acidity:**

| Exp. | Cabbage | Temp. | Duration | Total gas | Derived L gas / kg cabbage | Final acidity (as lactic) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 300 lb (136 kg) | cold; 6.1 °C at removal | 260 h | **212.3 L** | 1.56 | 0.26% |
| II | 300 lb (136 kg) | steamed to 20 °C; 17.0 °C at removal | 423 h | **328.9 L** | 2.42 | 1.90% |
| III | 306 lb (138.8 kg) | 25–28 °C, then raised | 305 h (+~59 L over 10 more d) | **301.4 L** | 2.17 | 2.24% |
| IV | 300 lb (136 kg) | 25–28 °C | 141 h | **414.7 L** | 3.05 | 2.1% |

"L gas / kg cabbage" is **my arithmetic** from the paper's reported totals; the totals themselves are the paper's. **Confidence: MODERATE–STRONG.** The 1928 paper is primary and peer-reviewed for its era; I read it via a text mirror of the scan, so exact digits carry a small transcription risk.

**Gas composition:**
> "The gas evolved during the formation of sauerkraut is **almost 100 per cent carbon dioxide**." Reported values 98.0–99.6% CO₂; O₂ falling to 0.2–0.4%.
> "**Most of the gas formed is given off within 40 to 160 hours** after the cabbage is packed into the container."
> Hydrogen and methane: "if present at all, the quantities of hydrogen and methane are **insignificant**."
> Exp IV: "**All the residual air in the barrel had been displaced at the end of the second day**."

**Acid/gas parallelism (Exp IV):** acidity reached **0.89% at 40 h**; rose **0.5% in the next 24 h**; gas evolution "was not very great after 64 hours"; gas ceased at 141 h / 414.7 L; final 2.1% at 11 days.

**A widely-cited 1928 mechanism claim worth flagging:** the authors report that mannitol-forming bacteria isolated from sauerkraut "convert **20 to 25 per cent of the glucose and other sugars into ethyl alcohol and slightly higher percentages of the sugars into carbon dioxide**," and that 20% of 105 picked colonies were gas-positive (vs 70% reported by Priem, Peterson & Fred for commercial kraut fermented below 10.5 °C). **Confidence: MODERATE** (old methodology).

### A3.2 CO₂ production during CUCUMBER fermentation and brining

> Fleming H.P., Etchells J.L., Thompson R.L., Bell T.A. (1975) "Purging of CO₂ from cucumber brines to reduce bloater damage," *Journal of Food Science* 40(6):1304–1310.
> ARS Pickle Pubs scan: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p125.pdf

> Humphries E.G. & Fleming H.P. (1988) "Purging CO₂ from Cucumber Fermentation and Storage Tanks," *Applied Engineering in Agriculture* 4(2):166–171.
> ARS Pickle Pubs scan: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p209.pdf

**CO₂ accumulation and the critical threshold:**
- "Accumulated production from all sources up to **120 mg of CO₂/100 mL in brine** have been reported (Fleming et al., 1973)" [Humphries & Fleming 1988].
- "Maximum rates of CO₂ production in the brine … are probably on the order of **25 mg/100 mL per day**" [ibid.].
- "The critical concentration for bloater damage is inversely related to temperature and salt concentration and **ranges from 30 to 50% saturation; it may be as low as 25 mg CO₂/100 mL of brine** (Fleming, 1979)" [ibid.].
- "Hydrostatic pressure varies with tank depth and **increases the tolerance level of CO₂ in the brine to near saturation levels (about 100 mg/100 mL at 28 °C and 25 °C) at depths greater than 170 cm**" [ibid.].

### A3.3 Quantitative PURGING parameters

**Lab / 5-gallon pail scale — Fleming et al. 1975:**

- Preliminary solubility work: 18 L distilled water in an 11-in. diam × 35-in. high glass chromatographic jar; sparger = ¼-in. i.d. tube perforated with **37 holes of a 27-gauge needle**, fastened to a plexiglass plate at the jar bottom; "**Nitrogen was bubbled through at a rate of 740 ml/min**."
- **Target:** "immediate purging on a continuous basis is preferable, with the **CO₂ concentration being maintained below 20 mg/100 ml brine** (Etchells et al., 1973)."
- **Effective range:** "**Continuous purging at nitrogen flow rates of 5–100 ml/min** reduced and maintained CO₂ concentrations in the brine below the initial values."
- "**Purging of brines intermittently for 2 hr each day at 425 ml nitrogen/min** also reduced CO₂ levels."
- Bubbler design note: "Smaller bubbles because smaller bubbles provide more surface area per unit of purging gas than larger bubbles, and should be more efficient in removing the CO₂ from the brine."

**Gas usage table (5-gal pails), Fleming et al. 1975, Table 2:**

| Purging method | Flow rate (ml/min) | Time purged (hr/day) | Gas volume used (L/day) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intermittent | 425 | 2 | **51.0** |
| Continuous | 5 | 24 | **7.2** |
| Continuous | 25 | 24 | **36.0** |
| Continuous | 100 | 24 | **144.0** |

**Bloater outcome, controlled fermentation, 5-gal pails, 32 °C — Fleming et al. 1975, Table 3:**

| Treatment | Flow (ml/min) | Total bloaters (%) | Max CO₂ in brine (mg/100 mL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| N₂ continuous | 5 | 15 | 41 |
| N₂ continuous | 25 | 8 | 41 |
| N₂ continuous | 100 | 8 | 40 |

**Natural fermentation, closed 5-gal pails, 32 °C — Table 4:** N₂ at 100 ml/min gave 39% total bloaters vs **51% unpurged** (Exp I); N₂ intermittent 425 gave 25% vs **76% unpurged** (Exp II). Max brine CO₂ 33 vs 90 mg/100 mL (Exp I) and 67 vs 103 mg/100 mL (Exp II).

**A critical quality caveat on air purging:** air purging gave the *lowest* bloater counts (1–2%), but "**the skins and internal flesh of air-purged cucumbers were gray**" and they had "a more cured appearance … notably different from the nonpurged cucumbers (P < 0.05)." Nitrogen-purged and nonpurged cucumbers "were not noticeably different in color." So air is not a drop-in substitute.

**Commercial tank scale — Humphries & Fleming 1988:**
- Tanks studied: **fiberglass, 3.63 m diameter, 3.89 m overall height, 35° slope cone top with center manhole, total capacity 31,420 L.** Also: "typically, open-top wood, fiberglass, or polyethylene tanks up to **40,000 L** capacity are employed for fermentation which may require up to **30 days**; the storage period (in the same tank) is usually less than one year but may be several years."
- **Routine commercial rate:** "On the order of **1.133 m³/h (40 SCFH)** are routinely employed on large tanks (Fleming et al., 1983)." (= **18.9 L/min**, i.e. ≈0.036 L gas per L tank per hour for a 31,420 L tank.)
- **Headspace/exhaust gas CO₂ at that rate:** "The 1.133 m³/h (40 SCFH) N₂ purging rate, CO₂ in the exhaust gases from the tank **ranged between 1 and 3% (by volume)** as measured by the Bacharach FYRITE® analyzer, suggesting that **excess N₂ was being used**."
- **Under-purging is what fails:** "Tank III, at the lower purging rate [1/20 nominal, ≈0.057 m³/h], indicated increasing CO₂ levels of **71 and 62 mg/100 mL** for locations 2 and 3 respectively, with a **high incidence of cucumber bloating**." By contrast Tanks II and IV at 1.133 m³/h showed "a decreasing concentration, especially during the latter stages of fermentation, suggesting that the purging system removed CO₂ faster than it was generated."
- **Headspace CO₂ measurement method (useful detail):** "the headspace above the brine was accessible via ports in the manhole cover/vent stack. Samples of the headspace gas were analyzed for CO₂ by a portable **Bacharach FYRITE® gas analyzer**… The analyzer was equipped to read **0 to 20% CO₂ (by volume) and could be read to the nearest 1%**."
- **Brine circulation coupling:** "brine circulation rates of **4,000 L/h** can be attained at gas flow rates of **0.85 m³/h** while CO₂ levels in the brine are maintained at **30 mg/100 mL, or less**."
- **Sparger hardware:** "a gas supply line that releases gas through a sparger unit located near the bottom of the eduction tube… **sparger (usually 20 μm porosity)**"; the tank unit was "constructed from **10.2 cm (4 in.) PVC pipe**, Porex gas diffuser."

**Confidence: STRONG** on all of the above (two primary ARS papers; I read the scanned pages directly, including tables).

### A3.4 Why commercial cucumber fermentation PURGES CO₂ while sauerkraut relies on a CO₂ blanket

**Directly sourced reasons:**

1. **The damage mechanism is internal to the fruit, and driven by the brine CO₂ concentration.** Fleming et al. 1975: the CO₂ "accumulation in the brine results from that produced **within, and diffusing from, the cucumbers** in addition to that produced by microbial metabolism within the brine." Corey, Pharr & Fleming (1983, *J. Food Sci.* 48(2):389–393) established "Role of gas diffusion in bloater formation of brined cucumbers" — CO₂ diffuses into the cucumber interior faster than N₂ (the predominant gas in fresh cucumbers) can diffuse out, so the fruit inflates.
2. **Deep tanks put the most vulnerable fruit where hydrostatic protection is lowest.** Humphries & Fleming 1988: hydrostatic pressure raises CO₂ tolerance to ~100 mg/100 mL below 170 cm depth, but "**hydrostatic pressure … increases the buoyancy forces on cucumbers near the top of the tank** (restrained below the free liquid surface) and can lead to physical damage." And in a closed tank, "the total CO₂ … will funnel all of the CO₂ being removed into a relatively small space at the top of the tank that may result in **abnormally high concentrations of CO₂ (in gas form) at a position in the tank where hydrostatic pressure is lowest and cucumbers are most vulnerable**."
3. **Economics.** Bloaters are hollow, unusable fruit — a direct yield loss on a product held in brine for up to 30 days fermentation plus up to a year of storage, in 31,000–40,000 L tanks. "Bloater damage results in a loss of yield and quality of fermented pickle products."
4. **The commercial process is a controlled fermentation with a defined CO₂ budget.** Etchells et al. developed "the controlled fermentation process" — chlorination, acidification to pH 4.7, addition of sodium acetate buffer, inoculation with *L. plantarum*, and CO₂ removal by purging — precisely so that the CO₂ balance can be managed.

**Sourced reasons why sauerkraut is different:**
5. **Sauerkraut generates its own blanket, fast.** Preuss et al. 1928 measured the barrel headspace reaching ~98% CO₂ within 40–160 h, with O₂ down to 0.2–0.4%. Shredded cabbage has no gas-retaining internal structure analogous to a hollow cucumber, so there is no bloater defect to prevent. The CO₂ blanket is protective (it excludes O₂ and suppresses mould), so purging it away would be counterproductive.
6. **Fleming et al. 1975 found direct evidence of this protective effect:** "When pails were opened, all brines were free of surface growth and had a clean, typical brine-stock odor, **even when air was the purging gas**" in controlled fermentations — but for *natural* fermentations, air purging is a problem because (Humphries & Fleming 1988) "Air as a replacement for the more expensive N₂ has been successfully used by some processors (Costilow et al., 1976) but **introduces large quantities of O₂ into an essentially anaerobic process that could stimulate the growth of undesirable aerobic microorganisms** (Fleming, 1979)."

**My synthesis (flagged as inference, not sourced):** the deeper reason is that the two processes optimise different things. Cucumber brining optimises *fruit integrity* and treats CO₂ as a contaminant to be stripped; sauerkraut fermentation optimises *anaerobiosis and shelf stability* and treats CO₂ as a useful blanket. A sauerkraut maker has no analogue of the bloater defect, so the entire ARS purging literature has no sauerkraut counterpart. **I found no study applying the ARS CO₂-purging framework to sauerkraut — the question appears not to have been asked.**

|---|---|---|
| pH | **4.3** | **3.8** |
| Headspace O₂ | **14.3%** | **1.3%** |
| Headspace CO₂ | **27.7%** | **45.3%** |

Also: headspace volatiles increased 20.0% over 7 d, then decreased 8.3% from d 7 to d 27. LAB rose from 3.1 to 4.5 (×10⁸ cfu/mL) over 17 d, then decreased 40% from d 17 to d 27. 40 volatile compounds identified, including 18 sulfur compounds.

**Confidence: STRONG** for the reported values (peer-reviewed; abstract retrieved from the publisher's Crossref deposit). Note the abstract reports only the day-2 and day-27 endpoints — **the full daily time-series is behind a paywall and I could not retrieve it.** Note also that headspace O₂ was already down to 14.3% by day 2, i.e. the headspace is substantially displaced very early.

### (3) KIMCHI — onggi vs hermetic glass, CO₂ time course

> Kim S. & Hu D.L. (2023) *J. R. Soc. Interface* 20(201):20230034. doi:10.1098/rsif.2023.0034

Salted Napa cabbage (2 wt% brine soak, 6 h), no seasonings, **n = 3 per container**, CO₂/pressure/O₂ sensors (Vernier), 25 °C, dark. Measured the **time course of CO₂ partial pressure** in onggi (unglazed porous earthenware) vs hermetic glass.

- Onggi's permeable wall "clearly permits carbon dioxide to escape, resulting in a **slower increase in carbon dioxide and a lower plateau level**."
- "Porosity thus acts as a **safety valve for carbon dioxide, maintaining its levels at less than half the values of hermetically sealed containers**."
- **CO₂ generation rate: glass 0.552 mmol/h; onggi 0.695 mmol/h** — onggi generated **26% more** CO₂. One-tailed t-test, **P = 0.0498**.
- **Per unit mass of cabbage: glass 2915 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹; onggi 3670 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹.**
- Literature comparison cited in the paper: fermenting kimchi in glass containers generated **2531 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹** at 25 °C and 2 wt% salt.
- Gas permeability: glass container **0.796 × 10⁻¹⁸ m²**; onggi **1.701 × 10⁻¹⁸ m²**. Gas permeance **1.70 × 10⁻⁵ mol kPa⁻¹ m⁻¹ h⁻¹** (literature range for unglazed onggi: 0.68–19.75 × 10⁻⁵).
- Liquid permeability k = (4.58 ± 1.10) × 10⁻¹⁵ m²; hydraulic conductivity K = (4.58 ± 1.10) × 10⁻⁸ m s⁻¹.
- Water loss through the wall: 0.75 ± 0.18 g h⁻¹ (R² = 0.989) from 2000 g water.
- Pore diameters 1–100 μm; liquid breakthrough pressure Pc ≈ 4.1 kPa in the onggi (vs 24.9 kPa computed for the material).
- After 24 h of kimchi fermentation, liquid volume increased by 500 mL (~10% of initial liquid, ~half the volume of the 1 kg cabbage).
- Model fit R² = 0.954 (glass) and 0.850 (onggi).

**Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers (peer-reviewed, n = 3, sensors, stated statistics). **Caveat: the primary outcome is a CO₂ generation rate, not a pH trajectory.** The paper's causal claim — that lower headspace CO₂ *accelerates* fermentation — is an inference from a model, and the authors themselves flag the headspace-dilution confound (§A2a). P = 0.0498 is marginal, and it is a **one-tailed** test on n = 3.

### (4) CUCUMBER — tank exhaust gas, Humphries & Fleming 1988
At 1.133 m³/h N₂ purging, CO₂ in the gas leaving the tank was **1–3% by volume** (Bacharach Fyrite). This is the exit gas, not a quiescent headspace sample, but it is the only measured gas-phase CO₂ figure for cucumber tanks I found. **Confidence: STRONG** for the measurement; **note that a low headspace CO₂ here is a *consequence of purging*, not a natural state.**

### Explicit gap
**No study was found that reports a full headspace N₂/O₂/CO₂ time-series for a fermenting CUCUMBER vessel.** N₂ is essentially never measured; the ARS work measures dissolved CO₂ in brine and, latterly, exhaust-gas CO₂. If you need headspace N₂ data, it does not exist in the accessible literature.

# TOPIC B — VESSEL AND COVER METHOD SIDE-BY-SIDE DATA

## B1. Side-by-side vessel/cover comparisons with pH/time data

### The one real study: glass jar (airlock) vs stoneware vessel

> Satora P. & Strnad S. (2024) "The Influence of Fermentation Vessels on Yeast Microbiota and Main Parameters of Sauerkraut," *Applied Sciences* 14(1):236. doi:10.3390/app14010236
> https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/14/1/236 · open-access PDF: https://res.mdpi.com/d_attachment/applsci/applsci-14-00236/article_deploy/applsci-14-00236.pdf

**Design:** Galaxy cabbage, shredded to 2.5 mm, **4 kg per vessel**, **2.5% (w/w) NaCl**, **20 °C**, **14 days**, **each variant in triplicate**. Sampling on days 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 14, under nitrogen in an anaerobic chamber.
- **Glass jars:** "in which anaerobic conditions are quickly created," closed with lids equipped with glycerol-filled fermentation tubes (= airlocks).
- **Stoneware vessels:** Browin® stoneware, surface covered with **double-layer LDPE foil (O₂ permeability 50–150 cm³ × mm × m⁻² × day⁻¹ atm⁻¹)** with pressure stones on top.

**Headline results:**
- **Glass jars reached the "finished" threshold (pH ~3.8 AND ~1.5% lactic acid) on day 10. Stoneware had only reached pH 3.86 by day 14 and 10.12 g/L lactic acid.** The vessel with the *faster* establishment of anaerobiosis acidified *faster and further*.
- "sauerkraut obtained in stoneware vessels was characterized by the presence of a **larger amount of yeast, including those considered spoilage, such as *Rhodotorula* and *Wickerhamomyces***."
- Stoneware "contained **50% less lactic acid** and **a few times more acetic acids**" — the acetic acid data confirm this at day 14 (2.07 vs 0.89 g/L, **2.3×**).
- **Oxygen-indicator organisms:** "*Wickerhamomyces anomalus* may be an **indicator of the presence of oxygen** during sauerkraut fermentation, while *Clavispora lusitaniae* may indicate **anaerobic conditions**." *C. lusitaniae* was first observed on day 4 of fermentation in glass jars only.

**Confidence: STRONG** for the data (peer-reviewed, n = 3, replicated, defined conditions, full statistics reported).
**Caveats that limit generalisation:** (i) a single cabbage variety at a single temperature; (ii) **only two vessel types**, and the "stoneware" arm differs from the "glass" arm in *material, cover, geometry and headspace simultaneously* — so this cannot be read as a headspace or airlock result in isolation; (iii) the paper is framed around yeast microbiota, and the organic-acid/pH results are secondary; (iv) units are g/L, not g/kg, which complicates comparison to other sauerkraut literature.

### Studies I searched for and did NOT find

**No side-by-side study was found comparing any of the following pairings with pH/time data:**
- open crock vs airlocked jar
- plastic bag with weight vs plate-and-jar weight
- **vacuum-sealed bag vs jar**
- **Harsch / water-seal crock vs any other vessel**
- closed bucket vs crock
- Fido jar vs airlock vs silicone lid

I also found **no university extension side-by-side fermentation trial** and **no thesis or dissertation** on vessel comparison with pH data. This is a real and notable gap: **the vessel advice in the extension literature is not backed by comparative experimental data.** The Satora & Strnad 2024 study above appears to be the *only* published side-by-side vessel comparison with a pH trajectory for sauerkraut.

### A near-miss that is worth knowing about
> Kim S. & Hu D.L. (2023), *J. R. Soc. Interface* 20(201):20230034 — onggi vs hermetic glass, salted Napa cabbage. **Vessel comparison with a measured CO₂ time course but no pH trajectory** (see §A4.3). It is the only other vessel side-by-side I located.

## B2. Harsch crock / water-seal claims vs independent evidence

### The manufacturer's claims — COULD NOT BE RETRIEVED

**Finding: the Harsch manufacturer/distributor web presence is gone.** `harschcrock.com`, `harsch-crock.com` and `gaertopf.com` all fail DNS resolution (curl exit 000). The Wayback Machine CDX index for `harschcrock.com*` returns **only a single capture, `robots.txt` from 2005-08-26.** There is no archived product page with the manufacturer's claim language.

**I therefore cannot give you a verbatim, URL-cited manufacturer claim.** Anyone quoting Harsch's exact wording should be asked for the archived URL, because I could not find one.

### The closest thing to the claim, from a published book (not the manufacturer)

> Amanda Feifer, *Ferment Your Vegetables* (Quarto, 2015), excerpt "Tips and Guidelines: Fermentation Crocks," reprinted at https://www.myfermentation.com/non-alcoholic/tips-guidelines-crocks-zerz1909zsta

Verbatim:
> "Water-sealed crocks, sometimes referred to under the brand name Harsch crocks, make for a very easy ferment… **No air enters the crock once the water 'seal' is poured, but CO₂ created during fermentation easily bubbles out. Batches made in water-sealed crocks rarely accrue a layer of Kahm yeast during fermentation.**"

Same source, on open crocks:
> "Open crocks that are not covered with a fitted lid and left undisturbed are **prone to developing a layer of Kahm yeast**… checking the surface of the crock every few days and skimming off any Kahm yeast that may have appeared on the surface is a good bet."

**Confidence: WEAK-OR-CONTESTED.** This is a book assertion by a practitioner, not a measurement, and not the manufacturer.

### Independent experimental test: NONE FOUND

**Verdict on the water-seal claim: UNVERIFIED.**

I searched English and German ("Gärtopf," "Wasserverschluss," "Milchsäuregärung," "Gärgefäß Vergleich," plus Stiftung Warentest / Ökotest and German food-science sources). I found:
- **no laboratory study** comparing a water-seal crock to an airlock jar or open crock with pH, acidity, or microbial enumeration;
- **no university extension trial**;
- **no thesis**;
- **no German-language food-science paper** testing the water seal.

The claim is therefore **unverified, not contradicted** — the honest status is "plausible, mechanically sensible, and untested."

### Adjacent evidence that partially bears on it

1. **Cover method does change the yeast microbiota and the acid profile** — Satora & Strnad 2024 (§B1) is the strongest available proxy. Their foil-covered stoneware grew *Wickerhamomyces anomalus* (an oxygen indicator) and made 2.3× more acetic acid than airlocked glass jars. That is consistent with the *direction* of the water-seal claim (better air exclusion → different, less oxidative microbiota) — **but their stoneware was foil-covered, not water-sealed**, and the study cannot distinguish cover from vessel material.
2. **A water seal is a one-way valve, and its behaviour is pressure-dependent.** The onggi paper measured a liquid breakthrough pressure of ≈**4.1 kPa** for its porous wall — a reminder that "one-way gas escape" designs all operate against a finite back-pressure. A water-seal moat's bubble point depends on the water column depth; **I found no source that measures or specifies it for any commercial crock.**
3. **Extension guidance treats scum removal as normal and acceptable,** not as evidence of failure. USDA Guide 6, Clemson, Penn State and OSU all instruct the user to check and skim. **OSU PNW 355:** "Check the container several times a week and promptly remove surface scum or mold." **USDA:** "If you use jars as weight, you will have to check the kraut two to three times each week and remove scum if it forms." The implication is that the water seal's benefit is **convenience (set-and-forget), not a safety or acidification requirement** — but no source quantifies either benefit or risk.
4. **A directly relevant caution from Penn State:** "Some recipes recommend saving these leaves to use as a cover for the fermenting sauerkraut, but the outer leaves are more likely to be contaminated with mold spores and should not be used for this purpose." — i.e. Penn State's concern is *inoculum*, not headspace.

## B3. Vacuum fermentation of vegetables — quantitative work

### What exists

**Noma's method (practitioner, not experimental):**
> Noma Projects, "Lacto-Fermentation: noma's Not So Secret Ingredient" — https://nomaprojects.com/blogs/journal/lacto-fermentation-nomas-not-so-secret-ingredient
> "The process is simple: **Weigh your ingredient, add 2% salt by weight, and wait. How many days depends on how sour you want the final product to be.**"

Underlying book: Redzepi R. & Zilber D., *The Noma Guide to Fermentation* (Artisan, 2018).
**Confidence: WEAK for quantitative purposes.** The accessible web source gives **2% salt by weight** and no pH, temperature, or day-count figures. The book gives per-ingredient day counts, but **I could not verify any pH/time data from an accessible source.** Do not treat "Noma vacuum fermentation" as a quantified protocol without the book in hand.

**Vacuum-packaged kimchi — a substantial Korean literature exists, but I could not retrieve the numeric pH data:**
- "Effect of Packaging Material on Quality of Kimchi During Storage," *Korean Journal of Food Science and Technology* 26(1):62 (1994). https://koreantk.com/ktkp2014/thesis/thesis-view.view?ctrlNo=SPGHB5-1994-v26n1-62
- "Quality Changes of Commercial Kimchi Products by Different Packaging Methods," *Korean J. Food Sci. Technol.* (1995). https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO199503042017097.pub
- Kwon H.R. et al., "Estimation of pressure and volume changes for packages of kimchi, a Korean fermented vegetable," *Packaging Technology and Science* 10(1):15–25 (1997). doi:10.1002/(SICI)1099-1522(199701/02)10:1<15::AID-PTS378>3.0.CO;2-A
- "Use of Laser-Etched Pouches to Control the Volume Expansion of Kimchi Packages During Distribution," *Journal of Food Science* 82(8):1876–1884 (2017). doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13798 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28678333/

**Confidence: WEAK as delivered here** — I verified these citations exist and are on-topic, but I did not retrieve their numeric pH/acidity data.

### The important conceptual point about vacuum fermentation

Vacuum sealing **removes the headspace gas entirely**, but it does not eliminate the headspace problem — it converts it into a **package-volume problem**. CO₂ generated by the fermentation has nowhere to go, so the package inflates. That is exactly what the Korean kimchi-packaging literature is about ("volume expansion," "pressure and volume changes," "laser-etched pouches to control the volume expansion"). **So vacuum fermentation is not "zero headspace" — it is a sealed system with a growing internal gas phase whose pressure is set by the package's compliance.** I found no study that measures both that internal pressure/volume and the pH trajectory in the same experiment for a vegetable ferment.

### Explicit gap
**No peer-reviewed study was found reporting pH/time for vacuum-sealed-bag sauerkraut with a jar or crock control.** For a technique this widely promoted in the home and restaurant fermentation world, the absence of a controlled comparison is striking.

# What does not exist — explicitly

These are the findings I would most want a downstream user to know, because they are the questions the literature has not answered:

1. **No headspace recommendation expressed as a percent of vessel volume** exists in any official source (USDA, any extension service). All are linear depths.
2. **No experiment manipulates headspace volume and measures pH/time** in sauerkraut, kimchi, or cucumber fermentation. The central premise of home-fermentation headspace advice is untested.
3. **No quantified dose–response linking headspace to mould/kahm incidence.** The risk is universally asserted and never measured.
4. **No side-by-side comparison of the vessel types people actually argue about** — open crock vs airlock jar vs bag-with-weight vs vacuum bag vs water-seal crock vs bucket. Only glass-jar-vs-stoneware (Satora & Strnad 2024) exists.
5. **No test of a water-seal / Harsch crock against any control** in any language I searched. The claim is unverified, not refuted.
6. **No peer-reviewed controlled study of vacuum-sealed-bag sauerkraut** with pH/time data.
7. **No data at all on Fido jars, silicone airlock lids, or waterless valves.**
8. **No headspace N₂ measurements** in any vegetable fermentation; O₂/CO₂ data exist only for sauerkraut (1928), kimchi (2003, 2023) and cucumber tank exhaust gas.
9. **No Harsch manufacturer claim is retrievable** — the site is dead and essentially unarchived, so any quotation of it should be treated as unverifiable.
10. **No sauerkraut analogue of the ARS CO₂-purging research.** The entire quantitative purging literature is cucumber-specific, and the reason is that sauerkraut has no bloater defect — but nobody appears to have tested whether purging would help or harm sauerkraut.

# Method note and limitations

- Primary PDFs were retrieved directly (ARS Pickle Pubs scans, the MDPI open-access PDF, the Royal Society accepted manuscript, the Internet Archive USDA Complete Guide, the NMSU and Wisconsin extension PDFs, the Noma web article, the Feifer book excerpt).
- The two ARS Pickle Pubs papers from 1975 and 1988 are **image-only scans with no text layer**. Their tables were read by rendering pages to images and reading them visually. Digits in those tables should be treated as high-confidence but worth spot-checking against the original if a number is load-bearing.
- The 1928 *Ind. Eng. Chem.* paper was read via a third-party text mirror of the scan, not the publisher PDF. Its early-table rows carry transcription risk and are flagged MODERATE above.
- Wiley, MDPI (HTML), ScienceDirect and Royal Society (HTML) block automated retrieval. Where possible I substituted the publisher's open-access PDF (MDPI `res.mdpi.com` route), the Crossref abstract deposit (Kang et al. 2003), or an accepted-manuscript PDF (Kim & Hu 2023).
- The ARS "Fermented and Acidified Vegetables" bibliography at https://www.ars.usda.gov/southeast-area/raleigh-nc/fsmqhru/docs/fermented-acidified-vegetables-bibliography/ indexes **451 full-text PDFs** and is the single best entry point for further work on cucumber fermentation; several cited-but-unread items in §A3 are available there.
