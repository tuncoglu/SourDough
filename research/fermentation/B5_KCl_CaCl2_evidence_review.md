# PART B5a + B5b — Literature-evidence review
## KCl partial substitution for NaCl, and CaCl2 / calcium addition in vegetable lactic acid fermentation

Scope: sauerkraut, kimchi, paocai/suancai, cucumber pickles, table olives (as supporting fermented-vegetable
evidence). All numbers below are taken from the cited source; nothing is invented. Where a number could not be
verified in the primary text, this is stated explicitly.

**Confidence labels**
- **strong** = primary experimental paper read in full text (or full abstract of a primary experimental paper),
  numbers directly quotable.
- **moderate** = primary experimental paper, but only abstract/metadata accessible, or the number is a secondary
  statement inside a review/chapter.
- **weak-or-contested** = indirect evidence, single study, or evidence from a different matrix (e.g. olives,
  cheese, meat) being extrapolated to vegetables.

# PART B5a — POTASSIUM CHLORIDE (KCl) PARTIAL SUBSTITUTION

## A. Headline answer: what fraction of KCl is acceptable?

**Fraction-definition warning (important for the model):** papers mix two conventions.
- Choi 1994 and Li 2022’s "50%" refer to **KCl as a share of the NaCl/brine salt** (NaCl:KCl weight ratio).
- Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 calls it **"40% sodium replacement"**, but KCl is only 30% of the total salt weight
  (4.5 of 15 g/kg), because MgCl₂ + CaCl₂ make up the rest.
- Li et al. 2025 npj and Huang et al. 2025 define K30/K50 as **% of total salt replaced by KCl**.
Always record which convention a number uses.

**Best-supported single number: 30% KCl of total salt** is the level with the most consistent positive
experimental support across sauerkraut, kimchi, paocai and suancai. **50% is the empirical ceiling** at which
sensory acceptability is still reported (kimchi 1:1, sauerkraut ≤50%), but the probability of bitter/metallic
off-flavour rises (K50 paocai had the highest bitterness of 0/10/30/50%).

## B. Sauerkraut — detailed findings

### B1. Wolkers-Rooijackers, Thomas & Nout (2013), *LWT – Food Science and Technology* 54(2):383–388
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lwt.2013.07.002 — free full text: https://edepot.wur.nl/291541
(also https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/effects-of-sodium-reduction-scenarios-on-fermentation-and-quality) — **confidence: strong**

Design: 100 kg pilot sauerkraut, white cabbage cv. Atria; 12 °C for 6 weeks then 9 °C for 6 weeks; sampled day 2, 4,
9, 37; texture + sensory at 6 and 12 weeks. Three treatments:
- **A (control):** 15 g/kg NaCl
- **B (salt reduction only):** 9 g/kg NaCl
- **C (salt replacement, isotonic to A):** 9 g/kg NaCl + **4.5 g/kg KCl + 0.75 g/kg MgCl₂ + 0.75 g/kg CaCl₂**
  (KCl = 30% of total salt; CaCl₂ = 5% of total salt)

Numbers:
- **pH:** A 4.5 / B 4.4 / C 4.3 at day 2; 4.1 / 4.1 / 4.0 at day 4; 3.9 / 3.9 / 3.9 at day 9;
  final (day 37) **3.6 (A) / 3.7 (B) / 3.5 (C)**. pH < 4 within 9 days in **all** treatments.
- **Titratable acidity at day 37 (g/kg as lactic acid):** A 15.0a, B 13.0b, C 15.0a
  → the KCl/MgCl₂/CaCl₂ mix matched the control; plain salt reduction lagged.
- **Lactic acid day 37 (g/kg):** A 9.4, B 8.5, C 8.7.
- **LAB counts:** 8.0–8.5 log CFU/g in all treatments; TVC 7.6–8.7 log CFU/g; Enterobacteriaceae detected at
  day 2 but not from day 4; yeasts/moulds <3.3 log CFU/g from day 9.
- **Texture (Miniature Kramer Shear, maximum force, N):**
  - 6 weeks: A **74 N**, B **56 N**, C **136 N**
  - 12 weeks: A **98 N**, B **52 N**, C **123 N**
  (So B, the pure salt reduction, is the soft one; C is ~1.5–2× firmer than the control.)
- **Sensory (1–5 scale, industrial panel, n=5 trained):**
  - Aroma: A 3.4→3.2 (6→12 wk), B 2.0→3.0, C 3.5→3.7
  - Taste: A 3.4→3.6, B 1.8→2.8, C 3.7→3.8
  - Texture: A 3.5→3.8, B 2.2→2.4, C **4.4→4.4**
  - B was significantly worse (P≤0.05) than A and C for texture and taste; A and C were not different.
- **Microbiota (PCR-DGGE + clone libraries):** *Lactococcus lactis* subsp. *lactis* and *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*
  dominated A, B and C. Treatment C additionally showed *Lactobacillus paraplantarum* and *Lactobacillus curvatus*
  at day 9. At the end, at least 10 species were present in all treatments, including *Weissella cibaria*.
- **Mechanistic conclusion (directly relevant to a model):** softening in B was **not** due to pectolytic enzymes
  (PME not detectable; exo-PG ~15 mU/g equal in A/B/C; no endo-PG products). The authors attribute it to **lower
  osmotic/ionic strength**. The CaCl₂ contribution is explicitly noted as too small to explain the effect via an
  "egg-box" mechanism ("we only substituted 5% of the NaCl by CaCl₂").
  → **KCl substitution is partly an osmotic-strength substitution, not merely a taste substitution.**

### B2. Li Z, Xie S, Sun B, Zhang Y, Liu K, Liu L (2022), *Journal of Food Processing and Preservation* 46(9):e16622
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jfpp.16622 — record: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df381e0f3e94b9e5d6263b
— **confidence: moderate (abstract only; Wiley full text not accessible from this session)**

Claims verbatim from the abstract:
- "The proportion of KCl had a significant impact on the fermentation kinetics, especially for kinetic parameters
  such as titratable acid (TTA) and reducing sugar (RS)."
- "The content of malic acid, acetic acid, lactic acid, and succinic acid in different treatments showed
  significant differences (p < .05)."
- "the number of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) were also affected by KCl."
- "According to quality descriptive analysis (QDA), the substitution ratio of KCl was **no more than 50%** to avoid
  the unacceptable taste and low overall liking."

**Not obtained:** the actual KCl percentages tested, the total salt level, the pH/TTA curve values, and the LAB
counts. The paper is the single most on-point sauerkraut KCl study; anyone with Wiley access should extract the
numeric tables. Treat "≤50%" as the only quotable number from it.

### B3. Johanningsmeier SD, McFeeters RF, Fleming HP, Thompson RL (2007), *Journal of Food Science* 72(2):M116–M172
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-3841.2007.00372.x — **confidence: strong (abstract)**

Not a KCl study, but the key control experiment for salt reduction in cabbage: shredded cabbage salted at
**0.5%, 1.0% or 2.0% NaCl (wt/wt)**, with or without *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* LA 81 starter at 10⁶ CFU/g.
- Natural (uninoculated) fermentations became variable, with softening and off-flavours as salt decreased.
- Starter-added fermentations gave firm texture and reduced off-flavours at **all** salt levels (P<0.05), and a
  more uniform pH decline → **50% NaCl reduction was achievable with a Leuconostoc starter**.
- Aroma/taste/texture judged after 10 months' storage at 18 °C.
This is the evidence that plain salt reduction (no K⁺) is the risky intervention, whereas keeping ionic strength up
(e.g. with KCl) is what protects texture.

### B4. Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 also cites (secondary): Viander, Mäki & Palva (2003); Wiander & Ryhänen (2005)
— sauerkraut juice made at only **5 g/kg of a mineral salt mixture** (57% NaCl, 28% KCl, 12% MgSO₄, 2% lysine·HCl,
1% SiO₂) received good sensory evaluation. **confidence: moderate** (cited within Wolkers-Rooijackers; the primary
papers were not retrieved). At 28% KCl of the mix this is consistent with the ~30% sweet spot.

## C. Kimchi — detailed findings

### C1. Choi SY, Beuchat LR, Perkins LM, Nakayama T (1994), *International Journal of Food Microbiology* 21(4):335–340
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-1605(94)90063-9 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8043352/ — **confidence: strong (abstract)**

- Brine water was **15% salt**; NaCl:KCl ratios tested **1:0 (control), 5:1, 2:1, 1:1 (wt:wt)** —
  i.e. **0%, 17%, 33%, 50% KCl**.
- Total acidity reached the acceptable range **0.4–0.6% (as lactic acid)** and pH **4.4–4.7** after
  **13 days at 13 ± 1 °C** — for the kimchi overall (not per treatment in the abstract).
- Kimchi brined at **5:1 and 2:1 (NaCl:KCl)** showed **faster growth of LAB and total aerobic microorganisms than
  the 100% NaCl control**.
- Sensory qualities (saltiness, bitterness, sourness, hotness, texture) at **all** test ratios were judged
  **acceptable** — including 1:1 (50% KCl).
**Not obtained:** per-treatment pH/TTA curves, LAB CFU/g, and bitterness scores.

### C2. Lee YN, Lee SM, Kim KO (2019), *Journal of Food Science* 84(10):2961–2972
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.14784 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31612540/ — **confidence: moderate (abstract)**

- Control kimchi = **2.0% (w/v) NaCl**. Six sodium-reduced samples: **25% and 50% sodium reduction** ×
  **KCl at 0, 0.47%, or 0.93%**.
- n = 167 consumers, balanced young (<40) vs old (>40); blind vs informed ("sodium-reduced" label) conditions.
- Key results: healthiness perception improved with a high sodium-reduction rate, but there was an **unfavourable
  prior notion about salt replacers**; giving "sodium-reduced" information generally **increased acceptance**, and
  the effect depended on sample and consumer age.
- **This is a consumer-acceptance study, not a fermentation-kinetics study.** It provides no pH, TTA or LAB data.

### C3. Lee M-A, Yun Y-R, Choi Y-J, Kim Y-S, et al. (2022), *Heliyon* 8(11):e11360
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e11360 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9663873/ — **confidence: strong (full text)**

This is a **salt-mineral-content** study (solar salt vs dehydrated solar salt vs purified salt), not a KCl
substitution study, but it is the cleanest direct evidence that **K⁺/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺-rich salt shifts the LAB community
towards Leuconostoc/Weissella and away from Lactobacillus**:
- Salt mineral contents (mg/kg): purified salt (PS) Na 386,334, Mg 262 (K lowest);
  solar salt 1 yr (SS1) Na 334,887, **Mg 7,069, K 1,907**; solar salt 3 yr (SS3) Na 335,830, Mg 5,150, K 1,340;
  dehydrated solar salt (DSS) Mg 12,545.
- Fermentation 6 °C, 6 weeks. Main organisms: *Weissella koreensis*, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*,
  *Latilactobacillus sakei*.
- **Solar-salt kimchi → higher proportion of *Leuconostoc* and *Weissella*; purified-salt kimchi → higher
  *Latilactobacillus sakei* (the over-ripening/spoilage-associated species).**
- Mannitol (heterofermentative marker) was highest in the solar-salt kimchi, especially SS3K.
- The authors cite a prior study (Lee et al. 2018) reporting higher viable counts in solar-salt kimchi, attributed
  to Mg and Ca.
**Interpretation for B5a:** mineral cations including K⁺ appear to *favour* the heterofermentative
*Leuconostoc*/*Weissella* early community over homofermentative *Lactobacillus*. **confidence: moderate**
(the K⁺ effect is confounded with Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺; this is a salt-type study, not a KCl-dosing study).

## D. Other fermented vegetables (KCl substitution, with numbers)

### D1. Li Y, Gu Y, Cheng W, et al. (2025), *npj Science of Food* 9:156
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41538-025-00528-2 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12304172/ — **confidence: strong (full text)**

Radish paocai, starter *Levilactobacillus brevis* PL6-1 at 7.00–7.50 log CFU/g, **total salt 1.5%**, 15 °C, 14 d.
Treatments: **K00** 100% NaCl, **K10** 90/10, **K30** 70/30, **K50** 50/50 (NaCl/KCl).

- **LAB counts:** initial ≈7.40 log CFU/g → **8.31–8.47 log CFU/g on day 1** → peak **8.75–8.87 log CFU/g on
  day 5**. "Partial replacement of NaCl with KCl showed **no significant impact on LAB growth dynamics**."
- **pH:** 5.95 → **3.61 by day 5**, then stable (all groups).
- **TTA:** 0.06% → **>0.3% by day 5**; "**TTA showed no significant variation**, suggesting that KCl substitution
  for NaCl did not significantly influence radish paocai maturation."
- **Growth-model nuance:** the modified Weibull model gave the **highest y_max for K00**, i.e. KCl slightly
  reduced the *maximum potential growth increment* of LAB; conversely **K50 had the highest shape parameter
  k = 0.4918**, i.e. the fastest early growth.
- **Organic acids at day 14:** lactic and acetic acid were **significantly lower in K10 and K30** than others;
  **K50 had the highest lactic and acetic acid**. Attributed to K⁺-dependent malate dehydrogenase and pyruvate
  dehydrogenase.
- **Mannitol:** K30 highest, **5.60 g/kg at day 5** (fructose → mannitol; K⁺ promotes mannitol synthesis).
- **Texture:** hardness rose then fell during fermentation; **K50 had the highest hardness** among groups;
  crispness was enhanced in all KCl groups (K⁺ as compatible solute).
- **Bitterness:** "Bitterness was not a dominant taste attribute in the radish paocai, however, the **K50 group
  exhibited the highest bitterness intensity** among the four groups." E-tongue astringency (K00–K50) ranged
  **3.24–3.36**; bitterness started at **6.02 (K00)** and declined across the series (the endpoint value was cut
  off in the extracted text). Aftertaste-B (bitter aftertaste) did not differ significantly among groups.
- **Overall:** K30 gave the highest sourness, sweetness, umami and overall acceptability; total odour-activity
  values highest for K30 (126.14 at day 5; 107.59 at day 14).

### D2. Cen Y, Li Y, Sun X, et al. (2026), *International Journal of Food Microbiology* 461:112012
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2026.112012 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42585874/ — **confidence: moderate (abstract)**

Radish paocai, *L. brevis* PL6-1; **K30** (30% KCl), **Mg30** (30% MgCl₂), **KMg15** (15% KCl + 15% MgCl₂).
- "All low-sodium groups maintained **comparable LAB growth, acidification, and overall acceptability**."
- 30% MgCl₂ **significantly reduced radish hardness**; K⁺ **promoted mannitol synthesis** and influenced citric
  acid metabolism; KMg15 increased ethyl propanoate.
- Transcriptomics: **targeted** gene-expression changes in *L. brevis* (propanediol utilisation, hexuronic acid
  degradation, stress response), not a wholesale reprogramming; *pduE* up-regulation linked to ethyl propanoate.

### D3. Huang R, Fang Y, Zhong Y, Lu W, Deng Y (2025), *International Journal of Biological Macromolecules* 322(Pt 4):147107
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.147107 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40858162/ — **confidence: moderate (abstract)**

Yunnan suancai; NaCl partially replaced by **KCl + κ-carrageenan at 30%, 40%, 50%**.
- *Weissella* and *Lactiplantibacillus* dominated the microbiota.
- Salt level mainly influenced microbiota structure in the **mid–late** stages of fermentation; 60 bacterial
  clades identified by LEfSe as salt-level biomarkers.
- Beneficial flavour contributors: *Levilactobacillus brevis*, *Latilactobacillus sakei*,
  *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*, *Weissella cibaria*.
- **"the 30% KCl/CG replacement preserved suancai texture and acceptability"** — i.e. 40% and 50% did not.

### D4. Total sodium substitution in green pickled tomatoes (2022), *Molecules* 27(5):1609
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27051609 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8911622/ — **confidence: moderate/strong (full text available; numbers quoted from abstract + results text)**

Romanian fermented green tomatoes; **100% substitution of NaCl by KCl or by MgCl₂**, 28 days at 16–18 °C.
- Final LAB: **4.11–4.63 log CFU** for all variants (KCl did not prevent LAB growth).
- Yeast counts below detection (<1 log CFU/g) throughout.
- Mesophilic population 1.91–3.66 log CFU/g with NaCl; "similar results ... for the samples with KCl and MgCl₂".
- **"the NaCl addition induced the most stable characteristics for the pickles"**; salt content in the KCl
  samples declined during storage (0.70 ± 0.05% → 0.57 ± 0.06%).
- **Texture:** firmness (max first-cycle compression force) decreased continuously in all variants;
  "**NaCl has the lowest influence on the textural parameters**".
- Sensory: overall acceptance of KCl and MgCl₂ pickles "proper to be consumed".
- **Not obtained:** pH and TTA values (the paper reports lactic acid, ash, salt, carotenoids, polyphenols,
  antioxidant activity, texture and sensory, but no pH/TTA trajectory in the accessible text).

## E. Does KCl inhibit LAB, and at what concentration?

### E1. Ndiaye A, Fliss I, Filteau M (2024), *Frontiers in Microbiology* 15:1328416
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1328416 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904479/ — **confidence: strong (full text)**

High-throughput colony-robot + image analysis of **31 LAB strains** and **93 random pairwise co-cultures** on
PCASL (milk-based) solid medium, at **3% NaCl, 5% NaCl and 3% KCl**.
- **Monocultures, community-level average:** growth score reduced by **(value at 5% NaCl)** (p<0.0001) and by
  **15% at 3% KCl** (p<0.0001). Acidification reduced **14% at 5% NaCl** (p<0.0001) and only **3% at 3% NaCl**
  (p<0.02), and at **3% KCl there was NO statistically significant effect on acidification at the community level
  (p = 0.18)**.
- **Co-cultures:** growth lower than control at 3% NaCl (**−23.9%**), 5% NaCl (**−57.4%**), 3% KCl (**−14.9%**),
  all p<0.0001. Acidification reduction significant **only** for 3% NaCl (−3.6%, p=0.02) and 5% NaCl (−15.1%,
  p<0.0001) — **not** for 3% KCl.
- **Some strains grew BETTER at 3% KCl** — e.g. *Lactococcus lactis* subsp. *lactis* 74310, **+23%** (p=0.01).
  Positive-effect cases were more abundant for 3% KCl than for either NaCl condition.
- Interactions: co-cultures were **more resilient** than monocultures; the significant salt effect "disappeared"
  (suppression) in 5% of co-cultures at 3% KCl vs 41.7% at 3% NaCl and 58.8% at 5% NaCl.
- Statistically: at 3% KCl, acidification and growth scores remained positively correlated (Pearson r=0.7694,
  p<0.0001), whereas at 3% and 5% NaCl that correlation broke down (r≈0.21–0.23, n.s.).
- Authors: "**KCl reduces the growth of the majority of our LAB strains, but not as much as NaCl** ... and may not
  drastically alter their acidification capacity."
- Note: 3% NaCl/KCl ≈ 0.51 M Na⁺/K⁺ — well above the 1.5–2.5% total salt of normal sauerkraut/kimchi.

### E2. Bautista-Gallego J, Arroyo-López FN, Durán-Quintana MC, Garrido-Fernández A (2008),
*Journal of Food Protection* 71(7):1412–1421
DOI: https://doi.org/10.4315/0362-028X-71.7.1412 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18680941/ — **confidence: moderate (abstract + publisher summary; exact MIC values not accessible)**

- Quantitative dose–response of NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂, MgCl₂ against ***Lactobacillus pentosus*** and
  ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae*** in DMR broth / YMPG broth; relative maximum specific growth rate and lag phase
  modelled vs chloride-salt concentration; **non-inhibitory concentrations and MICs were estimated and compared**.
- Key conclusion (verbatim): "All chloride salts exerted a significant antimicrobial effect on the growth cycle;
  particularly, **CaCl₂ showed a similar effect to NaCl, while KCl and MgCl₂ were progressively less
  inhibitory**. Microbial susceptibility and resistance were found to be nonlinearly dose related."
- **NOT OBTAINED: the numeric non-inhibitory concentrations and MICs.** The article is marked free-to-access by
  Mendeley but the ScienceDirect PDF returned HTTP 403 from this session. Anyone with access should extract the
  MIC/non-inhibitory concentration table — it is exactly the "KCl inhibits LAB at what concentration" number.

### E3. Corroborating cross-matrix evidence (all **weak-or-contested** for vegetables)
- **Zonoubi R, Goli M (2020), *Food Science & Nutrition* 8(12):6770–6783**, DOI https://doi.org/10.1002/fsn3.2050:
  complete NaCl→KCl/MgCl₂/CaCl₂ substitution in UF Feta brine. "All chloride salts exerted a significant
  antimicrobial effect on the fermentation growth cycle; particularly, **CaCl₂ showed a similar effect to NaCl,
  while KCl and MgCl₂ were progressively less inhibitory**." No overall-acceptance difference between Na and K brine.
- **Etchells JL, Costilow RN, Anderson TE, Bell TA (1964), *Applied Microbiology* 12(6):523–535**,
  DOI https://doi.org/10.1128/am.12.6.523-535.1964: the three cucumber-fermentation LAB species
  (*Pediococcus cerevisiae*, *Lactobacillus plantarum*, *L. brevis*) had an upper limit for appreciable growth and
  measurable acid production at about **8% salt** (NaCl) — the most salt-tolerant of nine species tested;
  thermophilic lactobacilli stopped at 2.5–4.0%. This is the NaCl reference point for "how much salt LAB tolerate".
- **Saccharomyces cerevisiae biofuel work** (Casey et al. 2013, *Biotechnol Biofuels* 6:83,
  https://doi.org/10.1186/1754-6834-6-83): among six
  cation/anion pairs, "**the salts with potassium cations were the least inhibitory**", and chloride was more
  inhibitory than sulfate. Yeast, not LAB — **weak** for this model.

### E4. Summary answer on KCl inhibition of LAB
- **Up to ~2–3% KCl (≈0.27–0.40 M K⁺) in a vegetable brine: no meaningful inhibition of acidification.**
  Growth may be modestly reduced (≈15% on solid medium at 3% KCl, Ndiaye 2024), and some strains are stimulated.
- **Equimolar KCl is consistently LESS inhibitory than NaCl** for LAB (Bautista-Gallego 2008; Ndiaye 2024;
  Zonoubi & Goli 2020 all agree in direction).
- **The "KCl is inhibitory at high concentration" concern is real but is a high-concentration phenomenon and is
  weaker than the NaCl effect.** Above roughly 3–4% K⁺ the growth penalty should be modelled, but I found **no
  experimental study that measured a KCl MIC for a vegetable-fermentation LAB species and reported the number**.
- **KCl's practical limit in these products is sensory (bitterness/metallic), not microbiological.**

## F. Does KCl substitution change the microbial community (Leuconostoc vs Lactobacillus)?

- **Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 (sauerkraut, PCR-DGGE)** — the KCl/MgCl₂/CaCl₂ treatment C showed **additional**
  *Lactobacillus paraplantarum* and *Lactobacillus curvatus* at day 9 relative to A and B, while *Lc. lactis* and
  *Leuc. mesenteroides* dominated all three. No shift away from *Leuconostoc*. **confidence: strong but low
  resolution (DGGE, total DNA, not RNA).**
- **Lee et al. 2022 Heliyon (kimchi)** — K/Mg/Ca-rich solar salt favoured ***Leuconostoc* + *Weissella***;
  near-pure NaCl favoured ***Latilactobacillus sakei*** in late fermentation. **confidence: moderate**
  (confounded with Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺; not a KCl-dosing study).
- **Pérez-Díaz et al. 2020 (cucumbers, 16S metagenetics, CaCl₂ not KCl)** — see B5b §B1 below; day-1 community
  was *Leuconostoc*/*Lactococcus*/*Weissella* under low-salt/CaCl₂ vs *Weissella* + Enterobacteriaceae under 6%
  NaCl, then *Lactobacillus* dominated everything by day 3–7. **confidence: strong** — but it is a salt-*level*
  effect, and directly relevant because CaCl₂ brines are lower-ionic-strength than 1 M NaCl brines.
- **Bottom line:** I found **no experimental study that directly measured a KCl-driven
  Leuconostoc→Lactobacillus shift in sauerkraut or kimchi.** The available evidence is (i) consistent with
  mineral/K⁺-rich salt favouring *Leuconostoc*/*Weissella*, and (ii) dominated by the stronger driver, which is
  total ionic strength / salt level rather than the Na:K identity. Label any model term for this as
  **weak-or-contested**.

## B. Detailed B5b findings

### B1. Microbial community in CaCl₂ brines — Pérez-Díaz IM, Dickey AN, Fitria R, Ravishankar N, Hayes J, Campbell K, Arritt F (2020)
*Journal of Applied Microbiology* 128(6):1678–1693 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/jam.14597 —
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31997433/ — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Compared **no salt**, **100 mmol/L (1.1%) CaCl₂**, and **1.03 mol/L (6%) NaCl** brines; commercial + lab scale.
- **CaCl₂ and no-salt brines sustained FASTER microbial growth and FASTER pH reduction than 6% NaCl.**
- **Day 1:** *Leuconostoc*, *Lactococcus* and *Weissella* dominated in **no-salt and 100 mM CaCl₂** brines;
  in 6% NaCl brines *Weissella* + **Enterobacteriaceae** dominated.
- **By day 3:** *Lactobacillus* dominated **all** fermentations regardless of salt type.
- **By day 7:** *Lactobacillus* made up **84–96%** of the population, except in the no-salt fermentations,
  which retained a mixed LAB population.
- Relative abundance after *Lactobacillus*: *Pediococcus* > *Leuconostoc* > *Lactococcus* > *Weissella*.
- **Bottom line: CaCl₂ does not slow acidification — it accelerates it relative to 6% NaCl — and it does not
  prevent the *Lactobacillus* takeover, but the day-1 community differs (fewer Enterobacteriaceae).**

### B2. CaCl₂-only cucumber fermentation — McFeeters RF, Pérez-Díaz IM (2010)
*Journal of Food Science* 75(3):C291–C296 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-3841.2010.01558.x —
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20492282/ — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Objective: ferment cucumbers with **CaCl₂ as the only salt**.
- Sugars converted primarily to lactic acid, **pH decreased to less than 3.5** — same pattern as NaCl brines.
- Lactic acid and pH stable during storage; **no detectable propionic or butyric acid** (no spoilage-bacteria growth).
- **Firmness retention in cucumbers fermented with 100–300 mM CaCl₂ during storage at 45 °C was NOT
  significantly different** from cucumbers fermented with **1.03 M NaCl + 40 mM CaCl₂**.
- In closed jars, with- and without-NaCl fermentations were similar chemically and in firmness retention.

### B3. Commercial scale-up — Pérez-Díaz IM, McFeeters RF, Moeller L, Johanningsmeier SD, et al. (2015)
*Journal of Food Science* 80(12):M2827–M2836 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.13107 —
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26512798/ — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Lab-stable CaCl₂ brines **rapidly underwent secondary fermentation** in 6,440 L open-top commercial tanks.
- Fix: limited air purging + starter culture + preservative in the cover brine.
- Successful process: **CaCl₂ and potassium sorbate at equilibrated 100 mM and 6 mM**, **Lactobacillus plantarum
  LA0045 inoculated at 10⁶ CFU/mL**, air purged 2 × 2–3 h/day for days 1–10 and 1 × 2–3 h/day days 11–14,
  in 12,490 L and 28,400 L open-top tanks.
- **All fermentations completed: full sugar→lactic acid conversion, pH decrease to 3.0, microbiologically stable
  for a minimum of 21 days.**
- Conclusion: usable for **short-term** storage; reduces pollution and waste-removal cost.

### B4. Commercial product quality — McMurtrie EK, Johanningsmeier SD (2018)
*Journal of Food Quality* 2018:8051435 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1155/2018/8051435 —
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2018/8051435 — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- 6 commercial processors, June–September over 2 years, vessels 10,000–40,000 L, cucumbers 2.7–5.1 cm diameter,
  bulk storage 55–280 days; **0.1 M CaCl₂ + 6 mM potassium sorbate, equilibrated**, with starter culture,
  vs standard industrial NaCl fermentation.
- **Terminal pH 3.23 ± 0.09 (CaCl₂) vs 3.30 ± 0.12 (NaCl)** — complete lactic fermentation in both.
- **CaCl₂-brined fermented cucumbers were on average 1.8 N LESS firm** — significant in the finished product
  (P<0.0001).
- Colour differences (higher hue, lower chroma; P<0.0269) consistent with increased photooxidation.
- Commercial implementation in open tanks "variably resulted in texture and color defects".
- **Caution: this is the key contradiction to the lab results — the same CaCl₂ concentration that preserves
  firmness in sealed jars gives a small but significant firmness loss in open commercial tanks.**

### B5. Bloater damage, CO₂ and Ca(OH)₂ — Zhai Y, Pérez-Díaz IM (2017)
*Journal of Food Science* (2017) — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.13945 — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Reformulated CaCl₂ cover brine with **calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), NaCl and acids** to raise buffer capacity,
  inhibit indigenous CO₂-producing microbiota, and reduce CO₂ solubility.
- Ca(OH)₂ + acetic acid → complete fermentation, **final pH 0.5 units HIGHER** than the un-supplemented control.
- **Lactic acid production +22%**; **Enterobacteriaceae relative abundance −92%**;
  **Leuconostocaceae relative abundance +7%**; **CO₂ +25%**.
- **50% reduction in acetic acid formation** when cover brines were supplemented with Ca(OH)₂ + **690 mM (4%) NaCl**.
- **No significant difference in bloater index** from Ca(OH)₂ or NaCl supplementation — because CO₂ stayed above
  the **20 mg/100 mL** threshold needed to induce the defect.
- Practical conclusion: the modified brine completes sugar conversion and lowers Enterobacteriaceae but does
  **not** meaningfully reduce bloater index.

### B6. Bloater defect causation (background) — Etchells JL, Borg AF, Bell TA (1968)
*Applied Microbiology* 16(7):1029–1035 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1128/am.16.7.1029-1035.1968 — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Bloaters (hollow stock) in cucumbers brined at 5–10% salt were associated with gaseous fermentation, chiefly
  yeasts; more recently, serious **early** bloater damage not attributable to yeasts was seen in 3.0–4.5% salt
  overnight dill pickles.
- **0.025, 0.05 and 0.1% sorbic acid** (or sodium salt) effectively controlled fermentative yeasts.
- **Inoculation of *Lactobacillus brevis* produced bloaters identical to yeast-made bloaters in all jars;
  no bloaters were produced by *L. plantarum* or *Pediococcus cerevisiae*.**
- Implication: at low salt, bloater control may require inhibiting **gas-forming LAB (*L. brevis*)**, not just yeasts.
- **I found no study that shows CaCl₂ itself prevents bloater damage.** The Ca(OH)₂ study (B5) explicitly reports
  no significant bloater-index improvement.

### B7. No-salt cucumber fermentation with calcium acetate — Fleming HP, McDonald LC, McFeeters RF, Thompson RL, Humphries EG (1995)
*Journal of Food Science* 60(5):1063–1065 — DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1995.tb05662.x — **confidence: strong (abstract)**
- Lab: cucumbers **blanched (3 min, 77 °C)** before brining in a **calcium acetate buffer**, brine inoculated with
  *Lactobacillus plantarum*. **Bloater formation was prevented by blanching even without CO₂ purging.**
  Firmness similar to salted brines after 1 month, but **lower after 12 months** in the salt-free cucumbers.
- Pilot scale: **severely bloated, unacceptable firmness after 7 months**, apparently due to microbial
  recontamination after blanching.
- **Key caveat for the model: the "no NaCl" success in the lab is fragile and does not survive scale-up unless
  recontamination is controlled.**

### B8. Calcium slows softening (mechanistic, industrial reference)
Pérez-Díaz IM, Breidt F, Buescher RW, Arroyo-López FN, Jiménez-Díaz R, Garrido Fernández A, Bautista Gallego J,
Yoon SS, Johanningsmeier SD (2013). "Fermented and Acidified Vegetables", Ch. 51 in *Compendium of Methods for the
Microbiological Examination of Foods* (APHA). USDA-ARS copy:
https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p390.pdf — **confidence: moderate (chapter/review)**
- "The addition of **calcium chloride can slow down the rate of enzymatic softening** of fermenting cucumbers.
  However, this should not be relied upon to eliminate enzymatic softening problems."
- "Studies have indicated that **calcium chloride, ca. 0.2–0.4%**, and other calcium salts may inhibit the action
  of softening enzymes. **Calcium chloride is now being added to commercial cucumber brines.**"
- "**Calcium salts have been found to enhance firmness retention in cucumbers at reduced concentrations of NaCl.**"
- Also documents: sauerkraut fermentation normally at 2.0–2.25% NaCl; kimchi cabbage soaked in 5–10% NaCl brine;
  fermented dill pickles max 0.35% (w/v) lactic acid; fermented cabbage contains mannitol, acetic acid (~1% each)
  and **lactic acid in excess of 2%**.

### B9. Cucumber fermentation physiology chapter (secondary, useful for model structure)
Franco W, Johanningsmeier S, Lu J, Demo J, Wilson E, Moeller L. "Cucumber Fermentation", Ch. 7 (USDA-ARS copy:
https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p406.pdf). **confidence: moderate (chapter).**

### B10. USDA-ARS summary presentation (rate + practice)
"Production of fermented cucumbers with CaCl₂ and without NaCl", USDA-ARS Food Science Research Unit (McFeeters,
Pérez-Díaz, Johanningsmeier; with B&G Foods and Mount Olive Pickle Company):
https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/ARS%20Website%202012%20CaCl2%20Fermentations%20.pdf — **confidence: moderate (institutional summary of peer-reviewed work)**
- Control tanks: **6% NaCl + 1.1% CaCl₂ + 0.05% acetic acid**.
- Experimental tanks: **1.1% CaCl₂ + 0.09% potassium sorbate + *L. plantarum* starter + 0.32% fumaric acid**.

- "**post-fermentation pH was lower in the presence of CaCl₂ than in the presence of sodium chloride (~3.0)**".
- At B&G Foods in open-top tanks, primary fermentation succeeded but **a secondary fermentation developed after
  4 months of storage**.
- "a key factor in doing fermentations without salt is to **limit purging only to what is needed to prevent
  bloating**".

## C. Bitterness / off-flavour from calcium — thresholds

1. **Consumer detection threshold of CaCl₂ in dill pickle chips = 61.8 ± 7.6 mM** (50% detection threshold).
   Consumer liking of flavour (n = 73) was **not** influenced by 23 or 35 mM CaCl₂ in finished products (P>0.05);
   there was no significant preference between CaCl₂- and NaCl-fermented pickles stored 2 months (P=0.75) or
   8 months (P=0.68). But NaCl-fermented pickles were preferred over CaCl₂-fermented pickles stored **10 months
   and desalted only once (P<0.01)**; a follow-up preference series indicated the **taste of CaCl₂ was not the
   factor** — **variability in texture decreased consumer liking (P<0.05)**.
   → **Wilson EM, Johanningsmeier SD, Osborne JA (2015), *Journal of Food Science* 80(6):S1360–S1367,
   DOI https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.12882, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25981784/ — confidence: strong (abstract).**

2. **CaCl₂ increases bitterness in table olives** while improving hardness, crunchiness and fibrousness;
   the authors recommend caution with CaCl₂ for that reason, while KCl was "predominantly favourable" and MgCl₂
   "neutral".
   → **López-López A, Moreno-Baquero JM, Garrido-Fernández A, *Foods* (Basel) 15(1):104,
   DOI https://doi.org/10.3390/foods15010104 — confidence: strong (abstract).** Cross-matrix (olives): **weak** for
   cucumbers/sauerkraut.

3. **KCl, not CaCl₂, is the classic bitterness problem** in this literature: "at high concentrations, KCl imparts a
   bitter metallic taste to food products, leading to lower consumer acceptability" (Ndiaye et al. 2024, citing
   Khetra et al. 2016). The Li et al. 2025 npj paocai study measured the highest bitterness in **K50** among
   K00/K10/K30/K50. **confidence: strong for the general claim; the specific K⁺ bitterness threshold in a
   vegetable brine was not determined.**

## D. "NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND"

The following specific claims were searched for and could **not** be substantiated with real experimental evidence
in this session. They should **not** be entered into the model as evidence.

**B5a — KCl**
1. **No experimental study was found that substitutes KCl for part of the NaCl in a cucumber / pickled-vegetable
   brine and reports fermentation-rate or product-quality endpoints.** Searches of Europe PMC, OpenAlex and the
   web returned only: (a) the Yousefi et al. 2025 cucumber study, which added KCl at only **50–400 ppm (≈0.7–5.4 mM)**
   alongside 6% NaCl, i.e. a trace-ion treatment, not a substitution; and (b) table-olive work, which is a
   different matrix. **The cucumber-brine KCl substitution case is an evidence gap.**
2. **No numeric KCl maximum-inhibitory / non-inhibitory concentration for LAB.** Bautista-Gallego et al. (2008)
   estimated MICs for NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂ and MgCl₂ against *Lactobacillus pentosus* and *S. cerevisiae*, and states
   the rank order (CaCl₂ ≈ NaCl > KCl > MgCl₂ in inhibition), but the actual numbers were not retrievable
   (publisher PDF blocked, HTTP 403). **The "at what concentration does KCl inhibit LAB" number is not in hand.**
3. **No study reporting a KCl bitter-taste detection threshold in sauerkraut, kimchi or cucumber brine.**
   The only quantitative bitterness-related numbers found are E-tongue bitterness intensities in paocai
   (Li et al. 2025 npj) and the general statement that high KCl gives a bitter metallic taste.
4. **No experimental evidence that KCl substitution shifts sauerkraut or kimchi from *Leuconostoc* to
   *Lactobacillus* (or vice versa).** Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 (PCR-DGGE) is the closest and shows only an
   *additional* presence of *Lb. paraplantarum* / *Lb. curvatus* in the mineral-salt treatment. The Heliyon 2022
   kimchi result (solar vs purified salt) is confounded by Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺.
5. **No quantitative pH/TTA/LAB numbers from Li et al. 2022 (sauerkraut KCl).** Only the qualitative statements
   and the "≤50%" sensory ceiling are verifiable from the abstract.
6. **No dose–response study testing KCl at 60%, 70%, 80% or 100% of total salt in sauerkraut or kimchi** with
   sensory and rate endpoints. The only 100%-substitution vegetable dataset found is the pickled-green-tomato
   study (Molecules 2022), which reports LAB counts and texture but no pH/TTA.

**B5b — CaCl₂ / calcium**
7. **No experimental study found that reports white sediment / white precipitate / turbidity caused by excess
   CaCl₂ in cucumber brines or sauerkraut.** This is a widely repeated practical claim in extension and
   troubleshooting material, but I found **no controlled experimental measurement** of it. Treat as folklore
   unless a primary source is produced.
8. **No experimental data found on calcium lactate addition to cucumber brines or sauerkraut fermentation.**
   Calcium lactate evidence located in this session was for table olives (Ca-lactate improved firmness of
   Iranian black olives, e.g. *Foods* 12(15):2970, https://doi.org/10.3390/foods12152970) and for alginate
   spherification — **not** for lactic-acid vegetable fermentations. **Do not model calcium lactate for
   sauerkraut/cucumber brine on the basis of evidence.**
9. **No CaCl₂ dose–response curve (e.g. 0 / 10 / 20 / 40 / 80 mM) for sauerkraut or kimchi fermentation rate**
   with pH curves, TTA and LAB counts. The nearest data are: the 0.75 g/kg CaCl₂ (5% of total salt) arm of
   Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013, which is confounded with KCl and MgCl₂; and the salicornia/cabbage study
   (Pires-Cabral 2025), which does not report the CaCl₂ concentration in the accessible abstract.
10. **No firmness values in Newtons for a CaCl₂ concentration series in sauerkraut.** The only N values found for
    sauerkraut texture are the Kramer-shear maxima of Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 (74 / 56 / 136 N at 6 weeks for
    control / salt-reduced / mineral-salt-mix).
11. **No experimental evidence that CaCl₂ reduces bloater damage.** Zhai & Pérez-Díaz 2017 explicitly found **no
    significant difference in bloater index** from Ca(OH)₂ or NaCl supplementation.
12. **No CaCl₂ MIC / growth-inhibition number for vegetable-fermentation LAB** — only the qualitative ranking from
    Bautista-Gallego et al. 2008 (CaCl₂ ≈ NaCl, both more inhibitory than KCl/MgCl₂ for *L. pentosus*).

## E. Practical implications for the SourDough model (evidence-graded)

1. **Set the KCl ceiling at ~30% of total salt for "safe by default"; allow up to 50% with a bitterness penalty
   term.** 30% is supported by Li 2025 (paocai, K30 best), Huang 2025 (suancai, 30% preserved texture and
   acceptability), Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 (KCl = 30% of the salt mix, best texture and equal acceptability) and
   Cen 2026 (K30 acceptable). 50% is the reported sensory ceiling (Choi 1994 kimchi 1:1; Li 2022 sauerkraut ≤50%)
   with a measurable bitterness increase (Li 2025 K50).
2. **Model KCl substitution as an ionic-strength substitution, not a taste swap.** The dominant effect of KCl in
   these systems is to keep osmotic/ionic strength high, which is what protects tissue firmness
   (Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013). Removing NaCl without replacing the ionic strength softens the product even if the
   fermentation chemistry is fine.
3. **KCl does not meaningfully slow LAB acidification at realistic brine concentrations.** pH/TTA trajectories
   were statistically indistinguishable at up to 50% KCl in paocai (Li 2025) and kimchi (Choi 1994), and in
   sauerkraut at 30% KCl (Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013). At 3% KCl in vitro, acidification was unaffected at the
   community level (p=0.18) while growth was reduced ~15% (Ndiaye 2024).
4. **For CaCl₂ in cucumber brines, use 20–40 mM as the "texture-only" addition** (Tang & McFeeters 1983;
   Fleming et al. 1987; industry chapter: 0.2–0.4%) **and 100 mM (1.1%) only if NaCl is being eliminated** and the
   process includes a starter, potassium sorbate, and controlled air purging (Pérez-Díaz 2015; McMurtrie &
   Johanningsmeier 2018). Expect terminal pH 3.0–3.3 in CaCl₂-only processes (slightly lower than NaCl).
5. **CaCl₂ does not slow acidification in cucumbers — it speeds it up** relative to 1 M NaCl (Pérez-Díaz 2020).
   It does slow lactic-acid production in **olives** (Rodríguez-Gómez 2012), so matrix matters.
6. **Budget for a small firmness penalty in commercial-scale Ca-only cucumber fermentation:** 1.8 N less firm on
   average, plus colour/photooxidation defects (McMurtrie & Johanningsmeier 2018).
7. **Ca²⁺ has a micronutrient side-effect worth modelling:** in cabbage, CaCl₂ addition reduced K, Mg and
   especially **Zn (−37%)** in the final product (Pires-Cabral et al. 2025).
8. **CaCl₂ bitterness is not a problem at ≤35 mM in pickles** (no liking effect at 23–35 mM; 50% detection
   threshold 61.8 ± 7.6 mM; Wilson et al. 2015).
