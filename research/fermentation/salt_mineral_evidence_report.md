# Trace/Minor Element Contents of Commercial Salts — Evidence Report

**Scope:** peer-reviewed analytical chemistry / food-composition literature reporting *measured* Mg, Ca, K, Fe, Na (and related elements) in refined table salt, sea/solar/sun-dried salt, Himalayan pink salt, grey/Guélorande/Celtic salt, and bamboo salt, plus NaCl purity.

**Evidence rules applied:** every number below was read in a source I actually retrieved. Sources I could not retrieve are listed explicitly in §9 as NOT RETRIEVED, with the failure mode. No blogs, marketing pages, or vendor claims are used as evidence anywhere in this report. Where an element was **not measured** by a study, that is stated rather than left blank.

**Conversions I performed** (flagged inline as "converted"): 1 mg% (mg/100 g) = 10 mg/kg = 10 ppm; 1% = 10,000 mg/kg.

---

## 0. Executive answer to the key question

**How much Mg, Ca, K, Fe does Himalayan pink salt actually contain vs refined table salt?**

The best single dataset is Fayet-Moore et al. 2020 (*Foods*), n = 31 pink salts + 1 iodized white table salt control, all analysed by ICP-MS in the same laboratory.

| Element | Refined iodized white table salt (n=1) | Pink salt mean (n=31) | Pink salt range (n=31) | Ratio (mean pink ÷ refined) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mg** | **83.94 mg/kg** | **2,655.31 mg/kg** | 146.78 – 11,937.98 | **≈ 32×** (range 1.7× – 142×) |
| **Ca** | **393.28 mg/kg** | **2,695.09 mg/kg** | 530.62 – 5,736.73 | **≈ 6.9×** |
| **K** | **151.68 mg/kg** | **2,406.05 mg/kg** | 98.39 – 4,528.89 | **≈ 15.9×** |
| **Fe** | **0.00 mg/kg** (not detected) | **63.75 mg/kg** | 0.00 – 167.52 | ratio undefined (control below detection) |
| Na | 427,636.27 mg/kg | 394,717.96 mg/kg | 343,384.79 – 459,115.58 | 0.92× (pink salt ≈8% less Na by weight) |

**So the marketing claim is directionally true and quantitatively trivial.** Pink salt really does carry roughly 7–32× more Mg, Ca and K per kilogram than refined table salt. But the absolute amounts are so small that it makes no nutritional difference at any realistic intake:

- **5 g of pink salt (≈1 teaspoon) delivers only:** Ca **13.48 mg**, Mg **13.28 mg**, K **12.03 mg**, Fe **0.32 mg**, Mn 0.01 mg, Zn 0.00 mg, Cu 0.00 mg, plus **1,973.59 mg Na** (Fayet-Moore et al. 2020, Table 4).
- Against Australian NRVs (as tabulated by the authors): that is **≈1.0–1.3 % of the Ca RDI**, **≈3.2–4.3 % of the Mg RDI**, **≈0.43 % of the K target**, **≈1.8–4 % of the Fe RDI** — while already supplying **≈99 % of the 2,000 mg/day sodium suggested dietary target**.
- Using the mean pink-salt concentrations, hitting 100 % of a nutrient target from pink salt would require eating **≈117 g/day** (Mg, 310 mg RDI), **≈158 g/day** (Mg, 420 mg RDI), **≈371 g/day** (Ca, 1,000 mg), **≈1,164 g/day** (K, 2,800 mg), or **≈126 g/day** (Fe, 8 mg). *(These are my arithmetic on the authors' measured means; the paper's own statement is that ">30 g per day (approximately 6 teaspoons) would be required to make any meaningful contribution to nutrient intake, a level that would provide excessive sodium.")*
- **The most extreme pink salt in the set was not Himalayan and had the LOWEST Mg and K of all 31 samples:** "Peruvian Pink Salt – Fine Grain Salt": Ca 5,736.73 mg/kg (highest Ca), **Mg 146.78 mg/kg (lowest)**, **K 98.39 mg/kg (lowest)**, Fe 16.40 mg/kg — and this same sample was the **only one exceeding the lead limit (Pb 2.59 mg/kg vs FSANZ maximum 2 mg/kg)**.
- **Some Himalayan-labelled products contained no detectable Fe at all** (e.g. SAXA Natural Pink Himalayan Salt Grinder, Fe 0.00 mg/kg; Mount Zero Australian Pink Lake Salt, Fe 0.00 mg/kg).
- **The lowest-Mg Himalayan product (SAXA Pink Himalayan Salt Flakes, Mg 918.43 mg/kg) had the highest Na of the whole set (459,115.58 mg/kg)** — i.e. the "least mineral-rich" pink salt is also the saltiest by weight.

Independent Korean datasets agree on the *direction and rough magnitude* for solar salt vs refined salt (Mg 20–48× higher in solar salt, Lee et al. 2022; ~9.7× for Mg and ~5.6× for Ca, Jo & Shin 1998), but they disagree substantially on the *absolute* Mg content of "refined salt" (84 vs 262 vs 1,110 mg/kg — a 13× spread across studies). See §7 for that contradiction.

### 1.4 XRF analysis of Himalayan pink salt — WARNING, widely misquotable

- **CLAIM (as the authors present it):** elements detected in pink salt by XRF include Mg, Si, Ca, S, Fe, Al, K.
- **NUMBERS:** Table 2 means over n = 8 Brazilian retail brands (mass %): Mg **10.64 ± 3.33**, Si 10.17 ± 3.54, Ca **4.84 ± 3.39**, S 3.67 ± 3.71, Al 3.40 ± 1.31, **Fe 2.72 ± 2.94**, **K 1.96 ± 1.37**, Cl 1.04 ± 1.54, **Na 0.82 ± 1.34**, Sr 0.17 ± 1.20, Zr 0.05 ± 0.05, P 0.04 ± 0.02.
- **SOURCE:** Ishigooka MY, Borges JFM, de Souza ECF, Cintho OM. *Análise comparativa de sal rosa do Himalaia via fluorescência de raios x.* **Revista Caribeña de Ciencias Sociales, Miami, v.13, n.8, p. 01-15, 2024.** DOI 10.55905/rcssv13n8-006. PDF retrieved at https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/10179788.pdf
- **CONFIDENCE: weak-or-contested — and these percentages must NOT be quoted as whole-salt mineral content.**
- **Study-quality note (decisive):** the samples were **repeatedly diluted and washed to dissolve out NaCl before XRF pelletisation**; the authors state explicitly that "a metodologia de análise envolveu a remoção da grande parte do sódio e do cloro pelo processo de diluição" (the method removed most of the sodium and chlorine by dilution). The unit is therefore **g/100 g of dried residue**, not g/100 g of salt. Na 0.82 % and Cl 1.04 % are self-evidently impossible for a product that is ~98 % NaCl, confirming this reading. n = 8 brands, no replicates stated, portable/pressed-pellet XRF without a reported certified reference material. Published in a **social-sciences journal**, not an analytical chemistry journal. The same paper **correctly** notes that using Fayet-Moore et al.'s whole-sample data, **≈146 g/day of pink salt would be required to reach a 1,000 mg/day Ca intake — about 29× the WHO 5 g/day salt limit.**

## 2. Refined table salt, sea salt, solar salt, grey/Celtic salt — comparative panels

### 2.1 Korean solar salt (cheonilyeom) vs purified/refined salt — the kimchi-relevant dataset

- **CLAIM:** 3-year-aged Korean solar salt has lower moisture and higher NaCl than 1-year-aged; both are safe on heavy metals.

**Implication for brining:** because NaCl content spans roughly **79 % to 98 %** across these products, **equal weights of salt do not give equal brine salinity**. To match the salinity of a refined-salt brine you must scale the salt mass by the ratio of NaCl fractions. Examples from the data above:

- Refined salt (96.0 %) vs Korean sun-dried salt (84.3 %): need **96.0 / 84.3 = 1.14×** more sun-dried salt by weight.
- Refined salt (96.0 %) vs Korean jangpan solar salt (85.06 %): need **96.0 / 85.06 = 1.13×** more.
- Refined salt (96.0 %) vs Korean flower of salt (78.81 %): need **96.0 / 78.81 = 1.22×** more.
- Dehydrated solar salt (≈82.3 %) vs 3-year solar salt (≈85.4 %): dehydrated salt is *less* salty per gram than the aged salt, despite the name.

This is exactly the confound the Korean kimchi literature has to control for: Lee et al. 2022 standardised all four brines to **12 % salinity (16 h)** rather than to equal salt weight, and Shin et al. 1999 likewise matched salt concentration and found **no significant differences in kimchi pH, acidity or sensory quality** across Korean, Chinese, Thai and Australian salts.

## 5. Cross-study contradictions that matter

1. **Mg content of "refined salt" varies 13× across studies.** Fayet-Moore 2020 (Australian iodized table salt, ICP-MS): **83.94 mg/kg**. Lee et al. 2022 (Korean purified salt, ICP-MS): **261.94 mg/kg**. Jo & Shin 1998 (Korean refined salt, AAS): **1,110 mg/kg**. Any "pink salt has N× more Mg than table salt" claim is therefore highly sensitive to which refined-salt baseline is used: the ratio ranges from **≈2.4×** (vs 1,110 mg/kg) to **≈32×** (vs 83.94 mg/kg).
2. **Trace-metal levels disagree by 1–2 orders of magnitude between laboratories.** For Himalayan pink salt: **Pb 5.5 mg/kg** (Di Salvo 2023, ICP-MS) vs **Pb 0.2 mg/kg** (Caridi 2019, ICP-MS) vs **Pb 0.00–2.59, mean 0.13 mg/kg** (Fayet-Moore 2020, ICP-MS). Cu: **28.8 mg/kg** (Di Salvo) vs **1.2 mg/kg** (Caridi). The Di Salvo Cu and Zn values and its blanket "Pb exceeded the maximum level in all samples" conclusion are not corroborated by either other ICP-MS study and are, on mass-balance grounds, implausible.
3. **Na values frequently imply >100 % NaCl.** Fayet-Moore control 427,636 mg/kg Na → 108.7 % NaCl; pink salt mean 394,718 mg/kg Na → 100.3 % NaCl; Kim et al. 2014 foreign salts 407,346–426,612 ppm Na → 103.5–108.4 % NaCl. ICP-based Na in saturated chloride matrices is positively biased. **Do not convert published Na values into NaCl purity without checking stoichiometry.**
4. **The mineral "enrichment" of unrefined salt is real but nutritionally inert, and the same unrefined character brings the contaminants.** In the same n=31 panel, pink salt was simultaneously ~7–32× richer in Mg/Ca/K than table salt **and** carried Al up to 192.65 mg/kg, Si up to 372.10 mg/kg, S up to 33,754 mg/kg, and one sample at **Pb 2.59 mg/kg (above the FSANZ 2 mg/kg maximum)**. Independent work supports the contaminant side: **Kim et al. 2014** found DEHP above the 1.5 ppm criterion in 5 of 38 foreign solar salts (up to 4,554.20 ppb); **Stajic et al. 2025** report that sea salts were highly contaminated with microplastics; **Caridi 2019** reports ⁴⁰K at 0.89 ± 0.07 Bq/g in Himalayan pink salt.
5. **Bamboo salt is not the most mineral-rich salt despite the traditional claim.** Jo & Shin 1998 measured **Mg 5,650 mg/kg in bamboo salt vs 10,780 mg/kg in plain sun-dried salt** — bamboo salt had about half the Mg, plus the highest Pb (1.15 ppm) and the highest water-insoluble fraction (1.98 %) of the four salt types. Ha & Park 1998 claim bamboo salt is high in K, P, Fe and Ge, but published **no numbers** in the accessible abstract. **The specific numeric claim that bamboo salt is Mg/Ca-rich is NOT SUPPORTED by the one study I retrieved that measured it.**

## 6. What "mineral-rich" buys you — the arithmetic, stated plainly

Using Fayet-Moore et al. 2020's measured means and their own NRV comparison (Table 4):

| Nutrient | Per 5 g pink salt | Adult target (as tabulated) | % of target | Pink salt needed for 100 % of target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ca | 13.48 mg | 1,000–1,300 mg | 1.0–1.3 % | **371–482 g/day** |
| Mg | 13.28 mg | 310–420 mg | 3.2–4.3 % | **117–158 g/day** |
| K | 12.03 mg | 2,800 mg | 0.43 % | **1,164 g/day** |
| Fe | 0.32 mg | 8–18 mg | 1.8–4.0 % | **126–282 g/day** |
| Mn | 0.01 mg | 5–5.5 mg | ~0.2 % | ~2,500 g/day |
| Zn | 0.00 mg | 8–14 mg | ~0 % | — |
| Cu | 0.00 mg | 1.2–1.7 mg | ~0 % | — |
| **Na** | **1,973.59 mg** | **2,000 mg SDT / 2 g WHO limit** | **≈99 %** | — |

The salt needed for a meaningful Mg or Ca contribution (117–482 g/day) would deliver **46–190 g of sodium per day**, i.e. **23–95× the WHO 2,000 mg/day sodium limit**. That is the quantitative form of the "pink salt is mineral-rich" claim's failure: the minerals are real, the delivery vehicle is not.

The same conclusion holds for solar salt: at Lee et al. 2022's highest solar-salt Mg (12,544.98 mg/kg, dehydrated solar salt), 5 g delivers **62.7 mg Mg** (≈15–20 % of the Mg RDI) — better than pink salt but still requiring ~25–33 g/day for 100 % of the Mg RDI, at which point sodium intake is ~8–10× the WHO limit.

## 10. Bottom line

1. **Himalayan pink salt vs refined table salt, per kg:** Mg ≈ 2,655 vs 84 mg; Ca ≈ 2,695 vs 393 mg; K ≈ 2,406 vs 152 mg; Fe ≈ 64 vs 0 mg (below detection). Roughly **7–32× richer**, with enormous product-to-product scatter (Mg spans 147–11,938 mg/kg across 31 retail pink salts).
2. **Nutritionally this is immaterial.** 5 g of pink salt gives ~13 mg each of Ca and Mg and ~12 mg K — about **1–4 % of daily targets** — while already supplying ~99 % of the daily sodium target. Matching a single nutrient target from salt alone would require 117–1,164 g/day of salt.
3. **The "mineral-rich" story is partly a purity story.** Higher mineral content is the flip side of lower NaCl (Korean solar salt 79–91 % vs refined 96–98 %). Equal weights of different salts therefore do **not** give equal brine salinity — scale by NaCl fraction (e.g. 1.13–1.22× more solar/flower salt than refined salt).
4. **Bamboo salt is not supported as Mg/Ca-rich** by the one retrieved study that measured it (Mg ≈ half of plain sun-dried salt).
5. **The unrefined-salt trade-off is real and documented:** Al up to 192.65 mg/kg, Si up to 372.10 mg/kg, one pink salt at Pb 2.59 mg/kg (above the FSANZ 2 mg/kg limit), DEHP up to 4,554 ppb in foreign solar salts, and microplastic contamination in sea salts.
6. **Two specifically requested papers could not be read** — Basaran et al. 2024 (Türkiye, *J Food Compos Anal* 134:106514) and Hwang et al. 2016 (*Analytical Letters* 49(17)) — both blocked at the publisher. **NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE from those two is reported here**, and neither should be cited for numbers until the full texts are obtained.
