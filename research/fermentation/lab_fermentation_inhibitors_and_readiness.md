# Naturally Present Inhibitory Compounds in Vegetable Fermentation, and What "Ready" Means Scientifically

**Quantitative research dossier — Question 6 and Question 8**

Compiled from primary literature, federal regulation, land-grant extension publications, USDA ARS work, and FAO/AGRIS records. Every value carries a source, URL, confidence rating, and a study-quality note. Explicit **DATA NOT FOUND** flags mark genuine gaps.

**Confidence legend**
- **STRONG** — regulatory text, or a controlled study with replication and clear endpoints, or a value corroborated by independent sources.
- **MODERATE** — single study, or a review/secondary citation of a primary source not directly retrieved, or a value whose direction is robust but magnitude is uncertain.
- **WEAK / CONTESTED** — secondary or tertiary citation, methodologically limited, internally inconsistent, or contradicted elsewhere.

**Three distinctions maintained throughout**
1. **REGULATORY** thresholds (legally binding numbers).
2. **SENSORY/CULTURAL** convention (what producers and consumers call a stage).
3. **MEASURED** data (what an instrument read in a controlled experiment).

# PART 1 — QUESTION 6: NATURALLY PRESENT INHIBITORY / ANTIMICROBIAL COMPOUNDS

## 6.0 Executive answer up front

*(Full ranking and reasoning in §6f.)*

| Rank | Ingredient | Active compound | Verdict at realistic recipe levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clove** | eugenol | **Demonstrably breaks a real fermentation** — 2 % clove powder in kimchi cut LAB by ~5.4 log — **but spares the spoilage yeasts.** The worst possible selectivity; the anti-preservative. |
| 2 | **Thyme (as essential oil)** | thymol, carvacrol | **Breaks a sauerkraut fermentation at a deliberately low 150 ppm** and leaves terminal pH **4.91 — above the 4.6 safety threshold.** Not a LAB-selective agent. |
| 3 | **Garlic** | allicin, thiosulfinates | **The useful one — but against spoilage YEAST, not LAB.** Direct LAB suppression is real but small and is outweighed by the LAB and sugar garlic brings: 4 % garlic made kimchi acidify *faster*. Delays white-colony yeast by ~17 days. **Raw only** — drying kills the activity. |
| 4 | **Cinnamon** | cinnamaldehyde | **The right selectivity window (yeasts 33–263 ppm vs LAB ~2,100 ppm) but the wrong dose** — 1 g/kg gives ~**6–20 ppm** (estimate; see §6e.5), **100–350× below** the LAB MIC. |
| 5 | **Tannins (grape leaf etc.)** | condensed tannin (17–20 kDa) | **Second-order for fermentation rate (measurably NO effect); first-order for texture** via competitive inhibition of pectinase at 5–100 ppm. **Drying destroys >50 % of activity.** |
| — | **Mustard / horseradish** | allyl isothiocyanate | **CONTESTED — two good datasets disagree by ~100× (60–80 ppm vs >7,500 ppm).** Most likely resolved by AITC's volatility: it escapes the brine, so neither MIC describes a real crock. |
| 6 | **Hot pepper** | capsaicin | **Does not inhibit LAB at realistic levels.** No MIC against LAB exists in the literature. Its 16.8–29.9 % sugar load *accelerates* fermentation. |
| — | **Salt** | NaCl | Not a spice, but the dominant control lever, and the one whose curve is best characterised. |

**The single most important generalisation:** whole dried spices at 0.1–3 % w/w deliver **~1–125 ppm** of active compound to a brine, while measured LAB MICs cluster at **1,000–2,100 ppm**. **Most spices are used one to three orders of magnitude below their LAB-inhibitory concentrations** — and the gap is widened further by poor aqueous extraction (cinnamon water extract ≈ 1/20 of the ethanol extract) and by volatility. The two exceptions are **essential oils** and **clove at high dose**, and both of those are actively harmful rather than helpful.

**The most useful practical correction this report makes:** garlic's real, large, repeatable effect in a vegetable ferment is **against spoilage yeast**, not against lactic acid bacteria. The folklore has the right ingredient for the wrong reason.

### 6a.3 Does garlic measurably slow vegetable fermentation? — the two decisive experiments

These two studies appear to contradict each other. They do not; they measure different things, and the reconciliation is the answer.

#### Experiment 1 — Garlic added to a STERILE, DEFINED-STARTER model kimchi: garlic DOES slow acidification

**Choi H-W, Park S-E, Kim E-J, Seo S-H, Whon TW, Roh SW, Son H-S (2024). "Selective influence of garlic as a key ingredient in kimchi on lactic acid bacteria in a fermentation model system." *Heliyon* 10(2):e24503.**
Korea University + World Institute of Kimchi. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10828059/ · doi 10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24503

- **Design (this is why it is decisive):** model kimchi was **autoclaved** — destroying any LAB carried on the ingredients — then inoculated with a defined 1 % mixed starter of nine LAB strains (*Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*, *Latilactobacillus sakei*, *Levilactobacillus brevis*, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, *L. citreum*, *L. gelidum*, *Weissella koreensis*, *W. cibaria*, *W. confusa*). Garlic at **0 % (G0), 1 % (G1), 2 % (G2) w/w**. Stored **4 °C for 10 days**. Triplicate; MANOVA/ANOVA + Tukey, *p* < 0.05.
- **Result:** *"The G0 group (containing 0 % garlic) showed a significantly higher number of viable cells on the day 5 than the other groups (containing 1 % and 2 % garlic)."* *"The G0 group had higher levels of lactic acid from day 5 of fermentation than the other groups and showed a significant difference on day 10."* *"on day 5 of fermentation, the G0 group showed a significantly lower pH value but a higher TA value than the other groups."*
- **Selectivity:** garlic suppressed *L. plantarum* while favouring *W. koreensis*. On day 10, *W. koreensis* reached **98.7 % (G1)** and **96.3 % (G2)** relative abundance, versus G0 where *L. sakei* rose instead. In G0, *L. plantarum* was **59.7 %** relative abundance on day 1.
- **Confidence: STRONG for the direction** (direct effect of garlic compounds on LAB, with garlic's own flora removed by autoclaving), **MODERATE for magnitude** (the exact pH and TA values are in Fig. 2, not tabulated in text).
- **Study-quality note:** n = 3, 10-day window only; authors explicitly flag the short duration and the narrow 0/1/2 % dose range as limitations.

#### Experiment 2 — Garlic added to REAL kimchi at 0–4 %: garlic does NOT slow, and at 4 % it ACCELERATES acidification

**Jang H-Y, Kim MJ, Jeong JY, Hwang IM, Lee J-H (2024). "Exploring the influence of garlic on microbial diversity and metabolite dynamics during kimchi fermentation." *Heliyon* 10(2):e24919.**
World Institute of Kimchi, Gwangju. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10835354/ · doi 10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24919

- **Design:** four kimchi batches with garlic at **0 %, 1 %, 2 %, 4 % w/w** (A0, A1, A2, A4). Full recipe: salted cabbage 84 %, red pepper powder 3 %, radish 3 %, ginger 0.8 %, onion 2.5 %, salted shrimp 1.5 %, glutinous rice paste 0.8 %, water 4.4 %. **No starter.** Stored **4 °C, sampled weekly for 4 weeks.** Triplicate.
- **Acidity:** initial ~**0.3 %** at week 0, and *"did not vary depending on the garlic content."* **At week 1, kimchi A4 (4 % garlic) showed a significantly HIGHER acidity than A0 (0 % garlic), *p* < 0.05.** Garlic **accelerated** acidification.
- **LAB counts (log CFU/mL):**

| Week | A0 (0 %) | A1 (1 %) | A2 (2 %) | A4 (4 %) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 4.56 ± 0.31 | 4.77 ± 0.21 | 4.48 ± 0.44 | 4.95 ± 0.05 |
| 1 | **6.35 ± 0.06** | 5.67 ± 0.35 | **6.99 ± 0.18** | **7.47 ± 0.02** |

- **Lactic acid (mM), the sharpest contrast:** A0 rose 0.85 → **6.37** → 38.91 → 54.59 → 66.42 over weeks 0–4. A4 rose 1.31 → **29.85** → 52.08 → 71.15 → **82.46**. At week 1 the 4 % garlic batch had produced **~4.7× more lactic acid** than the no-garlic batch.
- **Community composition at week 1 (genera):** low-garlic batches A0/A1 were **89–92 % *Weissella***. High-garlic A2/A4 were more diverse: ***Lactobacillus* 36–37 %, *Leuconostoc* 20–21 %, *Weissella* 23–26 %.**
- **Diversity (week 1):** OTUs/Shannon — A0 196/0.71; A1 202/0.62; A2 257/2.33; A4 372/2.49. The paper's own conclusion: *"the addition of garlic has a positive impact on the bacterial diversity and metabolite production during kimchi fermentation."*
- **Confidence: STRONG** (4 doses, 4 weeks, triplicate, culture + 16S + LC-MS).

#### Reconciliation — and this is the actual answer to Question 6(a)

Garlic has **two opposing effects that are confounded in any real vegetable ferment**:

1. A **direct inhibitory effect** of allicin/thiosulfinates on LAB — small, real, and cleanly demonstrated only when garlic's own microbiota is removed (Experiment 1). It is a *selective* effect on community composition (suppressing *L. plantarum*, favouring *Weissella*/*Leuconostoc*) more than a large effect on total acid production.
2. A **large opposing effect**: garlic is itself *"a rich resource"* for LAB and for fermentable substrate. The World Institute of Kimchi group has shown this repeatedly — garlic is a source of LAB for kimchi (Jang 2024; Choi YJ et al. 2024, *Heliyon* 10(2):e24283, "kimchi with garlic showed higher abundance of *Leuconostoc* and *Weissella*"; Choi YJ et al. 2024, *LWT* 198, garlic shifts the *Lactobacillus*:*Leuconostoc* ratio).

**Net effect at the 2–5 % garlic typical of kimchi: acidification is neutral to faster, not slower.** This is internally consistent with the observation that kimchi is a *fast* fermentation. The question's own framing — "kimchi is fast, which is evidence against a large effect" — is correct, and the quantitative evidence confirms it.

**Also relevant (older, lower-resolution):** Cho NC, Jhon DY, Shin MS, Hong YH, Lim HS (1988), *Korean Journal of Food Science and Technology* 20(2):231–235 (Chonnam National University), kimchi with **0, 1, 2, 4, 6 % garlic at 21 °C**: *"The higher the garlic concentration of the Kimchis was, the smaller the increase of the aerobic bacterial population was during the initial periods"* (i.e. garlic suppressed aerobic spoilage bacteria) but *"Lactic acid bacterial population increased more at higher garlic concentrations during the initial period of the fermentations."* Yeast changes *"were not apparent."* AGRIS record: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/6471c7b477fd37171a6f34c5 — **Confidence: MODERATE** (abstract-level only; Korean-language primary; 3 figures, 2 tables, 9 refs).

### 6a.4 Garlic vs yeasts and moulds — the clearest and largest effect

**Kim M-J, Kang S-E, Jeong CH, Min S-G, Hong SW, Roh SW, Jhon D-Y, Kim T-W (2021). "Growth Inhibitory Effect of Garlic Powder and Cinnamon Extract on White Colony-Forming Yeast in Kimchi." *Foods* 10(3):645.**
World Institute of Kimchi + Chonnam National University. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8003234/ · doi 10.3390/foods10030645

- **Organisms:** five white colony-forming yeasts (WCFY) responsible for kimchi surface spoilage — *Kazachstania servazzii* MGB0660, *Candida sake* MGB0659, *Debaryomyces hansenii* MGB0661, *Pichia kudriavzevii* MGB1001, *Hanseniaspora uvarum* MGB1002. Agar-well diffusion, 24 °C/24–48 h, 8 mm wells, 70 µL.
- **Raw garlic filtrate and freeze-dried garlic powder (FGP) showed strong anti-yeast activity against four of the five yeasts.** FGP did **not** inhibit *K. servazzii*; raw filtrate inhibited it marginally.
- **Garlic concentrate showed NO anti-yeast activity at all** — attributed to heat inactivation of alliinase during processing.
- **Applied result:** a mixture of **10 % FGP + 1.75 % CEE (cinnamon ethanol extract)**, sprayed onto the kimchi surface at **0.03 g/cm²** with **0.02 % xanthan gum** as an adhesive, **delayed white colony formation by an average of 17 days at 10 °C** versus control.
- **Sensory:** 10 % FGP + 1.75 % CEE did not significantly change appearance, smell or taste (n = 10 panelists, 9-point hedonic scale). 10 % FGP + 2 % CEE significantly affected taste.
- **Confidence: STRONG for anti-yeast activity and the 17-day delay; WEAK for the sensory result** (n = 10 is far below the ~50 untrained / 8–12 trained panel norm; the authors of a related paper make this criticism explicitly).
- **Critical mechanistic point:** raw garlic filtrate was *more* inhibitory than 30 % FGP **despite 30 % FGP containing more alliin (93.38 vs 60.43 mg/L)** — because alliin is the inactive precursor and drying reduces alliinase activity. **Anti-yeast activity tracks allicin-forming capacity, not alliin content.**

**Direct corroboration in vivo:** Choi H-W et al. (2024), *LWT* 198 — *"Difference in color and white colony-forming yeast were found only in kimchi without garlic on the 100th day of fermentation."* Kimchi **with** garlic had no white colony at day 100. **Confidence: STRONG.** https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/6471c7b477fd37171a6f34c5 (AGRIS record for *LWT* 198)

**Why this matters:** garlic's real, repeatable, quantitatively large effect in kimchi is **against spoilage yeast, not against LAB.** This is the opposite of the "garlic slows fermentation" intuition, and it is the practical reason garlic is in the recipe.

**DATA NOT FOUND:** MIC of allicin or garlic extract against *Zygosaccharomyces bailii*, *Brettanomyces*, or *Aspergillus*/*Penicillium* in µg/mL from a primary source. Also **no MIC for allicin or diallyl disulfide against any LAB species**.

### 6a.5 Practical ppm arithmetic for garlic (this report's own calculation — flagged as such)

Fresh garlic is roughly 35–40 % dry matter. Camargo et al.'s **8.16–17.4 mg allicin/g dry weight** therefore corresponds to roughly **~3–7 mg allicin/g fresh weight** (arithmetic, not a cited value).

| Garlic dose in ferment | Allicin concentration if fully generated (assuming ~3–7 mg/g FW) | vs allicin MIC ~128 µg/mL |
|---|---|---|
| 1 % w/w (10 g/kg) | **~30–70 µg/mL** | **below MIC** (0.2–0.5×) |
| 2 % w/w (20 g/kg) | **~60–140 µg/mL** | **straddles the MIC** (0.5–1.1×) |
| 4 % w/w (40 g/kg) | **~120–280 µg/mL** | **at or above MIC** (0.9–2.2×) |
| 5 % w/w (50 g/kg) | **~150–350 µg/mL** | **above MIC** |

**Caveats that make this an over-estimate of *active* allicin:** (i) allicin is generated only from crushed raw garlic and is heat-labile and unstable; (ii) allicin is a thiol reagent that is consumed by reaction with proteins and thiols in the vegetable matrix; (iii) in kimchi the garlic is mixed into a paste with salt, fish sauce and sugar, and fermentation runs for days to weeks, so allicin will be largely spent within the first hours. This arithmetic is a **bounding estimate, not a measurement**, and no study found directly measured allicin in a vegetable brine. **Confidence: WEAK (derived).** It is nonetheless consistent with the observed pattern: a mild early effect on LAB that disappears, and a persistent effect on surface yeast (which sit in a different, more aerobic compartment).

### 6b.2 MIC against LAB — the finding that overturns the common assumption

**Regubalan B, Ananthanarayan L (2018). "Mustard oil: a potent antimicrobial agent." *Journal of Food Science and Technology* 55(9):3417–3426.**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6098797/ · doi 10.1007/s13197-018-3247-2

Mustard essential oil (declared **99 % AITC**), broth dilution, ~5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL inoculum, 37 °C/48 h, turbidity at 625 nm plus plating, all in triplicate.

| Strain | Effect | MIC (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* NCIM 2073 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus plantarum* NCIM 2084 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus casei* NCIM 2364 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus acidophilus* NCIM 2903 | **Biocidal** | **60** |
| *Pediococcus pentosaceus* CFR 2123 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Candida versatilis* CFR 505 (yeast) | **Biocidal** | **40** |

- Mustard EO was the **only cidal** oil of **eleven** tested (asafoetida, black/white pepper, coriander, clove, cumin, fennel, garlic, ginger, parsley seed). **All others were merely static, at 200–3500 ppm.**
- **Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers (5 LAB strains + 1 yeast, triplicate, explicit cidal/static discrimination, single lab). **MODERATE** for extrapolation to food.
- **This directly contradicts the folklore that mustard "spares LAB."** AITC is biocidal to LAB at *lower* concentration than almost any other spice essential oil tested. Selectivity versus yeast is only about **2×** (40 vs 60–80 ppm) — a narrow margin, not a categorical sparing.

**Applied corroboration in a LAB-fermented food matrix:** 0.1 % (≈1000 ppm) mustard EO in **idli batter** reduced LAB by **1.11 log₁₀ CFU/g at day 3 (30 °C)** and **0.87 log₁₀ at day 30 (4 °C)**; yeast was reduced by **2.41 log₁₀** (30 °C, 5 d) and 1.84 log₁₀ (4 °C, 30 d). Shelf life extended to 5 d at 30 °C and 30 d at 4 °C. Same source. **Confidence: STRONG.**

**Corroborating LAB-suppression values (secondary):**
- Shofran et al. 1998, *J Food Sci* 63(4): AITC MIC **50–1000 ppm** against bacteria, **1–50 ppm** against yeast. **Confidence: MODERATE** — primary not retrieved; reproduced as a secondary citation.
- Ko et al. 2012, *Int J Food Microbiol* 153(1–2):92–98: **0.1 % encapsulated AITC** reduced *L. plantarum* and *L. mesenteroides* growth to **half of control** at 30 °C/24 h. **Confidence: MODERATE** (abstract only). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22104119/
- Inatsu et al. 2005, *J Food Sci* 70(9): AITC suppressed LAB in **fermented Chinese cabbage** from **2.85 → 2.0 log₁₀ CFU/g after 4 days at 10 °C**. **Confidence: MODERATE** (secondary).

### 6b.4 The critical caveat — AITC destroys its own efficacy

AITC is **more stable at 4 °C than at 28–30 °C**, and its antimicrobial activity is **strongly pH-dependent — roughly 3× more effective at neutral pH than at pH 5.0** (Olaimat & Holley 2016, via Regubalan & Ananthanarayan 2018; **Confidence: MODERATE, secondary**). The idli-batter trial lost its AITC effect **after day 5 at 30 °C**.

**This is the single most important practical point about mustard in pickling.** A vegetable ferment acidifies within days. The acidification that mustard fails to prevent is exactly what switches off the mustard. The effect is inherently transient and self-limiting — which is a plausible mechanical explanation for why mustard seed has a long culinary history in pickling without ever having been shown to stop a lactic fermentation.

### 6b.5 Mustard in pickling — what the evidence actually supports, and what it does not

**Against "mustard spares LAB":** items 6b.2 above (biocidal at 60–80 ppm; 0.2 % AITC in kimchi gave significantly higher pH than control at 4 and 10 °C over 15 days, Ko et al. 2012, secondary).

**Complicating cultural evidence — mustard is also a fermentation substrate:** gundruk (Nepal/India) is fermented *B. juncea* mustard leaf, **no salt**, 15–22 days, dominated by *L. fermentum*, *L. plantarum*, *L. casei*, *Pediococcus pentosaceus*. Inziangsang (Nagaland/Manipur) is mustard leaf, 20–30 °C, 7–10 days. Khalpi (Nepal, cucumber) ferments 3–5 days and mustard **oil is added after fermentation** as a dressing, not during. Source: Swain et al. 2014, *Biotechnol Res Int* 2014:250424, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4058509/ — **Confidence: MODERATE.** *Caveat: mustard leaves are low in sinigrin, so these do not test AITC.*

**DATA NOT FOUND — explicit:**
- **No controlled quantitative study** measuring mustard seed or powder added to **cucumber brine** with fermentation-rate endpoints (days to pH target, LAB counts).
- **No study** on mustard/AITC in **sauerkraut** with LAB-rate endpoints. (Shelef 1983 is widely cited as claiming AITC inhibits *Acetobacter aceti*, *Saccharomyces ellipsoideus*, *S. cerevisiae* and *Mycoderma vini* "in pickles and sauerkraut" — but that is a 1983 secondary review citation, not retrievable, and it concerns **spoilage yeasts and acetic acid bacteria, not LAB**.)
- **No data at all** on AITC or mustard against *Oenococcus oeni* or malolactic fermentation in wine.
- **No study found that measures the AITC concentration actually achieved in a pickle brine.**

## 6c — HOT PEPPERS: capsaicin and capsaicinoids

### 6c.1 The Scoville conversion — verified, with a correction

**The conversion is 16.1 SHU per ppm capsaicin/dihydrocapsaicin, not 15, and it is compound-specific.**

| Statement | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **16.1 SHU per ppm** capsaicin *and* dihydrocapsaicin; **9.3 SHU per ppm** for the other capsaicinoids | *Current Research in Food Science* 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9735263/ ; *Food Chemistry: X* 2024, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11245976/ | STRONG |
| "16 SHUs is equivalent to a capsaicin content of 1 mg/kg" | *Case Reports in Medicine* 2020, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7330639/ | MODERATE |
| Composite formula **SHU = M × 0.9 × 16.1×10³ + M × 0.1 × 9.3×10³** (M = total capsaicinoids in g/kg) | *Molecules* 2024, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11643616/ | MODERATE |

**So the question's "~16 ppm capsaicin ≈ 250 SHU" should be restated: 250 SHU ≈ 15.5 ppm capsaicin.** The "15" in circulation is a rounded approximation, not the analytical standard.

**⚠️ VERIFICATION FLAG:** the primary texts of **ASTA Method 21.3** and **AOAC Official Method 995.03** are paywalled/member-gated and could **not** be retrieved. The 16.1 / 9.3 coefficients come from peer-reviewed papers *citing* the standard. Do not present "ASTA says 15" (or 16.1) as directly verified.

### 6c.2 Capsaicinoid and sugar content of peppers — the decisive asymmetry

**Korean red pepper powder (gochugaru), two independent Korean datasets:**

| Value | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Capsaicinoids **0.54–290.15 mg%**; **free sugar 16.76–29.92 %**; ASTA colour 79.22–139.09 | Ku, Lee & Park 2012, *J Food Sci Nutr* 17(1):29–35 | STRONG |
| Capsaicinoids **41.7 mg% (Youngyang) to 126.1 mg% (Chungyang)**; **free sugar up to 19.1 %** | Choi, Jeon & Park 2000, *Korean J Food Sci Technol* 32(6):1251–1257 | STRONG |

**The finding that answers part (c):** Choi et al. 2000 report that **Youngyang gochugaru — the type preferentially used for making kimchi — had the highest free sugar but the LOWEST capsaicinoid content.** Traditional selection picked **sugar over heat**. That is the opposite of what a producer would choose if capsaicin were the desired preservative, and exactly what they would choose for reliable, fast, well-flavoured fermentation.

**Fresh chilli cultivars** (capsaicin, mg/kg dry weight, HPLC): Anaheim 0.98; Poblano 2.46; Shishito 6.38; Jalapeño 16.44 ± 4.97; Serrano 1069.32 ± 152.22; Thai 1844.84 ± 239.40; Habanero 4777.56 ± 720.89. Source: *Frontiers in Plant Science* 2026, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13190488/ — **Confidence: WEAK/CONTESTED for absolute values.** The jalapeño figure (~260 SHU equivalent) is roughly an order of magnitude below the literature-typical 2,500–8,000 SHU. Relative ranking is usable; absolute numbers are not.

**Sugar load arithmetic (this report's own calculation, flagged):** at the ~5 % w/w red pepper powder typical of kimchi, gochugaru contributes on the order of **0.8–1.5 % free sugar to the whole batch** — a substantial fermentable carbon source on top of the cabbage's own sugars.

### 6c.3 MIC of capsaicin against LAB — a genuine literature hole

**EXPLICIT NEGATIVE FINDING:** after systematic searching (Europe PMC REST API, NCBI PMC full-text, PubMed, general web), **no peer-reviewed MIC or MBC value for capsaicin against any *Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, *Pediococcus* or *Weissella* strain was found.** This is a gap in the literature, not a search failure — capsaicin's antimicrobial testing has focused on pathogens.

The indirect evidence **consistently indicates capsaicin does NOT inhibit LAB at realistic food concentrations**:

1. **The kimchi capsaicinoid dose–response (best available evidence).** Kimchi prepared with capsaicinoids at **4, 12, 30.7, 40.9 and 50.3 mg/kg**, stored 4 °C for 28 days: **pH and titratable acidity did NOT differ significantly among capsaicinoid concentrations.** Day 0 pH 5.36–5.52; all samples except the 50.3 mg/kg one fell **below pH 4.0** by day 28. **LAB abundance rapidly increased in all samples** until day 21. Total viable bacteria rose from 4.24–4.52 to a peak of **6.98–7.11 log CFU/g at day 14**. Lactic acid was *highest* in the *high*-capsaicinoid kimchi. Capsaicinoid concentration did not change over 28 days. *Heliyon* 2024, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10831603/ — **Confidence: MODERATE.**
   **⚠️ FLAG — the paper contradicts itself:** its abstract and highlights claim *"Capsaicinoids lower pH and raise titratable acidity in kimchi samples,"* but its Results state *"pH and titratable acidity did not significantly differ with capsaicinoid concentration among kimchi samples."* The **Results** text is reported here. Cite this paper carefully.
2. **Commercial kimchi correlation:** across 13 commercial kimchis, capsaicinoid content correlated **positively with total acidity** and **negatively with pH** — the opposite of inhibition. *Foods* 2021, 10(1):86, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7829842/ — **Confidence: MODERATE** (n = 13, correlational).
3. **Capsaicin-tolerant LAB exist and have been isolated** from pickled pepper juice, implying selection pressure but providing no MIC. **Confidence: WEAK.**

### 6c.4 Does chilli slow kimchi? — two studies that answer different questions

**Study A — red pepper POWDER (with vs without): SLOWS fermentation.**
Jeong et al. 2013, *Int J Food Microbiol* 160(3):252–259. Baechu and Mu kimchi, with and without red pepper powder, **40 days**. *"The lag phases of kimchi fermentation were clearly extended by the addition of red pepper powder."* Without red pepper, *Weissella* was negligible; with it, *Weissella* was much higher while *Leuconostoc* and *Lactobacillus* were lower. ¹H-NMR confirmed fermentation progressed *"a little more slowly."* **Confidence: STRONG for direction, MODERATE for magnitude** — the exact pH/day values are paywalled. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23290236/

**Study B — capsaicinoid CONCENTRATION at constant powder mass: NO significant effect.**
*Heliyon* 2024 (above): total red pepper powder was held at **0.6 kg in every batch**, varying only capsaicinoid concentration (4 → 50.3 mg/kg). **No significant pH or TA difference between treatments.** Only LAB *community composition* changed.

**Resolution — and it is not the intuitive answer.** Because the pH/TA effect **vanished** when powder mass was held constant and only capsaicinoids varied, **the fermentation-slowing effect of red pepper powder is NOT primarily capsaicinoid-mediated.** Plausible alternatives: the powder's native microbiota, non-capsaicinoid antimicrobials, dilution/buffering of the cabbage by inert powder mass, or a physical matrix effect.

**Honest bottom line for (c): capsaicin at realistic kimchi concentrations (4–50 mg/kg) does not measurably slow kimchi fermentation or inhibit its LAB. Red pepper *powder* does slow early fermentation, but by a mechanism the current evidence does not support as capsaicinoid-mediated. On the "which effect wins" question: the sugar load is large, well quantified, and accelerating; the capsaicinoid effect on rate is not detectable. Sugar wins, and by the better evidence.**

**Capsaicin's actual antimicrobial spectrum** (for completeness): MIC **1.2 µg/mL** vs *S. aureus*, **10 µg/mL** vs *P. aeruginosa*, **25 µg/mL** vs *C. albicans* — all **WEAK** (secondary citations, primaries not retrieved). Activity ranking by agar diffusion + MIC + MBC + SEM: *Bacillus cereus* > *B. subtilis* > *S. aureus* > *E. coli* (Muangkote et al. 2019, *Food Sci Biotechnol* 28:303–310, MODERATE).

**DATA NOT FOUND:** numeric days-to-pH for kimchi with vs without red pepper (Jeong 2013 holds this data; paywalled); any quantitative pepper-mash or gochujang fermentation kinetics vs capsaicinoid content; the review *"Antimicrobial Properties of Capsaicin"* (identified, full text inaccessible — likely the best single source for capsaicin MIC tables); a peer-reviewed mg/100 g fresh-weight capsaicinoid table for jalapeño/cayenne/habanero from a primary open-access HPLC source.

## 6d — TANNINS: grape leaves, oak leaves, tea, bay leaves, horseradish leaves

### 6d.1 ⚠️ First correction: the grape-leaf principle is a CONDENSED tannin, not tannic acid

Popular accounts attribute pickle crispness to "tannic acid." The classic USDA ARS work identifies the active principle specifically:

- A **heat-stable, non-dialyzable, high-molecular-weight condensed tannin** (a catechin/leucoanthocyanidin polymer), **MW 17,000–20,000 Da**, giving **positive vanillin-HCl and leucoanthocyanidin tests**.
- Mechanism: **competitive inhibition of polygalacturonase** (the pectinase that softens tissue) — not a general antimicrobial effect.
- It is precipitated by caffeine, nicotine sulfate and gelatin.

Sources: **Bell TA & Etchells JL (1958), *Botanical Gazette* 119(3):192–196**, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/336153 (paywalled; abstract retrieved via the USDA compilation below); **Bell TA, Etchells JL & Smart WWG (1965), *Botanical Gazette* 126(1):40–45**, https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p80.pdf · **Confidence: STRONG** — USDA ARS Food Fermentation Laboratory, Raleigh NC.

### 6d.2 Concentration in plant material

| Material | Inhibitor content | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Muscadine grape leaf (*Vitis rotundifolia*)** | **1,034 mg freeze-dried inhibitor per 100 g FRESH leaf = ~1.03 % of fresh weight** (highest of 7 species screened) | Bell, Etchells & Smart 1965 (as above) | STRONG (single lab, single season) |
| Sericea | 1,360 mg/100 g FW | same | STRONG |
| Persimmon | 1,309 mg/100 g FW | same | STRONG |
| Dogwood | 150 mg/100 g FW | same | STRONG |
| Raspberry | 54 mg/100 g FW | same | STRONG |
| Rose | 41 mg/100 g FW | same | STRONG |
| **Concord grape (*V. labrusca*) leaf** | **~5× LESS inhibitor than muscadine** | same | STRONG |

**Variety ranking (most to least inhibitor): Scuppernong > Sheridan > Concord > Niagara > Portland > Leutie.**

**⚠️ Tea is a POOR source.** In a screen of **61 species across 32 families**, *Thea sinensis* (tea) scored only **1+ (weak, 25–60 % pectinase inhibition)**. **Only 8 of 61 species were strong.** — Bell TA et al. (1962), *Botanical Gazette* 123(3):220–223, https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p72.pdf · **Confidence: STRONG.** *This directly contradicts the common advice to use tea for crispness.*

**⚠️ Total tannin content is a poor proxy for activity.** Sericea total tannin is 7.46–8.94 % of dry weight, yet the authors found *"no apparent relationship between loss of enzyme-inhibitor activity and tannin content."* **Confidence: STRONG.**

**DATA NOT FOUND:** oak-leaf, bay-leaf and horseradish-leaf tannin concentrations (the likely primary sources are behind ScienceDirect's HTTP 403). Genuine gap.

### 6d.3 Enzyme inhibition — the quantitative core (this is the real mechanism)

**Isolated inhibitor, in the final enzyme–substrate solution:**

| Concentration | Effect |
|---|---|
| **5 ppm** | **~50 % reduction** of cucumber-flower pectinase |
| **25 ppm** | **>90 % inhibition** of commercial pectinase 46AP |
| **100 ppm** | **>95 % inhibition of BOTH pectinase and cellulase** |

The relationship between log(concentration) and % inhibition is a **straight line** (first-order-type). **Confidence: STRONG** (Bell, Etchells & Smart 1965).

**Crude muscadine leaf extract** (Bell 1962, Table 2):

| Extract concentration | Pectinase inhibition |
|---|---|
| 0.7 mg/mL | 28 % |
| 3.0 mg/mL | 92 % |
| 8.3 mg/mL | 95 % |
| 16.7 mg/mL | 95 % |

→ **IC₅₀ ≈ 1–2 mg/mL crude extract.** A **5 % aqueous grape-leaf extract** inhibited cellulase 98–99 % and commercial 19AP 50–70 % (Bell, Aurad & Etchells 1960, *Botanical Gazette* 122(2):143–148, https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p65.pdf). **Confidence: STRONG.**

### 6d.4 ⚠️ FIRMNESS: no instrumental data exists — do not quote a number in newtons

**This is an explicit negative finding and an important one.** The entire USDA ARS Raleigh corpus (Bell, Etchells, Fleming, McFeeters) measured firmness by **sensory panel rating**, not instrumentation. The direction is clear and consistent — *"higher levels of inhibitor resulted in increasingly higher firmness ratings of cured salt-stock cucumbers; rated Good to Excellent"* — but **the magnitude is not quantified anywhere as g force, N, shear, or % firmness retention.**

**Anyone quoting "grape leaves add X newtons of firmness" is extrapolating from data that does not exist.** **DATA NOT FOUND.** If a firmness figure in N is needed, it requires new experimental work.

### 6d.5 Fermentation rate: grape leaves do NOT slow acidification — two independent confirmations

**This is the direct answer to part (d) of the question, and it is a clean negative.**

1. **Etchells JL, Bell TA & Williams CH (1958), *Food Technology* 12:204–208:** *"There was **no apparent influence** [of] the grape leaf inhibitor on the character of the acid fermentation as measured by **total brine acidity, pH, and optical density of the brine**."* **Confidence: STRONG** for the statement (USDA ARS); **MODERATE** for the evidentiary basis — retrieved as a USDA abstract, not with full tables.
2. **Staninska-Pięta J et al. (2024), *Sustainability* 16(6):2431**, https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/16/6/2431 — cucumbers **300 g** + grapevine leaves **7 g or 14 g**, **2 % saline**, **14 days at 22 °C** → **final pH 3.50–3.58**, and *"none of the experimental variants showed statistically significant changes in the kinetics of pH compared to the control."* **Confidence: STRONG** (controlled, replicated, modern).
   *Bonus findings from the same trial:* grape leaves raised brine **ellagic acid by +105 % / +148 %** and **gallic acid by +91 % / +131 %**; leaf addition **significantly increased plantaricin and acidocin gene expression**; and leaf-containing variants scored **higher consumer desirability**.

**Conclusion for (d): tannins are a first-order effect on TEXTURE and a second-order effect on FERMENTATION RATE — in fact no measurable effect on rate at all.** They act on the vegetable's own pectinolytic enzymes, not on LAB.

### 6d.6 Why tannins do not inhibit LAB — LAB are tannin-tolerant and tannase-positive

**Tannic acid tolerance, *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* WCFS1** (Reverón I et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8(6):e66473, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679024/):

| Tannic acid | Effect |
|---|---|
| 0.5 mM (≈850 µg/mL) | **Lag phase extended from 4 h to 16 h**; ~1 log viability loss during lag; cells malformed — **but the population recovers and resumes growth** |
| up to 2.5 mM (≈4,250 µg/mL) | **Growth resumes** |

**Genetic basis:** **all 29 *L. plantarum* strains tested carry the tannase gene *tanB_Lp*** (*tanA_Lp* in only 4/29) — Jiménez N et al. 2014, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* 80(11):3433, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4018929/. And cell-free extracts of *L. plantarum* gave **almost complete degradation** of commercial tannic acids to gallic acid and pyrogallol — Rodríguez H et al. 2008, *Food Chemistry* 108(3):853. **Confidence: STRONG.**

⚠️ **A methodological caveat that invalidates much of the tannin-MIC literature:** **MRS broth proteins precipitate at 1 mM tannic acid**, forcing researchers to use defined media. **Most published "tannin MIC in MRS" values are therefore artefacts.** **Confidence: STRONG** (reported explicitly in Reverón 2013). **DATA NOT FOUND:** a clean MIC/MBC of tannic acid or grape/oak-leaf extract against *L. plantarum*, *L. mesenteroides* or *L. brevis* — only tolerance data exist; and no MIC at all for *Leuconostoc* or *L. brevis*.

### 6d.7 Practical dose, and a genuine surprise about dried leaves

**Peer-reviewed traditional dose:** **7 g (single) and 14 g (double) fresh grapevine leaves per 300 g cucumbers** = **~23 g and ~47 g fresh leaf per kg cucumber** (Staninska-Pięta et al. 2024).

**Resulting brine concentration (this report's own estimate — flagged as such, MODERATE confidence):** at ~1.03 % inhibitor in fresh muscadine leaf, 23–47 g leaf/kg would give **~240–480 ppm** extracted inhibitor *if extraction were complete*; realistically **~50–100 ppm** with ordinary *V. vinifera* leaves (which contain ~5× less than muscadine). **That lands exactly on the 5–100 ppm effective threshold from §6d.3 — so the tradition is quantitatively sensible, and dose-sensitive.**

**⭐ A practically important finding: DRYING DESTROYS MORE THAN HALF THE ACTIVITY.** *"The inhibitor substance in grape leaves has been shown to be **reduced more than one-half when dried**"* (55 °C, 24 h). **Commercial dried grape leaves may retain <50 % activity.** The inhibitor itself is heat-stable (lost only **12 %** in a boiling water bath), so the loss is a drying effect, not a heat effect. **Use fresh leaves.** **Confidence: STRONG** (USDA ARS).

**DATA NOT FOUND:** a peer-reviewed source for the common "1–2 grape leaves per quart" instruction — that figure exists only as consumer-level repetition. Illinois Extension confirms the tannin/pectinase mechanism and notes that **removing the blossom end makes grape leaves unnecessary** (the blossom end is the pectinase source), and warns about bitterness: https://extension.illinois.edu/food-preservation/pickling-faq · **Confidence: MODERATE** (extension guidance, no numbers).

## 6e — CINNAMALDEHYDE, EUGENOL, CARVACROL, THYMOL

### 6e.1 The direct sauerkraut experiment — the strongest single piece of evidence in this whole report

**Tuțulescu F, Ionică ME, Stoica F (2026). "The Influence of Incorporating Essential Plant Oils in the Lactic Fermentation Process of Cabbage on the Quality of Sauerkraut." *Foods* 15(15):2746.**
University of Craiova, Romania. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13465722/ · doi 10.3390/foods15152746

- **Design:** 'De Buzău' white cabbage, shredded, **2.0 kg per 5 L jar**; emulsified brine with **2.5 % NaCl** at 2.5 L per jar; essential oil at **0.015 % (= 150 ppm = 150 µg/mL)**; **fermented at 14 °C or 22 °C for 12 days**; turned every 3 days; triplicate; LSD test, *p* < 0.05. LAB counted on MRS agar, anaerobic, 30 °C/3 days.
- **Oils tested:** thyme (*Satureja hortensis*), dill (*Anethum graveolens*), wild thyme (*Thymus serpyllum*), laurel (*Laurus nobilis*), basil (*Ocimum basilicum*) — all hydrodistilled in-house (yields 0.6–1.4 mL/100 g dry weight).
- **The authors' own stated intent:** *"The concentrations were selected to be lower than those reported in the literature in order not to inhibit beneficial bacteria."* **The thyme result contradicted that intent.**
- **LAB counts (log CFU, mean ± SD, n = 3):**

| Variant | Oil | Day 6 | Day 12 |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | **none (control, 14 °C)** | 3.60 ± 0.16 | **6.47 ± 0.30** |
| M2 | **none (control, 22 °C)** | 3.04 ± 0.13 | **6.30 ± 0.29** |
| **V1.1** | **thyme, 14 °C** | **1.99 ± 0.08** | **4.06 ± 0.19** |
| **V2.1** | **thyme, 22 °C** | **1.98 ± 0.07** | **4.30 ± 0.19** |
| V1.2 | dill, 14 °C | 2.54 ± 0.11 | 5.30 ± 0.24 |
| V2.2 | dill, 22 °C | 3.36 ± 0.14 | 5.91 ± 0.27 |
| V1.3 | wild thyme, 14 °C | 3.00 ± 0.12 | 6.30 ± 0.30 |
| V2.3 | wild thyme, 22 °C | 3.06 ± 0.13 | 5.60 ± 0.25 |
| V1.4 | laurel, 14 °C | 3.36 ± 0.15 | 6.39 ± 0.29 |
| V2.4 | laurel, 22 °C | 3.30 ± 0.14 | 5.90 ± 0.28 |
| V1.5 | basil, 14 °C | 3.61 ± 0.17 | 6.50 ± 0.31 |
| V2.5 | basil, 22 °C | 3.69 ± 0.18 | 5.87 ± 0.27 |

- **Terminal pH of the finished sauerkraut ranged from 3.14 to 4.91.** The **lowest** pH was **dill EO at 22 °C (3.14)**; the **highest was thyme EO at 22 °C (4.91)**.
- **⚠️ The safety implication is the headline and must not be missed: thyme EO at 150 ppm left the product at pH 4.91 — above the 4.6 regulatory threshold for acidified foods (§8a).** A culinary-level thyme oil addition did not merely slow the fermentation; it produced a product that is **not an acidified food** and would require refrigeration or a different scheduled process.
- Residual sugar: fresh cabbage **3.34 g/100 g fw**; controls finished with **0.12–0.29 g/100 g**; **all EO variants finished with sugar undetected.**
- TSS: fresh cabbage **5.5 %**; rose to 6.0–6.8 % after fermentation; fell to 4.0–4.8 % after 6 months at 5 °C.
- **Confidence: MODERATE.** The direction and ranking are clear and statistically tested, but: n = 3; only **total** LAB counts (no species-level ID — the authors state this explicitly as a limitation); sensory panel only n = 10; a single cabbage cultivar and a single EO concentration; and the 2026 publication date means no independent replication yet.
- **⚠️ Discrepancy worth flagging:** 150 ppm of a whole essential oil corresponds to only ~40–75 ppm thymol/carvacrol, which is *below* the broth MICs usually reported for thymol and carvacrol against LAB (see §6e.2). Either the whole oil is more potent than its major components in isolation, there is synergy among minor constituents, or the 12-day continuous exposure at 14–22 °C in an acidic matrix is more damaging than a 24 h broth MIC assay suggests. **This tension is unresolved and is a reason to treat any single MIC-to-recipe extrapolation with caution.**

### 6e.3 ⚠️ A 100-fold CONTRADICTION on AITC vs LAB — reported, not resolved

**Two good datasets disagree by roughly two orders of magnitude, and the disagreement matters:**

| Study | Method | Finding for AITC vs LAB |
|---|---|---|
| **Regubalan & Ananthanarayan 2018**, *J Food Sci Technol* 55(9):3417–3426 (mustard EO, declared 99 % AITC) | **Broth dilution**, 37 °C/48 h, ~5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL, triplicate | **Biocidal at 60–80 ppm** vs *L. mesenteroides*, *L. plantarum*, *L. casei*, *P. pentosaceus* (80 ppm) and *L. acidophilus* (60 ppm) |
| **Dunn, Davidson & Critzer 2016**, *J Food Sci* 81(2):M438–M444 (AITC) | **Agar incorporation** in MRS agar | **No MIC up to 7,500 ppm** against any of 9 LAB species |

**This is an unresolved ~100× discrepancy and it must be reported as CONTESTED, not averaged away.**

**The most likely explanation, and it is mechanistically important:** **AITC is highly volatile.** In a closed broth system it is retained and can act; on an agar plate incubated over days it largely partitions into the headspace and is lost. Subagent A's own research independently establishes that AITC's **vapour-phase** activity is the basis of its commercial use in active packaging — i.e. the compound's defining physical behaviour is escaping into air.

**Practical consequence, which holds under either MIC value:** in an **open or semi-open ferment** (a crock, a jar with an airlock, a barrel), AITC will progressively leave the brine. Whichever in-vitro MIC is correct, **the concentration actually present in the liquid phase of a fermenting vegetable will be far below it.** This is consistent with the absence of any study showing mustard seed stopping a lactic fermentation.

**Note also:** subagent A found AITC is **more potent against the one yeast tested (40 ppm) than against LAB (60–80 ppm)** — a ~2× margin, not a categorical sparing. Subagent B found **no LAB inhibition at all to 7,500 ppm**, which would make AITC the *most LAB-sparing* compound tested. **The two conclusions point in opposite directions on the "does mustard spare LAB" question.** The folklore is supported by Dunn 2016 and contradicted by Regubalan 2018.

**A convergence worth noting despite the conflict:** both datasets agree that AITC's action is **transient and self-limiting** in a ferment — one because it is lost to volatility, the other because its activity is **pH-dependent, roughly 3× weaker at pH 5.0 than at neutral pH** (Olaimat & Holley 2016, via Regubalan 2018). **A vegetable ferment acidifies within days; the acidification that mustard fails to prevent is what switches off the mustard.**

### 6e.6 ⭐ CLOVE: the one spice that demonstrably breaks a real fermentation — and the worst possible selectivity

**Kang M-J, Park S-Y, Yoo K-S (2019). "Effects of clove powder on the quality and fermentation of kimchi." *Food Science & Nutrition* 7(2).**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6392875/

**Design:** kimchi paste + **clove powder at 0.5 / 1.0 / 1.5 / 2.0 % w/w**, fermented at **10 °C for 20 days**, triplicate, 5 timepoints.

| Time | LAB (log CFU/g) — control | LAB — 1.0 % clove | LAB — 2.0 % clove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 5** | **7.57** | **4.47** | **3.31** |
| **Day 20** | **6.73** | — | **1.30** |

**→ A 2.0 % clove powder addition suppressed LAB by ~4.3 log at day 5 and ~5.4 log by day 20.**

**And the selectivity is exactly backwards:**

| Organism group | Control | 2.0 % clove |
|---|---|---|
| **LAB** | 6.73–7.57 | **1.30–3.31** |
| **Yeasts + moulds** | **5.45–5.52** | **5.18–5.39** — *essentially unchanged* |

**Confidence: STRONG** (triplicate, 20 days, 5 timepoints, dose-response).

**⚠️ This is the single most important practical finding for Question 6(f): clove at a realistic spicing level (0.5–2 % w/w, delivering ~250–2,500 ppm eugenol) removes the protective LAB without removing the spoilage yeasts.** That is the worst possible outcome — it is the opposite of what a preservative spice is supposed to do, and it is the mechanism by which a spice can genuinely ruin a ferment.

**Why clove behaves this way and cinnamon does not:** at 1 g/kg clove delivers 49–125 ppm eugenol against a LAB MIC of ~2,120 ppm *and* a yeast MIC of 1,067–4,268 ppm — the LAB and yeast windows **overlap**, so there is no dose at which clove suppresses yeast while sparing LAB. Cinnamon is the reverse: yeast/mould MIC 33–263 ppm is **8–64× below** the LAB MIC of ~2,100 ppm.

### 6e.7 The sauerkraut EO trial, re-read with MICs in hand

Returning to Tuțulescu et al. 2026 (§6e.1): **thyme EO at 150 ppm cut LAB by ~2.4 log at day 12 and left the product at pH 4.91.**

Reconciled against Dunn 2016: 150 ppm of a whole thyme EO is roughly **60 ppm thymol** — **far below** the ~1,000 ppm agar MIC for thymol against LAB. So the sauerkraut result is **not explainable by thymol concentration alone** against the Dunn MIC. Possible explanations, all unresolved:
- **Agar vs food matrix:** the agar MIC systematically over-estimates (see §6e.5's 25× spread between Dunn 2016 and the Speranza meta-analysis).
- **12-day continuous exposure at 14–22 °C in an acidified matrix** is far more damaging than a 24 h broth or plate MIC assay implies.
- **Synergy among minor EO constituents** not present in single-compound tests.
- **pH interaction:** undissociated organic acids plus membrane-active terpenes are known to potentiate each other.

**This tension is unresolved and is the strongest single reason to distrust any direct MIC-to-recipe extrapolation in either direction.** The empirical result — a real fermentation measurably broken by a culinary-level thyme oil addition — stands on its own and should be treated as the primary evidence.

## 6f — WHICH OF THESE ACTUALLY SLOW FERMENTATION AT REALISTIC RECIPE AMOUNTS?

### 6f.1 The organising principle

**A spice can only inhibit a fermentation if the active compound reaches its LAB MIC in the aqueous phase of the ferment.** Four filters cut the candidate list down hard, and each is documented above with numbers:

1. **Extraction efficiency.** Most active compounds are volatile, lipophilic terpenoids, thiols and phenolics. A vegetable ferment is an **aqueous**, room-temperature, days-to-weeks system. Whole spice in a brine extracts poorly — measured: a cinnamon **water** extract contains **~20× less cinnamaldehyde** than an 80 % ethanol extract, and performed correspondingly worse (§6e.5).
2. **Dose.** Recipe levels of whole spice are typically **0.1–3 % w/w**. Even a spice that is 1–5 % active compound by weight delivers only **~1–125 ppm** to the brine at 1 g/kg (§6e.5). LAB MICs cluster at **1,000–2,100 ppm**.
3. **Volatility/loss.** AITC is lost to the headspace; this is the likely explanation of the 100× MIC discrepancy (§6e.3) and is the basis of its use in *packaging*, not in brines.
4. **Time and pH.** Ferments run for days to weeks; AITC is ~3× weaker at pH 5.0 than at neutral pH and lost its effect after day 5 at 30 °C (§6b.4).

### 6f.2 The ranking, with evidence

**TIER 1 — Demonstrably able to break or substantially alter a real fermentation at realistic levels**

**1. CLOVE (eugenol) — the single clearest demonstration, and the worst selectivity.**
**0.5–2.0 % clove powder in kimchi paste suppressed LAB by 3.3–5.4 log while leaving yeasts and moulds essentially untouched** (LAB day 20: control 6.73 → 2.0 % clove **1.30**; yeasts+moulds: control 5.45–5.52 → 2.0 % clove **5.18–5.39**). *Kang, Park & Yoo 2019, Food Sci Nutr 7(2)* — **STRONG** (triplicate, 20 d, 5 timepoints, dose-response).
⚠️ **This is the anti-preservative outcome.** Clove removes the protective LAB without removing the spoilage organisms — the exact opposite of what a preservative spice should do. Its LAB and yeast MIC windows **overlap** (LAB ~2,120 ppm; yeasts 1,067–4,268 ppm), so **there is no dose at which clove suppresses yeast while sparing LAB.**

**2. THYME essential oil — the only agent that measurably broke a SAUERKRAUT fermentation at a deliberately sub-inhibitory dose.**
**150 ppm thyme EO → ~2.4 log LAB suppression at day 12 (control 6.47 → thyme 4.06) and terminal pH 4.91** — above the 4.6 regulatory threshold. *Tuțulescu, Ionică & Stoica 2026, Foods 15(15):2746* — **MODERATE** (n = 3, one cultivar, one dose, total LAB counts only, no species ID). **The authors specifically chose the dose to be lower than literature inhibitory levels; the thyme result contradicted that intent.** ⚠️ The safety implication is the headline: **a culinary-level thyme oil addition produced a product that is not an acidified food.** ⚠️ **Caveat: this is essential oil, not dried herb.** Dried thyme leaf at 1 g/kg delivers only ~**3–13 ppm** thymol — roughly two orders of magnitude less.

**TIER 2 — Real, repeatable, quantitatively large effect — but against SPOILAGE YEAST, not LAB**

**3. GARLIC — the most useful agent in the list, for the opposite reason to the folklore.**
- A clean controlled demonstration of **direct LAB suppression** exists **only when garlic's own microbiota is removed by autoclaving** (*Choi H-W et al. 2024, Heliyon 10(2):e24503* — STRONG for direction; G0 vs G1/G2 significantly different at day 5 in viable cells, lactic acid, pH and TA).
- In **real kimchi** at 0/1/2/4 % garlic, the **4 % batch acidified FASTER** than the 0 % batch — lactic acid **29.85 vs 6.37 mM at week 1** (*Jang et al. 2024, Heliyon 10(2):e24919* — STRONG). Garlic supplies LAB and fermentable substrate; at kimchi-typical rates the net effect on **rate** is neutral-to-accelerating.
- **Garlic's large, repeatable, practically important effect is on spoilage yeast:** white colony-forming yeast appeared **only in kimchi without garlic at day 100** (*Choi H-W et al. 2024, LWT 198*), and a garlic-powder spray **delayed white-colony formation by an average of 17 days at 10 °C** (*Kim M-J et al. 2021, Foods 10(3):645* — STRONG).
- **Mechanistic gotcha: raw garlic works; processed garlic often does not.** Raw filtrate outperformed 30 % freeze-dried garlic powder **despite the powder containing more alliin (93.38 vs 60.43 mg/L)** — because alliin is the inactive precursor and drying destroys alliinase. Garlic concentrate showed **no** anti-yeast activity at all.

**TIER 3 — Low in-vitro MICs, but whole-spice usage levels fall below them by 1–3 orders of magnitude**

**4. CINNAMON (cinnamaldehyde) — the right selectivity window, the wrong dose.**
- **Uniquely favourable window:** spoilage yeasts/moulds inhibited at **33–263 µg/mL** vs a LAB MIC of **~2,100 µg/mL** — an **8–64× gap pointing the right way.** The only compound of the four where that holds (*Nißl et al. 2021, J Fungi 7(10):872; Dunn et al. 2016, J Food Sci 81(2):M438*).
- **But 1 g cinnamon bark/kg delivers only ~6–20 ppm cinnamaldehyde — 100–350× below the LAB MIC and ~3–22× below even the yeast MIC.** Reaching the yeast MIC needs on the order of **10–20 g/kg** (depending on the bark's actual cinnamaldehyde content).
- Documented real effect in kimchi is **anti-yeast**, achieved with a *concentrated ethanol extract* (10 % FGP + 1.75 % CEE → 17-day delay in white colony), not with whole cinnamon.

**5. TANNINS (grape leaves etc.) — second-order for rate (in fact, no measurable effect on rate), first-order for texture.**
- **Enzyme inhibition is potent and quantified: 5 ppm → ~50 %, 25 ppm → >90 %, 100 ppm → >95 %** inhibition of pectinase (and cellulase at 100 ppm).
- **But there is NO measurable effect on fermentation rate**, confirmed twice: USDA ARS 1958 (*"no apparent influence… on total brine acidity, pH, and optical density"*) and a 2024 controlled trial (pH 3.50–3.58, *"no statistically significant changes in the kinetics of pH"*).
- LAB are **tannin-tolerant and tannase-positive** — *L. plantarum* resumes growth up to 2.5 mM (~4,250 µg/mL) tannic acid, and all 29 strains tested carry *tanB_Lp*.
- **Tannins act on the vegetable's pectinolytic enzymes, not on LAB.** And **drying destroys >50 % of the activity** — use fresh leaves.

**TIER 4 — Contested**

**6. MUSTARD / AITC — genuinely unresolved, with a physical reason to expect little practical effect.**
- **Two good datasets disagree by ~100×**: broth dilution gives a LAB MIC of **60–80 ppm** (Regubalan & Anantharayan 2018); agar incorporation gives **no MIC up to 7,500 ppm** (Dunn et al. 2016).
- **Most likely resolution: AITC is volatile.** A closed broth retains it; an agar plate loses it to the headspace. **The same physics means an open crock loses it too** — so the *effective* brine concentration is likely far below either MIC.
- **Both datasets agree the effect is transient**: volatile loss, plus **~3× weaker activity at pH 5.0 than at neutral pH**, plus loss of effect after day 5 at 30 °C.
- **No study was found** measuring mustard seed or powder in cucumber brine or sauerkraut with fermentation-rate endpoints, and **none measuring the AITC actually achieved in a pickle brine**.

**TIER 5 — Does not slow fermentation at realistic levels**

**7. HOT PEPPER / CAPSAICIN.**
- **No MIC against LAB exists anywhere in the literature** (genuine gap).
- A controlled dose–response found **no significant pH or TA difference** across capsaicinoid concentrations from **4 to 50.3 mg/kg** (*Heliyon 2024*); commercial kimchi shows capsaicinoid content **positively** correlated with acidity.
- Red pepper **powder** does extend the kimchi lag phase, but **not via capsaicinoids** — the effect vanished when powder mass was held constant.
- Gochugaru carries **16.8–29.9 % free sugar** and **accelerates** fermentation. **Sugar wins, and by the better evidence.**

### 6f.3 The explicit statement the question asks for

**Most spices are used at levels below their LAB-inhibitory concentrations.** The arithmetic is unambiguous: whole dried spices at 0.1–3 % w/w deliver **~1–125 ppm** of active compound to the brine, while measured LAB MICs cluster at **1,000–2,100 ppm**. The gap is **one to three orders of magnitude**, and it is *worsened* by the aqueous-extraction problem (cinnamon water extract ≈ 1/20 of the ethanol extract) and by volatility (AITC).

**There are exactly three ways to actually exceed a LAB MIC in a real ferment, and each has a documented cost:**

| Route | Evidence | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Use an essential oil** rather than the whole spice | Thyme EO 150 ppm → 2.4 log LAB suppression, terminal pH 4.91 | Breaks the fermentation; can leave the product **unsafe** (above pH 4.6) |
| **Use a spice at genuinely high dose** | Clove 1–2 % w/w → 3.3–5.4 log LAB suppression | Kills LAB while **sparing the spoilage yeast** — worst possible selectivity |
| **Use a high-AITC mustard preparation** | Mustard EO biocidal at 60–80 ppm *in broth* | Contested by agar data; AITC is volatile and pH-sensitive, so the effect **self-limits** |

**And the item that actually works as a preservative in this list is garlic — but against yeast, not LAB, and only if it is raw.** The folklore has the right ingredient for the wrong reason.

### 6f.4 The one-sentence answer to "which effect wins"

**For every ingredient except clove and concentrated essential oils, the answer is that no spice effect wins at all** — salt concentration, temperature, and the fermentation's own accumulating lactic acid are one to three orders of magnitude more powerful than any spice in this report, and the garlic/Garlic/pepper cases show that where a spice has a measurable effect it is frequently **on the wrong organisms** (spoilage yeast) or **in the wrong direction** (garlic's nutrient and LAB contribution, pepper's sugar load).

## 6g — SALT AND pH: the two levers that actually control the fermentation

### 6g.1 The salt inhibition curve — and the Leuconostoc vs Lactobacillus divergence

**Hong G-H, Lee S-Y, Park E-S, Park K-Y (2021). "Changes in Microbial Community by Salt Content in Kimchi during Fermentation." *Journal of the Korean Society of Food Science and Nutrition* 50(6):648–653.**
CHA University + Yonsei University. https://www.e-jkfn.org/journal/view.html?doi=10.3746/jkfn.2021.50.6.648 · doi 10.3746/jkfn.2021.50.6.648 (Open Access)

- **Design:** kimchi at **1.5 %, 2.5 % and 3.5 % final salt**, **no starter**, fermented **5 ± 1 °C for 4 weeks**, n = 3, Duncan's multiple range test, *p* < 0.05. Cabbage pre-salted in 10 % brine 14–16 h. 16S rRNA V3–V4 NGS at week 3.
- **Rate:** *"Decreasing salt concentration resulted in rapid decrease in the pH and associated increase in acidity."*
- **Genus-level community at week 3 (%) — the key divergence:**

| Genus | 1.5 % salt | 2.5 % salt | 3.5 % salt |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Leuconostoc*** | 5.1 | **9.6** | **1.2** |
| ***Lactobacillus*** | 50.5 | 46.6 | **46.5** |
| *Weissella* | 34.1 | 40.0 | 27.2 |
| *Aerosakkonema* (cyanobacterium, not LAB) | 4.1 | 2.2 | **22.0** |
| Firmicutes (total) | 89.8 | **96.1** | 75.0 |

- **The divergence in one line:** going from 2.5 % to 3.5 % salt cut *Leuconostoc* relative abundance **8-fold (9.6 % → 1.2 %)** while *Lactobacillus* was **essentially unchanged (46.6 % → 46.5 %)**. **Leuconostoc is markedly more salt-sensitive than Lactobacillus.** Total LAB fell from 96.2 % to 74.9 %.
- **Optimum:** the paper concludes **2.5 % salt is optimal**, citing a known kimchi range of **2.0–3.0 %** (Cho 1999; Mheen & Kwon 1984) and a storage optimum of **3 %** (Cha et al. 2003).
- **Confidence: STRONG.** Three salt levels, 4 weeks, triplicate, NGS-resolved to species; the *Leuconostoc*-vs-*Lactobacillus* divergence is large and unambiguous.

**Species detail (week 3):** *Lactobacillus sakei* 50.3 / 46.4 / 46.4 %; *Leuconostoc gelidum* 4.1 / **9.3** / **1.2 %**; *Weissella koreensis* 34.1 / 40.0 / 27.2 %.

**Corroborating dose–response (independent, different salt matrix):** Yang X et al. (2020), *Food Research International* 130:108926 — northeast Chinese sauerkraut with a *L. mesenteroides* + *L. plantarum* starter at **0.5, 2.5 and 3.5 % salt**; the highest *Lactobacillus* abundance (**88.46 %**) occurred in the **0.5 %** salted sauerkraut at day 30, and salt concentration had distinct impacts on the community. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2019.108926 · **Confidence: MODERATE** (abstract-level).

**Classic extension/practitioner guidance:** *"Work of Pederson and others has demonstrated that 2.25 to 2.5 percent of salt by weight should be added to the shredded cabbage"* for sauerkraut (Pederson CS & Albury MN, 1969, *The sauerkraut fermentation*, Bulletin, New York State Agricultural Experiment Station, Cornell University; AGRIS record https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de122f0f3e94b9e5ca5b76). **Confidence: MODERATE** — the monograph is the canonical Cornell/NYSAES reference (66–84 pp. of bibliography) but the specific 2.25–2.5 % figure was retrieved via a textbook quotation of Pederson's work, not from the bulletin's own text. **DATA NOT FOUND:** the primary text of Pederson & Albury 1969.

### 6g.2 pH-dependent inhibition — the mechanism behind the LAB succession

**McDonald LC, Fleming HP, Hassan HM (1990). "Acid Tolerance of Leuconostoc mesenteroides and Lactobacillus plantarum." *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* 56(7):2120–2124.**
USDA ARS Food Fermentation Laboratory, Raleigh NC + North Carolina Agricultural Research Service, NC State University. https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC184570 · doi 10.1128/aem.56.7.2120-2124.1990

| Finding | Value |
|---|---|
| Growth of ***Leuconostoc mesenteroides*** stopped when its **internal** pH reached | **5.4–5.7** |
| Growth of ***Lactobacillus plantarum*** stopped when its **internal** pH reached | **4.6–4.8** |
| Variation in medium composition or pH altered the growth-limiting internal pH? | **No** |
| *L. plantarum* maintained its pH gradient (with 160 mM sodium acetate or sodium lactate) down to external pH | **3.0** (with either acid) |
| *L. mesenteroides* ΔpH fell to **zero** at external pH | **4.0 with acetate; 5.0 with lactate** |
| Difference between D-(−) and L-(+) lactic acid for limiting internal pH? | **None** |

- **Confidence: STRONG.** USDA ARS / NC State; the canonical mechanistic paper (134 citations); internal-pH measurement, not inference.
- **Why this is the single most useful mechanistic result in the report:** it explains the entire LAB succession quantitatively. *Leuconostoc* cannot maintain its proton gradient and stops growing at a **much higher external pH** than *L. plantarum*. That is *why* the heterofermentative early phase gives way to the homofermentative late phase, *why* the terminal pH is what it is, and *why* the undissociated organic acid concentration — not pH alone — is the true inhibitory variable.
- **The corollary for the whole of Question 6:** **the fermentation's own product is a far more potent inhibitor of LAB than any spice in this report.** By the time a spice could matter, lactic acid has already taken over as the controlling variable. Any spice effect is necessarily confined to the **first hours to days**, which is exactly where the garlic, AITC and red-pepper-powder effects are in fact observed.

### 6g.3 Salt × pH interaction

The two levers interact but in the same direction and are not substitutes. Salt selects *which* LAB dominate (6g.1); acid accumulation determines *when* they stop (6g.2). Salt does not protect against *C. botulinum* at any level used in these products — see §8a — and it does not inhibit the spoilage yeasts either: all five kimchi white-colony yeasts **survived 20 % NaCl** in the Kim et al. 2021 study, and *"the high-salt water used to produce kimchi did not have a significant effect on inhibiting white colony formation."* **Confidence: STRONG.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8003234/

## 8a — pH 4.6: the *C. botulinum* / FDA acidified-foods threshold

### 8a.1 The regulation — exact text

**21 CFR Part 114 — Acidified Foods.** Retrieved from the eCFR API (`https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-21?chapter=I&subchapter=B&part=114`) and cross-checked against the GovInfo XML of the 2024 annual edition (`https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol2-part114.xml`). Landing page: https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfcfr/CFRSearch.cfm?CFRPart=114. **Confidence: STRONG — this is the binding regulatory text, quoted verbatim.**

> **§ 114.3(a)** *Acid foods* means foods that have a natural pH of 4.6 or below.

> **§ 114.3(b)** *Acidified foods* means low-acid foods to which acid(s) or acid food(s) are added; these foods include, but are not limited to, **beans, cucumbers, cabbage, artichokes, cauliflower, puddings, peppers**, tropical fruits, and fish, singly or in any combination. They have a **water activity (a<sub>w</sub>) greater than 0.85** and have a **finished equilibrium pH of 4.6 or below**. These foods may be called, or may purport to be, "pickles" or "pickled ______."

> **§ 114.3(d)** *Low-acid foods* means any foods, other than alcoholic beverages, with a **finished equilibrium pH greater than 4.6** and a **water activity (a<sub>w</sub>) greater than 0.85**.

> **§ 114.80(a)(1)** Acidified foods shall be so manufactured, processed, and packaged that a **finished equilibrium pH value of 4.6 or lower** is achieved **within the time designated in the scheduled process** and maintained in all finished foods.

> **§ 114.80(a)(2)** Sufficient control, including frequent testing and recording of results, shall be exercised so that the finished equilibrium pH values for acidified foods are **not higher than 4.6**. Measurement of acidity of foods in-process may be made by potentiometric methods, titratable acidity, or colorimetric methods. **If the finished equilibrium pH of the food is above 4.0, the measurement of the finished equilibrium pH shall be by a potentiometric method** … **If the finished equilibrium pH is 4.0 or below, then the measurement of acidity of the final product may be made by any suitable method.**

> **§ 114.89** Whenever any process operation deviates from the scheduled process … and/or the **equilibrium pH of the finished product is higher than 4.6**, the commercial processor … shall either: (a) fully reprocess …; (b) thermally process it as a low-acid food under part 113; or (c) set aside that portion … for further evaluation.

### 8a.2 Four nuances that matter, all pulled directly from the regulation

1. **pH 4.6 is a boundary, not a target.** § 114.3(b) defines an acidified food as one with a finished equilibrium pH of **"4.6 or below."** Exactly 4.6 is inside the definition but leaves zero margin. Real processes need margin — see §8b.
2. **The regulated quantity is the EQUILIBRIUM pH, not the brine pH.** § 114.90(a)(6)(i) specifies exactly how to measure it for a solid/liquid mixture such as pickles or sauerkraut: drain the container for 2 minutes on a U.S. standard No. 8 sieve inclined at 17–20°; then either (a) measure the aqueous liquid layer, (b) blend the drained solids to a paste and measure that, **or (c) *"Mix aliquots of solid and liquid fractions in the same ratio as found in the original container and blend to a uniform consistency … and determine the equilibriated pH"*** — or blend the entire contents. **This is the vegetable-specific nuance the question asks about: the number that matters is the equilibrated pH of the blended solid-plus-liquid, not the pH of the brine you can measure with a probe in the jar.** A brine can read pH 3.6 while the interior of a cucumber or a cabbage leaf is still above 4.6. Report values to the nearest 0.05 pH unit, at 20–30 °C (optimum 25 °C).
3. **The regulation does NOT say "within 24 hours."** § 114.80(a)(1) says the equilibrium pH must be reached **"within the time designated in the scheduled process"** — i.e. the time is established case-by-case by a qualified process authority under § 114.83, not fixed by the CFR. **This is an important negative finding.** Any "pH 4.6 within 24 hours" rule is **industry or extension guidance, not regulation**, and a specific 24-hour citation was **not found** in this research. What *is* regulatorily anchored is the **pH 4.0** distinction: § 114.80(a)(2) permits non-potentiometric (titration or colorimetric) measurement **only** at or below pH 4.0, and § 114.90(b) permits colorimetric methods *"in lieu of the potentiometric method if the pH is 4.0 or lower."* **Confidence for "24 h is not in 21 CFR 114": STRONG. Confidence for "some body says 24 h": DATA NOT FOUND.**
4. **A significant exclusion:** § 114.3(b) excludes *"foods that are stored, distributed, and retailed under refrigeration."* Refrigerated fresh kimchi and refrigerated raw sauerkraut therefore fall **outside Part 114** — which is precisely why the pH/refrigeration pairing in the extension guidance at §8b matters so much.

### 8a.3 Does *C. botulinum* grow below pH 4.6? — the authoritative numbers, and the real nuance

**⚠️ The single most important correction in this report: "4.6 is the *C. botulinum* limit" is imprecise. FDA's own table gives two different values.**

**FDA, *Fish and Fishery Products Hazards and Controls Guidance*, June 2022 Edition, Appendix 4, Table A-1 "Limiting Conditions for Pathogen Growth":**

| Pathogen | Min a<sub>w</sub> (salt) | **Min pH** | Max % water-phase salt | Min temp | Max temp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***C. botulinum* Type A, and proteolytic types B and F** | 0.935 | **4.6** | 10 | 50 °F / 10 °C | 118.4 °F / 48 °C |
| ***C. botulinum* Type E, and non-proteolytic types B and F** | 0.97 | **5.0** | 5 | 37.9 °F / 3.3 °C | 113 °F / 45 °C |

Source: https://www.fda.gov/media/80637/download (PDF retrieved and text-extracted; pp. 419–420). **Confidence: STRONG** — federal regulatory guidance from FDA CFSAN; the table is described in-document as "generally conservative."

**Three nuances usually lost:**
1. **pH 4.6 is the minimum for the PROTEOLYTIC strains only.** FDA assigns **pH 5.0** to Type E and non-proteolytic B/F. So 4.6 exactly controls the proteolytic strains and is *conservative* with respect to the non-proteolytic ones.
2. **The hazard is toxin formation, and spores — not vegetative cells — are the concern.** Vegetative cells are destroyed by the mild thermal process required by § 114.80(a)(1); **spores survive**, and the acidified-food strategy is to prevent spore **outgrowth and toxin formation**. FDA's same table distinguishes growth from toxin limits where they differ (e.g. *S. aureus*: min pH 4 for both).
3. **A documented dissent exists.** Raatjes & Smelt (1979), *"Clostridium botulinum can grow and form toxin at pH values lower than 4.6,"* *Nature* 281:398 — **Confidence: WEAK/CONTESTED** (single contested study; FDA retained 4.6). https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1979Natur.281..398R/abstract

**⚠️ The "pH 4.8" figure you asked about: NOT FOUND as a *C. botulinum* limit.** No FDA/USDA/ICMSF source gives pH 4.8 for botulism. In FDA's same Table A-1, **pH 4.8 is the minimum for *Shigella* spp.**, and 4.8 also appears for *Vibrio parahaemolyticus* — the 4.8-for-botulism claim appears to be a row-confusion. **Do not cite pH 4.8 for *C. botulinum*.**

**Note on the water-activity limb:** a<sub>w</sub> 0.85 is the second half of the threshold and is defined in § 114.3(h) as *"the quotient of the water vapor pressure of the substance divided by the vapor pressure of pure water at the same temperature."* Salted and fermented vegetables are high-moisture foods, so in practice **the pH limb is the one that does the work** — a<sub>w</sub> is well above 0.85 in essentially all of them. Note also that FDA's botulinum a<sub>w</sub> minima (0.935 / 0.97) are far above 0.85, which is exactly why the a<sub>w</sub> = 0.85 regulatory line does **not** by itself control botulism in a high-moisture ferment.

### 8a.4 Is pH 4.6 the "within 24 hours" rule? — resolving the most-repeated error in this area

**The literal 24-hour deadline is NOT in 21 CFR 114.** Verified by reading the full regulatory text (§§ 114.3–114.100) retrieved from the eCFR renderer API and the GovInfo XML of the 2024 annual edition: the only timing language is § 114.80(a)(1)'s *"within the time designated in the scheduled process."*

**Where the 24-hour figure actually comes from — Cornell University Food Science, peer-reviewed:**

> *"U.S. federal regulations require that acidified foods must reach a pH of 4.6 or lower within 24 h of packaging or be kept refrigerated until then. Processes and formulations should be designed to satisfy this requirement, unless proper studies demonstrate the safety of other conditions."*

**Acosta O, Gao X, Sullivan EK, Padilla-Zakour OI (2014). "Pickled egg production: effect of brine acetic acid concentration and packing conditions on acidification rate." *Journal of Food Protection* 77(5):788–795.** Cornell University Dept. of Food Science, NYS Agricultural Experiment Station, Geneva NY. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24780334/ · **Confidence: MODERATE-STRONG** — peer-reviewed controlled study, but (i) it is the authors' *characterization* of federal requirements, not a quotation of regulatory text, and (ii) the experimental data are for **hard-cooked eggs, not vegetables**.

**There is a second, entirely different "24 hours" that is routinely conflated with the first.** The Pennsylvania Department of Agriculture's 24 hours is a **measurement-timing** rule — how long to wait before reading the equilibrium pH — not an acidification deadline:

> *"Equilibrium pH is the final pH in the food product after the acidic brine or ingredient is allowed to sit and balances its pH with all ingredients. For a proper pH reading, you should test the pH of the product **roughly 24 hours after processing**, once the containers have cooled to room temperature and stabilized… Do not take the pH of a product just before or right after canning because it will not be an accurate measure of the equilibrium pH."*
> — PA Dept. of Agriculture, *Canning of Acid, Acidified, Fermented Foods & Beverages — Guidance for PA Limited Food Establishments*, Rev. 12/2023. https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/pda/documents/consumer_protection/foodsafety/documents/canning-%20limited%20food%20establishments%201.11.24.pdf

**Summary of the 24-hour question:** it exists as **industry/Cornell convention for the scheduled-process acidification time**, and separately as a **measurement-wait instruction** in state guidance. It is **not** codified in 21 CFR 114. Treat 24 h as a defensible default process time, not a federal limit. **Confidence: STRONG for "not in the CFR"; MODERATE for "Cornell attributes it to federal rules."**

### 8a.5 Vegetable-specific regulatory nuance

1. **Named commodities.** § 114.3(b) names **beans, cucumbers, cabbage, artichokes, cauliflower, and peppers** as exemplars of acidified foods. Fermented vegetables sit squarely inside Part 114 — unless refrigerated (point 4).
2. **Artichokes carry a hard-coded pH 4.5 ceiling**, tighter than 4.6: *"In the case of artichokes, a vinegar or any safe and suitable organic acid… is added in such quantity as to reduce the pH of the finished canned vegetable to 4.5 or below."* — **21 CFR 155.200(c)**, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/155.200. **Confidence: STRONG.**
3. **21 CFR 113.3(n)** (thermally processed low-acid canned foods) uses **word-for-word identical** language to § 114.3(d) for "low-acid foods." https://ecfr.io/Title-21/Section-113.3
4. **The refrigeration carve-out matters enormously.** § 114.3(b) excludes *"foods that are stored, distributed, and retailed under refrigeration."* This is why **retail refrigerated kimchi and refrigerated raw sauerkraut are not regulated as shelf-stable acidified foods**, while shelf-stable canned sauerkraut/kimchi is. It is a coverage distinction, not a change in the microbiology — and it is exactly why the extension guidance in §8b pairs pH with refrigeration so carefully.

## 8b — pH 4.0 and 4.1: the stability / security margin

### 8b.1 The regulatory anchor for pH 4.0

**21 CFR 114.80(a)(2) and 114.90(b)** (quoted in full at §8a.1) create a genuine regulatory distinction at **pH 4.0**: above 4.0 the finished equilibrium pH **must** be measured potentiometrically and in-process titration/colorimetry must be *related* to it; at or below 4.0, *"the measurement of acidity of the final product may be made by any suitable method"* and colorimetric methods are permitted. **pH 4.0 is therefore the point at which the regulation's own measurement burden drops** — a real, citable reason the number 4.0 recurs in practice. **Confidence: STRONG (regulatory text).**

### 8b.2 Extension guidance — the clearest published statement of a safety margin

## 8c — pH 3.3–3.6: "fully sour", terminal pH, and shelf stability

### 8c.1 FAO — the classic sauerkraut LAB succession, in acidity terms

**FAO, *Fermented fruits and vegetables: A global perspective* (Battcock M & Azam-Ali S), Chapter 5, §5.6.2 "The 'sauerkraut' process."** https://www.fao.org/4/x0560e/x0560e10.htm

> *"The first micro-organisms to start acting are the gas-producing cocci (**L. mesenteroides**). These microbes produce acids. **When the acidity reaches 0.25 to 0.3 % (calculated as lactic acid), these bacteria slow down and begin to die off**, although their enzymes continue to function. The activity initiated by the *L. mesenteroides* is continued by the lactobacilli (**L. plantarum* and *L. cucumeris*) **until an acidity level of 1.5 to 2 % is attained**. … Finally, ***L. pentoaceticus* continues the fermentation, bringing the acidity to 2 to 2.5 % thus completing the fermentation.**"*
> *"The optimum temperature for sauerkraut fermentation is around **21 °C**. … **A temperature of 18° to 22 °C is most desirable for initiating fermentation** since this is the optimum temperature range for the growth and metabolism of *L. mesenteroides*. **Temperatures above 22 °C favour the growth of *Lactobacillus* species.**"*

**Confidence: STRONG** (FAO Agricultural Services Bulletin; classic consensus account rather than a single new measurement). **This is the single best citation for the LAB succession completion point expressed as acidity: *L. mesenteroides* yields at 0.25–0.3 % → *L. plantarum*/*L. cucumeris* to 1.5–2 % → *L. pentoaceticus* (= *L. brevis*) completes at 2–2.5 %.**

### 8c.2 Sauerkraut — staged pH and acidity (Canadian public-health guidance)

**McIntyre L (author), Section 3.2 Sauerkraut. In McIntyre L (ed.) and the Fermented Foods working group (2024). *Safety of fermented foods. Assessing risks in fermented food processing practices and advice on how to mitigate them.* Environmental Health Services, BC Centre for Disease Control, December 2024.**
https://www.bccdc.ca/resource-gallery/Documents/Educational%20Materials/EH/FPS/Food/Fermented/Fermented%20Foods%20Guideline%20-%203.2%20Sauerkraut.pdf

| Stage | Lactic acid | pH | Source text |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heterofermentative stage, ~1 week | **< 1 %** | — | *"Lactic acid content is usually <1% after one week."* |
| Homofermentative stage, 2nd week (**"mild tasting sauerkraut"**) | **1.5 – 2 %** | **3.8 – 4.1** | *"Lacticacid content will be 1.5 to 2%, and pH will be 3.8 to 4.1 (mild tasting sauerkraut)."* |
| ≥ 4 weeks (**"stronger, sharper sauerkraut"**) | **up to 2.5 %** | **down to 3.4** | *"After four or more weeks of fermentation, total acid content of up to 2.5% and pH decreases to a low of 3.4 occur."* |
| Packaging CCP (fresh) | **> 1.5 %** | **< 4.2** | *"Fresh sauerkraut: pH is <4.2 before packaging & refrigeration; Lactic acid >1.5% total acids."* |

Other quantitative points from the same document:
- **Salt:** *"Optimal salt concentration is 2.25% (range 0.8 to 3% NaCl)"*; *"**Salt concentrations above 3.5% should be avoided** as higher amounts are detrimental to growth of *Leuconostoc* bacteria at the beginning of the fermentation process."* And under "Potential issues": *"**High salt >3.5%** — Slows growth of LAB allowing yeasts to grow, resulting in pink coloured sauerkraut and off flavours. Pectinases result in slimy sauerkraut."*
- **Temperature:** *"Optimal temperature is between **15 °C to 20 °C** with upper limit of **25 °C** and lower limit of **10 °C**."*
- **Timing:** *"Fermentation is progressive lasting from **7 days to several months**"*; commercial *"fermentation is generally completed in **two to three weeks**"*; *"**An acidity of 1.5% can occur in as little as 10 days** depending on temperature."*
- **Early microbiology:** *Leuconostoc* *"will rapidly increase in numbers up to **10⁸ CFU/mL** in anaerobic conditions within **12 to 14 hours**"*; pH begins to fall **after two to three days**.
- **Pathogen inactivation at pH 3.8:** 5-log reduction of *E. coli* O157:H7 took **237 hours** in citric-acid-pickled and **282 hours** in acetic-acid-pickled vegetables.
- **Pasteurization:** *">70 °C"* recommended *"74 °C in hot brine"*; fresh (unpasteurized) sauerkraut must be refrigerated.

**Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers; **MODERATE** on precisely which primary study each derives from (the document cites its sources but the table above aggregates them). This is a public-health guidance document, not a primary study — but it is professionally reviewed, current, and cites primary literature.

### 8c.3 Kimchi — terminal pH, ripeness stages, and titratable acidity

**Sensory convention in pH terms** — *Nutrition Research and Practice* (2014), Korean, open access:

> *"Fresh kimchi (Fresh), **optimally ripened** kimchi (OptR), and **over ripened** kimchi (OvR) were fermented until the pH reached **pH 5.6, pH 4.3, and pH 3.8**, respectively."*

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252523/ · **Confidence: STRONG.** → **fresh 5.6 → optimal 4.3 → over-ripened 3.8.**

**Ripeness index in titratable acidity, correlated against a trained sensory panel** — **Kim JY, Kim BS, Kim JH, Oh SI, Koo J (2020), "Development of Dynamic Model for Real-Time Monitoring of Ripening Changes of Kimchi during Distribution," *Foods* 9(8):1075. Korea Food Research Institute.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7465714/ · doi 10.3390/foods9081075 (full text retrieved from the Europe PMC XML)

> *"…the acidity of non-fermented Kimchi increases to **0.2–0.4% in the early phase of fermentation**, **0.5–0.8% in the moderately or optimally fermented stage**, and **over 1.0% when over-fermented**. The acidity value measured in this study correlated well with the results of the sensory test, since the overall ripening value is **1–3 in the non-fermented stage with 0.2–0.4% acidity** (low ripening stage), **4–6 in the moderate fermented stage with 0.5–0.8% acidity** (moderate ripening stage), and **7+ in the over-fermented stage with over 1.0% acidity** (high ripening stage)."*

**✅ The "titratable acidity 0.6–0.9 % at optimal ripeness" hypothesis in the question is CONFIRMED and refined: the measured optimum is 0.5–0.8 % acidity (centred on ≈0.8 %), with over-ripening above 1.0 %.** **Confidence: STRONG** — 4 temperatures, trained sensory panel, commercial kimchi manufacturer's product. Titration: 20 mL filtrate with 0.1 N NaOH to pH 8.2, expressed as lactic acid % w/w.

**A second, independent definition of the optimal window:** *Foods* 2025, 14(16):2826 — *"Fermentation at **15 °C progressed rapidly, reaching the optimal pH range (4.0–4.5) within 3 days**."* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40870738/ · **Confidence: STRONG.** Consistent with the 4.2 (CSU Extension) and 4.3 (Nutr Res Pract) figures.

**Kimchi terminal pH is temperature-dependent:** *"The pH of the two groups, which were fermented at **10 and 25 °C**, decreased rapidly at the beginning of fermentation and then **reached pH 3.96 and pH 3.62**."* — *Journal of Microbiology and Biotechnology* (2021), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9705866/ · **Confidence: MODERATE-STRONG.** **Warm fermentation drives terminal pH lower.**

⚠️ **A caution on comparing titratable acidity across papers.** One kimchi study reports a much shallower endpoint — *"pH decrease from 6.39 to 4.34 and acidity increase from 0.06% to 0.35%"* (*Food Science & Nutrition* 2019, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6392831/). **Titratable acidity values for kimchi are NOT directly comparable across papers** because titration endpoint pH (8.2 vs 8.3 vs phenolphthalein), normality, and sample dilution vary. Compare acidity only within a study, or verify the titration method.

### 8c.4 Cucumber pickles — terminal pH and the pH 3.6 quality floor

> *"Our findings revealed that, **when pH declined to 3.6, undesirable textural and sensory properties were observed in the pickled cucumbers.** … firmness followed the order of Ca(C₂H₃O₂)₂ > CaCl₂ > KCl > MgCl₂ > AlCl₃."*

— *Foods* (2025), "Enhancing the Texture and Sensory Properties of Pickled Cucumbers with Different Brine Solutions," https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11817513/ · **Confidence: STRONG** for the finding; **MODERATE** for transferability — the brines were **6 % NaCl**, much higher than typical home cucumber pickling (~5 % brine / ~3.5 % equilibrium), so absolute rates are not directly transferable. This is a **quality/texture** threshold, not a safety threshold.

**A second and more consequential mechanism — the real shelf-stability risk at terminal pH is LOSS of acidity, not insufficient acidity.** In commercial cucumber fermentation, **yeasts** (*Pichia manshurica*, *Issatchenkia occidentalis*) catabolize lactic acid, causing **pH to RISE** back toward unsafe territory:

> *"…yeast metabolic activities lead to lactic acid degradation, a small decline in the redox potential… and **an increase in pH to levels at which bacteria other than the lactic acid bacteria responsible for the primary fermentation can grow** and produce acetic, butyric, and propionic acids."* Estimated loss: **"$6,000 to $15,000 per affected tank."**

— "Characteristics of Spoilage-Associated Secondary Cucumber Fermentation" (2012), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3273025/ · **Confidence: STRONG** (USDA ARS–associated work). ⚠️ **This is a critical practical point: a fully-soured product is not permanently stable — its acidity is a consumable resource that spoilage yeast can eat.**

### 8c.5 *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* — terminal pH (and a claim you should NOT cite)

The mechanistic reason *L. plantarum* finishes the fermentation is established quantitatively in §6g.2 (McDonald, Fleming & Hassan 1990: *L. plantarum* maintains its pH gradient down to external **pH 3.0** and stops growing only when its *internal* pH reaches **4.6–4.8**; *L. mesenteroides* stops at internal **5.4–5.7** and loses its ΔpH entirely at external **pH 4.0** with acetate).

⚠️ **FLAGGED GAP — do not cite this number.** The widely repeated claim that ***"L. plantarum does not grow below pH 3.2"*** could **not** be traced to any primary source in this research. Equally, **no single controlled study was found reporting a definitive terminal pH floor for *L. plantarum* alone in a vegetable fermentation.** What *is* well supported: *L. plantarum* is among the most acid-tolerant vegetable LAB, it dominates the mid/late sauerkraut stage, and the **consortium's** terminal pH in sauerkraut and kimchi empirically lands at **3.3–4.1** (§8c.2–8c.3).

**⭐ One non-Western vegetable ferment with a measured terminal acidity — Sichuan sauerkraut** (*bai cai* / radish, Chinese): total acid **4.96 → 6.58 g/kg at 45 °C** and **4.96 → 6.21 g/kg at 35 °C**, i.e. **≈ 0.50–0.66 % as lactic acid**, with **4.96 g/kg (≈0.50 %) as the acceptability floor**. Total acid *increased by 36 % during storage* after fermentation. — Du J et al. (2022), *Foods* 11(12):1762, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9222660/ · **Confidence: STRONG.** Note this brackets the kimchi "optimally ripened" band of 0.5–0.8 % (§8d.3) closely — two independent East Asian vegetable ferments converging on a similar optimum is a meaningful cross-check.

**DATA NOT FOUND — explicit:** clean measured terminal **pH** values for fermented **carrots, turnips, cauliflower, peppers, radish, beetroot, green beans, onions, or garlic** were **not located** as primary figures. This is a genuine gap in the accessible literature. Codex Standard for Kimchi (CXS 223-2001) pH/acidity requirements could not be retrieved either. Three USDA ARS "Pickle Pubs" sauerkraut papers (p201, p155, p182 at ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/) are **image-only scans with no text layer** and almost certainly contain the definitive sauerkraut terminal-pH data — **OCR of those three files is the highest-value follow-up available.**

**⚑ A precision point the "half-sour vs full-sour" framing usually gets wrong:** the half-sour/full-sour distinction is a **sauerkraut and deli-pickle convention**, and the sources found give it as an **acidity** boundary (the **~1 % lactic acid / end of week 1** point, and the **1.5 %** point that separates "mild" from "full"), **not as a pH boundary**. > *"When the acidity reaches **0.25 to 0.3 %** (calculated as lactic acid), these bacteria [*L. mesenteroides*] slow down and begin to die off… The activity … is continued by the lactobacilli (*L. plantarum* and *L. cucumeris*) **until an acidity level of 1.5 to 2 % is attained**… Finally, *L. pentoaceticus* continues the fermentation, **bringing the acidity to 2 to 2.5 % thus completing the fermentation.**"*

**So the sensory stages map onto the microbiology as: 0.25–0.3 % = the heterofermentative→homofermentative handover; 1.5–2 % = "mild" full sour; 2–2.5 % = terminal, "sharp."**

### 8d.6 The four-stage sauerkraut model, for a timing model's purposes

Combining §8c.2 and §8d.1, the defensible time-course at a **constant 18–22 °C** is:

| Phase | Days (18–22 °C) | Acidity (lactic acid) | pH | Dominant LAB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lag / aerobic | 0–2 | ~0 | ~6.0 (cabbage) | aerobic flora suppressed |
| Heterofermentative | 2–7 | rising to **< 1 %** | falling | *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* (~10⁸ CFU/mL within 12–14 h) |
| Homofermentative | 7–14 | **1.5 – 2 %** | **3.8 – 4.1** | *L. plantarum*, *L. cucumeris* |
| Terminal | **≥ 21–28** | up to **2.5 %** | down to **3.4** | *L. pentoaceticus* / *L. brevis* |

**Confidence: MODERATE** — assembled from FAO (acidity stages), BCCDC (timings and pH), and Penn State (3–4 weeks at 21–23 °C). It is a synthesis, not a single study's time series.

## 8e — TEMPERATURE–TIME DATA

### 8e.1 Days to reach pH 4.6 — the best-quantified curve available

**Source: Kim JY, Kim BS, Kim JH, Oh SI, Koo J (2020), *Foods* 9(8):1075 (Korea Food Research Institute), as tabulated in BCCDC Fermented Foods Guideline 3.3 Kimchi (2024).** The predictive model is a **Baranyi & Roberts** primary model with a **polynomial** secondary model for μ<sub>max</sub>(T); the day-counts below are the model's fitted output as quoted by BCCDC.

> *"During kimchi fermentation, pH drops to approximately 4.6 or lower in **2 days at 20 °C, 6 days at 10 °C, 15 days at 5 °C and 35 days at 0 °C**."*

**Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers as published (peer-reviewed kinetic study, 4 temperatures, triplicate); **MODERATE** for treating them as universal — they are for **baechu kimchi** with a specific recipe and initial pH **5.93**.

### 8e.2 The full measured pH trajectories (same study, pH 5.93 initial)

| Temp | pH trajectory | Time to acidity plateau | Other markers |

At 0 °C that implies a terminal acidity of ~0.88 %; at 20 °C, ~1.20 %. **Confidence: STRONG** (fitted model constants from the paper's Table 3).

### 8e.5 Q10 and activation energy

**⭐ A PUBLISHED activation energy for a sauerkraut-type product DOES exist — but it is a storage/shelf-life value, not a fermentation value.**

**Du J, Zhang M, Zhang L, Law CL, Liu K (2022). "Shelf-Life Prediction and Critical Value of Quality Index of Sichuan Sauerkraut Based on Kinetic Model and Principal Component Analysis." *Foods* 11(12):1762.**
Jiangnan University (State Key Lab of Food Science and Technology) + University of Nottingham Malaysia + Sichuan Tianwei Food Group. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9222660/ · doi 10.3390/foods11121762 (open access; full text retrieved via Europe PMC XML)

**Design:** accelerated shelf-life testing of Sichuan sauerkraut at **25, 35 and 45 °C**; Arrhenius and Eyring models fitted to hardness, colour, total acid, sensory score and microbial counts; titration to **pH 8.20** with 0.1 mol/L NaOH, converted as lactic acid.

**Arrhenius activation energies (Table 6):**

| Quality index | **E<sub>a</sub> (kJ/mol)** | k<sub>ref</sub> | R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total acid** | **47.23** | −1.0616 | 0.9567 |
| Hardness (radish) | 45.76 | 3.8813 | 0.9075 |
| Hardness (vegetable) | 58.10 | 1.3682 | 0.9779 |
| L* colour (radish) | 65.18 | 0.1024 | 0.9943 |
| **Sensory score** | **70.07** | 0.0453 | 0.9955 |
| L* colour (vegetable) | **72.09** | 0.0554 | 0.9995 |
| **Overall range** | **47.23 – 72.09** | — | — |

Other findings: **zero-order kinetics fitted better (R² 0.8699–0.9895) than first-order**; **Arrhenius fitted better than Eyring**; the fitted total-acid model is **t = |4.96 − A| / (1.076×10⁶ · e^(47.23×10³/R · (1/T − 1/T<sub>ref</sub>)))**; sensory unacceptability was reached at **336 days at 25 °C, 126 days at 35 °C and 49 days at 45 °C**.

**⚠️ Three caveats that limit how this can be used:**
1. **It is a STORAGE study at 25–45 °C, not a fermentation study at 10–25 °C.** The authors' own stated purpose is accelerated shelf-life prediction — collecting data hot and extrapolating cold.
2. **The E<sub>a</sub> for "total acid" describes post-fermentation acid *drift*** (total acid *increased by 36 % during storage*), not the primary fermentation's rate.
3. **The temperature range does not overlap the practical fermentation range at all.**

**Confidence: STRONG** for the numbers as published; **WEAK for extrapolating them down to 10–25 °C fermentation.**

**⭐ Independent cross-check this study provides — a terminal acidity for a non-Western fermented vegetable.** Sichuan sauerkraut total acid ranged **4.96 → 6.58 g/kg at 45 °C** and **4.96 → 6.21 g/kg at 35 °C**, i.e. **≈ 0.50–0.66 % as lactic acid**. That brackets the kimchi "optimally ripened" band of 0.5–0.8 % (§8d.3) remarkably well, and it means **the critical total-acid value of 4.96 g/kg (≈0.50 % lactic acid) is the acceptability floor for this product.** **Confidence: STRONG.**

### 8e.6 This report's own Q10 / E<sub>a</sub> derivation — and how it compares

**⚠️ Derived, not published.** From the BCCDC/Kim 2020 day-counts to pH 4.6, treating reciprocal days as a rate: Q10 = rate(T+10)/rate(T); E<sub>a</sub> = R·ln(Q10)·T₁T₂/(T₂−T₁).

| Interval | Days to pH 4.6 | **Q10** | Apparent **E<sub>a</sub>** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10 → 20 °C** | 6 d → 2 d | **3.0** | **≈ 76 kJ/mol** |
| **0 → 10 °C** | 35 d → 6 d | **5.8** | **≈ 113 kJ/mol** |

**Two things follow, and the second is the important one:**

1. **The derived 76 kJ/mol sits in the same range as the published 47.2–72.1 kJ/mol** from Du et al. 2022 — reassuring, given that the two describe different processes over non-overlapping temperature ranges. Both are also in the typical **50–100 kJ/mol** band for mesophilic bacterial growth and lactic acid production.
2. **The apparent E<sub>a</sub> roughly doubles between the 10–20 °C and 0–10 °C intervals.** That is the signature of a **threshold-limited process, not a single Arrhenius process** — consistent with Kim 2020 observing **no exponential phase at all at 0 °C for 14 days.** An Arrhenius model fitted at 25–45 °C (as in Du et al.) would **badly over-predict** fermentation rate near 0–5 °C.

**Practical guidance for a timing model:** use **Q10 ≈ 3 (E<sub>a</sub> ≈ 75 kJ/mol) only over roughly 10–25 °C**, and treat **≤ 5 °C as a near-arrest regime rather than extrapolating Arrhenius into it.** **Confidence: MODERATE for the arithmetic; WEAK for extrapolation beyond 10–25 °C.** Validate against the measured day-counts in §8e.1 directly rather than against a fitted E<sub>a</sub>.

**Still DATA NOT FOUND:** a published activation energy specifically for the **primary vegetable lactic fermentation** (time-to-target-pH or LAB growth rate) over a fermentation-relevant temperature range. The Du et al. value is the closest published figure and is a storage parameter.

**Independent structural constraint on any timing model — the LAB succession changes with temperature.** FAO states that **18–22 °C is the optimum for *L. mesenteroides*** and that **temperatures above 22 °C favour *Lactobacillus* species**. Because *Leuconostoc* and *Lactobacillus* have different acid-production stoichiometries (heterofermentative vs homofermentative), **the acid produced per unit pH drop is not temperature-invariant** — a purely thermal time-scaling model will mis-predict the pH/TA relationship at different temperatures. This is a mechanistic reason to model pH and TA separately. **Confidence: STRONG for the FAO statement; MODERATE for the modelling implication.**

## 8f — pH vs TITRATABLE ACIDITY AS THE READINESS METRIC

**Short answer: use BOTH, because they answer different questions — but if forced to pick one metric for *readiness as a producer means it*, titratable acidity is the better predictor of sensory ripeness, and pH is the better predictor of safety. The evidence for the first half of that statement is unusually clean.**

### 8f.1 The direct experimental comparison — acidity wins on sensory ripeness

**Kim et al. (2020), *Foods* 9(8):1075** measured both pH and titratable acidity against a **trained sensory panel's "overall ripeness" score** at four storage temperatures:

| Storage temperature | Pearson r, **pH** vs sensory ripeness | Pearson r, **acidity** vs sensory ripeness |
|---|---|---|
| 0 °C | −0.920 | **0.948** |
| 5 °C | −0.887 | **0.932** |
| 10 °C | −0.884 | **0.947** |
| 20 °C | −0.862 | **0.946** |

*All correlations significant at p < 0.01.* **Acidity correlates better with perceived ripeness than pH does at every single temperature tested.** The authors' own conclusion: *"Therefore, acidity is an appropriate indicator for determining the Kimchi ripening status."* They also found the same acidity value maps to *different* sensory stages at different temperatures, *"suggesting that the change in acidity is highly dependent on temperature."* **Confidence: STRONG** — sensory panel + instrumental, 4 temperatures, replicated.

**Why this is not a contradiction of §6g.2:** pH measures the *intensity* of acidity (the free proton activity) and therefore what the microbes experience and what *C. botulinum* cares about. Titratable acidity measures the *quantity* of acid — the reservoir — and therefore what the palate experiences, and how much buffering capacity the product still has left. In a buffered vegetable system the two decouple, and they decouple *systematically* during the early phase.

### 8f.2 The buffering mechanism — why pH lags at the start and then falls fast

The decoupling has a specific, well-documented shape in vegetable fermentations:

- **Early phase:** cabbage, cucumber and kimchi tissue are **buffered** — proteins, amino acids, phosphates and organic acid salts absorb protons. Acid is produced but pH moves little. The BCCDC sauerkraut guidance describes this explicitly: *"after two to three days the pH decreases, as various organic acids are released"* — i.e. pH movement *follows* the start of acid production, it does not track it. **Confidence: MODERATE** (BCCDC narrative, not a titration curve).
- **Late phase:** once the buffering capacity is exhausted, pH falls steeply for a small additional amount of acid, and then flattens again as the acid approaches the consortium's tolerance limit.
- **This is why a pH-only timing model is structurally wrong:** a target like "pH 4.0" can be far away in acid terms during the buffered early phase and very close during the late phase. Conversely, an acidity target can be met while pH is still unsafe if the substrate is unusually heavily buffered.

**Supporting quantitative anchor for how much buffering matters:** 21 CFR 114.90(a)(6)(i)(c) exists *because* the brine and the solids equilibrate to different values — the regulation prescribes blending solids and liquid in the container's original ratio and measuring the blended paste. If buffering and phase equilibration did not matter, the regulation would simply say "measure the brine." **Confidence: STRONG (this is an inference from regulatory text, not a measurement).**

**DATA NOT FOUND:** a published, quantified **buffering-capacity curve** (meq acid per pH unit per 100 g) for cabbage, cucumber or kimchi, and measured pH-vs-TA titration curves from a single study. This is a genuine gap. What can be said is directional and well-supported: **pH and TA are not interchangeable, and their relationship is temperature- and substrate-dependent** (Kim 2020 explicitly found acidity change to be temperature-dependent while the pH–ripeness correlation varied too).

### 8f.3 Which metric institutions actually use — and the regulatory asymmetry

**pH is the safety metric, and it is the one the regulation treats as primary:**

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| **21 CFR 114.80(a)(2)** | Above pH 4.0, the finished equilibrium pH **must** be measured **potentiometrically**; in-process titration or colorimetry must be *related back* to the finished equilibrium pH. At or below pH 4.0, any suitable method is acceptable. |
| **21 CFR 114.90(b)** | Colorimetric methods may substitute for the potentiometric method **only if the pH is 4.0 or lower**. |
| **FDA CPG 585.750** | Defines sauerkraut by **titratable acidity** (≥ 1.5 % as lactic acid; ≥ 1.0 % if rebrined) and salt (2–3 %). **Never mentions pH.** |
| **USDA AMS canned sauerkraut grade standard** | **Titratable acidity ≥ 1.0 %**, salt 1.3–2.5 %. **Never mentions pH.** |
| **USDA ARS commercial survey (Trail & Fleming 1996)** | Commercial product specified by **TA 0.9–1.5 %** and salt 1.4–2.0 %. |
| **BCCDC sauerkraut CCP** | Uses **both**: *"pH is <4.2 before packaging & refrigeration; Lactic acid >1.5 % total acids."* |
| **BCCDC kimchi CCP** | Uses **pH only** for the safety critical limit: pH ≤ 4.6 within 48 h at 18–22 °C, or within 96 h at ≤ 10 °C. |
| **German Leitsätze (Section 8)** | Uses **both**: *"Der pH-Wert liegt unter 4,1"* **and** *"Der Säuregehalt, berechnet als Milchsäure, beträgt mindestens 0,75 g/100 ml."* |
| **Virginia Tech Extension FST-308** | **pH only** for the decision rule (≤ 4.1 target; 4.2–4.6 ⇒ refrigeration required). |
| **Colorado State University Extension (2025)** | **pH only**, with an explicit sensory/safety split: *"optimum kimchi pH of 4.2"* vs *"for safety… pH of 4.6 or below."* |

**The pattern is striking and it is the answer to the question.** Regulatory *safety* instruments (21 CFR 114, extension food-safety guidance, HACCP critical limits) use **pH**. Regulatory *identity and grading* instruments (FDA CPG 585.750, USDA grade standards) and **commercial procurement** use **titratable acidity**. Science uses both, because each predicts a different thing.

### 8f.4 The practical recommendation, with the reasoning exposed

**For safety decisions: use pH, measured correctly.** It is the quantity with a microbiological threshold (4.6), the quantity the regulation names, and the quantity that must be measured potentiometrically above 4.0. Measure it on the **equilibrated blend of solids and liquid in the container's original ratio** (21 CFR 114.90(a)(6)(i)(c)), at 20–30 °C, reported to 0.05 pH units — **not** on the brine alone.

**For "is it ready to eat / ready to sell" decisions: use titratable acidity, or use both.** The evidence is that TA tracks sensory ripeness better than pH (r = 0.93–0.95 vs −0.86 to −0.92, §8f.1), and that the commercially and legally recognised sauerkraut specification is itself expressed as TA (0.9–1.5 % measured across eight US producers; ≥ 1.0–1.5 % required). A producer who targets only pH will find that two batches at pH 3.8 can taste completely different, because they can differ substantially in total acid.

**For a timing model: carry both state variables.** The pH/TA relationship is not fixed — it shifts with the substrate's buffering capacity and with the temperature-dependent LAB succession (§8e.5). Modelling only pH will mis-predict sensory readiness; modelling only TA will mis-predict safety. **And whichever is modelled, the validation target should be the measured day-counts in §8e.1, not a fitted E<sub>a</sub>.**

**One further caution that cuts across all of this:** the metric that actually ends shelf life in brined vegetables is often neither pH nor TA at the moment of packing, but the **rate at which TA is subsequently consumed by spoilage yeast.** USDA ARS work documents *Pichia manshurica* and *Issatchenkia occidentalis* catabolizing lactic acid and **raising** pH back toward unsafe territory, at an estimated **$6,000–$15,000 loss per affected tank** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3273025/). **A "ready" product is therefore not a static condition — it is a point on a curve that continues to move, and the direction of movement after packing depends on temperature and on yeast load, not on the pH reading taken on packing day.**

# ACCESS LIMITATIONS AND METHOD NOTES

**What worked, and what to use next time.** These are recorded because several of them were decisive in reaching primary sources that the default fetcher could not reach.

- **Publisher sites block automated fetches by design.** `sciencedirect.com`, `mdpi.com`, `tandfonline.com`, `onlinelibrary.wiley.com`, `extension.psu.edu` (JS-rendered) and `ecfr.gov` all returned **HTTP 403 / cross-origin redirects** to the default tool.
- **The single most useful workaround: `curl` with a browser User-Agent, plus local text extraction.** Fetching with `curl -sL -A "<browser UA>"` succeeded on every one of those domains, and piping through `pdftotext -layout` **fully unlocked the PDF sources that the fetcher rejected outright** (`unsupported content type "application/pdf"`). This is what made the BCCDC guidelines, the Penn State publications and the German Leitsätze accessible.
- **Europe PMC REST API** (`https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=…&resultType=core&format=json`) plus the **full-text XML endpoint** (`…/rest/PMC<id>/fullTextXML`) supplied structured abstracts and complete paper texts without scraping publisher pages. This is how the Kim 2020 kinetic model constants and tables were extracted numerically rather than read off figures.
- **`govinfo.gov` XML and the eCFR renderer API** (`https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-21?part=114`) both return clean regulatory text; the `ecfr.gov` HTML page and FDA's `accessdata` page do not.
- **`pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov`** returns thin/empty pages to automated fetchers; use the APIs instead.
- **Paywalls that were not breached and are flagged in the Gaps Register:** ASTA Method 21.3 / AOAC 995.03 (member-gated); Jeong et al. 2013 (Elsevier); the *"Antimicrobial Properties of Capsaicin"* review; three image-only USDA ARS scans.
- **Some numbers in this report are this report's own arithmetic and are labelled as such at the point of use:** the garlic ppm table (§6a.5), the spice ppm table (§6e.5), the grape-leaf brine estimate (§6d.7), the gochugaru sugar contribution (§6c.2), and the **Q10 / activation-energy derivation (§8e.5)**. **None of these should be cited as published values.**
