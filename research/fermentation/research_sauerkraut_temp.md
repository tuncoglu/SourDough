# Temperature vs. Speed of Sauerkraut Lactic Acid Fermentation
## Quantitative, cited findings from retrieved primary sources

**Compiled:** research pass over primary literature and extension sources.
**Rule applied:** every number below comes from a source whose text I actually retrieved and read.
Paywalled/abstract-only items are explicitly flagged. Derived values (my arithmetic on someone
else's published data) are labelled **[DERIVED — not reported by the authors]**.
Confidence tags: **strong** = full text retrieved, numbers read directly in the primary source;
**moderate** = retrieved but from a summary/abstract/preprint-adjacent page, or units/context need care;
**weak** = secondary summary only, or the number is attenuated by confounds.

## 0. Headline answer

The single cleanest quantitative dataset in the entire literature — and it directly answers
"time to reach a given titratable acidity at each temperature" — is **Parmele, Fred, Peterson,
McConkie & Vaughn (1927)**, the Wisconsin/ American Can Co. study that Pederson cites as the
origin of the field's temperature doctrine:

| Initial vat temperature | Time to reach **0.5 % titratable acidity** (as lactic) | Relative rate (1/time) |
|---|---|---|
| 44 °F (6.7 °C) | **11 days** | 1.0× |
| 53 °F (11.7 °C) | **6 days** | 1.8× |
| 65 °F (18.3 °C) | **3 days** | 3.7× |
| 76 °F (24.4 °C) | **24 hours (1 day)** | 11.0× |

Source: Parmele, H.B., Fred, E.B., Peterson, W.H., McConkie, J.E., Vaughn, W.E. (1927),
*Journal of Agricultural Research* **35**(11): 1021–1038, at p. 1025. **Confidence: strong.**
Full text: <https://archive.org/download/sim_journal-of-agricultural-research_1927-12-01_35_11/sim_journal-of-agricultural-research_1927-12-01_35_11_djvu.txt>
(also PDF: <https://archive.org/download/sim_journal-of-agricultural-research_1927-12-01_35_11/sim_journal-of-agricultural-research_1927-12-01_35_11.pdf>)

Verbatim: *"It can be seen that it was necessary for fermentation to go on for 11 days in the vat
whose initial temperature was 44° F. before an acidity of 0.5 per cent was reached. This period
was shortened to six days in the vat whose initial temperature was 53° F., to 3 days in the 65° vat,
and to 24 hours in the 76° vat."*

**Important caveat on this table:** these are **initial** shredded-cabbage/vat temperatures, not
held-constant temperatures. The vats self-heated 3–5 °F during the first 8 days (Marten et al. 1929),
and the 44 °F and 76 °F extremes are single vats. The 11 d → 1 d spread is therefore an upper bound
on the true isothermal effect.

### 1.2 Bulletin 824, Table 1 — the cleanest *isothermal* multi-temperature dataset

This is the single best controlled-temperature rate table I found anywhere in the sauerkraut
literature. It is expressed as **percentage of each species' maximum acidity** produced at 1, 2 and
10 days, in broth, at four held temperatures:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| *L. mesenteroides* | 10 °C (50 °F) | 3 | 25 | 62 |
| *L. brevis* | 10 °C (50 °F) | 0 | 0 | 10 |
| *L. plantarum* | 10 °C (50 °F) | 0 | 0 | 18 |
| *L. mesenteroides* | 15 °C (59 °F) | 19 | 47 | 90 |
| *L. brevis* | 15 °C (59 °F) | 0 | 0 | 48 |
| *L. plantarum* | 15 °C (59 °F) | 0 | 0 | 55 |
| *L. mesenteroides* | 20 °C (68 °F) | 50 | 84 | 100 |
| *L. brevis* | 20 °C (68 °F) | 8 | 18 | 74 |
| *L. plantarum* | 20 °C (68 °F) | 12 | 31 | 80 |
| *L. mesenteroides* | 25 °C (77 °F) | 77 | 94 | 100 |
| *L. brevis* | 25 °C (77 °F) | 15 | 33 | 91 |
| *L. plantarum* | 25 °C (77 °F) | 34 | 54 | 92 |

**Confidence: strong** (table read directly from retrieved full text).
This table also documents the *microbiological reason* temperature changes the speed: at 10 °C only
the heterofermenter *L. mesenteroides* moves at all (3 % of max at day 1) while both lactobacilli are
at 0 %; by 25 °C *L. plantarum* is already at 34 % at day 1.

### 1.3 Bulletin 614 (1932) — 125 commercial vats, atmospheric temperature vs curing time

- **Citation:** Pederson, C.S. (1932). *The Relation Between Temperature and the Rate of Fermentation
  of Commercial Sauerkraut.* NY State Agric. Exp. Stn., Geneva, Bulletin **614**, September 1932.
- **Handle:** <https://hdl.handle.net/1813/4545> · **Item:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/items/5f8fe5f2-d123-4065-b80c-ca217a5a4cc2>
- **Full text:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/b204a0db-ec43-45b0-bdd4-405c0f08267d/content>
- **Confidence: strong.**

Key retrieved numbers (days required to reach **1.60 % acid as lactic**, the plant's target):

- 1929 season: **14 to 80 days** across 125 vats. Seven tanks filled Oct 4–11 took **15 to 80 days**.
  Tanks 14, 15, 17, 18: **48–73 days**; tank 16 (same period): **15 days**.
- 1930 season: vats packed in the warm period **15–31 days**; two vats packed during a cold snap
  **47 and 65 days**; after the Oct 19 temperature drop, maximum curing time **65 days**.
- 1931 season: kraut packed after Oct 30 required **36 to 50 days** — the slowest of that season.
- Correlation: peaks in the daily air-temperature curve coincided with the fastest cures
  (**14–24 days**); the slowest vats (up to 80 days) coincided with low-temperature periods.
- Verbatim conclusion: *"the time required for complete fermentation of the kraut increases"* as
  atmospheric packing temperature falls; the temperature of the day *before* vat filling correlated
  better than the day of filling.

Also in Bulletin 614 (useful context, verbatim): *"LeFevre states that kraut can be fermented in
8 days at 80 °C"* — this is an **OCR/typographical error for 80 °F**, confirmed by Marten et al. (1929),
who write that *"LeFevre reports that sauerkraut can be made in from six to eight days by employing a
temperature of 80°."* Do not cite Bulletin 614's "80 °C".

### 1.4 Bulletin 595 (1931) — Pederson's early statements of days-vs-temperature

- **Citation:** Pederson, C.S. (1931). *Sauerkraut.* NY State Agric. Exp. Stn., Geneva, Bulletin **595**, July 1931. 21 pp.
- **Handle:** <https://hdl.handle.net/1813/4572> · **Item:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/items/6286a450-5e28-47e5-b75c-8732f23821fb>
- **Full text:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a7acfeae-7787-47bc-a981-0f2816e64866/content>
- **Confidence: strong.**

Verbatim numbers:

- *"At a temperature of 70° to 75°F fermentation is complete and the kraut ready for use in two weeks
  to a month."* → **14–30 days at 21–24 °C**
- *"At temperatures below 70°F it usually takes a month or more for complete curing."* → **>30 days below 21 °C**
- *"when kraut is packed at too low a temperature (30° to 60°F), fermentation is retarded too much.
  In such cases the Leuconostoc grow, producing about 1 per cent of acid, but fermentation ceases at
  this stage. A large number of krauts have been tested four to five months after packing that were
  not fermented completely and the outside temperature of spring and summer was necessary to warm
  them enough to complete fermentation."* → **≈1 % acid ceiling; 4–5 months still incomplete at −1 to 16 °C**
- *"The temperature of 65° to 70°F is easily attained and is probably the most desirable from the
  standpoint of quality and speed of fermentation."* → **18–21 °C recommended**

### 1.5 Bulletin 744 (1950) — same authors, same method, on cucumbers (vegetable-LAB analogue)

Useful because cucumbers were run at **five** controlled temperatures, unlike sauerkraut.

- **Citation:** Pederson, C.S. & Albury, M.N. (1950). *Effect of Temperature Upon Bacteriological and
  Chemical Changes in Fermenting Cucumbers.* NY State Agric. Exp. Stn., Geneva, Bulletin **744**, August 1950.
- **Handle:** <https://hdl.handle.net/1813/4307> · **Item:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/items/d88c31d7-1ec0-474f-adf4-b0563ecb61a2>
- **Full text:** <https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/362f01bc-60c3-4dcb-9776-3ddae908c710/content>
- **Confidence: strong** for the summary statements below; **weak** for exact per-day acid values, because
  Tables 1–3 in the scanned bulletin are column-mangled in OCR.

Temperatures used: **45, 65, 75, 86 and 97 °F (≈7, 18, 24, 30, 36 °C)**; salt 2.5, 5 and 7.5 %.

Verbatim findings:
- *"The initial rate of fermentation was greatest at 97°F, the highest temperature studied, but the
  extent of fermentation was usually greater at 75° and 86°F."*
- *"After 21 days in brines held at 75°F or above and after 54 days in brine held at 65° or below, a
  marked change in acid was observed in only a few cases."*
- *"fermentations at 50°F are retarded to such a degree that even after 5 months normal acidity is not
  attained."*
- *"In two cases the acidity rose to above 1.0 per cent in 13 days"* (high-temperature lots).
- *"For the salt concentrations used, temperature was the more important factor in controlling rate of
  fermentation."*
- Species shift with temperature: at 45–50 °F, *L. mesenteroides* and low-acid *L. plantarum* strains
  prevailed; *S. faecalis*, *Pediococcus cerevisiae*, *L. brevis* and typical *L. plantarum* were more
  active at 65 °F and above.

## 2. The Wisconsin / American Can Co. work — Parmele 1927 and Marten 1929

These are the papers Pederson repeatedly cites as the empirical basis for "65 °F is best".
Both are **public-domain US government-printed journals** and are freely available in full.

### 2.1 Parmele, Fred, Peterson, McConkie & Vaughn (1927)

- **Citation:** Parmele, H.B., Fred, E.B., Peterson, W.H., McConkie, J.E. & Vaughn, W.E. (1927).
  "Relation of temperature to rate and type of fermentation and to quality of commercial sauerkraut."
  *Journal of Agricultural Research* **35**(11): 1021–1038.
- **URL:** <https://archive.org/details/sim_journal-of-agricultural-research_1927-12-01_35_11>
- **Confidence: strong.**

Design: 8 vats in a commercial plant near Milwaukee, 8 tons each, single field/variety of cabbage,
initial temperatures engineered to ~44, 50, 52, 53, 65, 74 and 76 °F.

**Quantitative results retrieved:**
- **The 0.5 %-acid time table given in §0 above (11 / 6 / 3 / 1 days at 44 / 53 / 65 / 76 °F).**
  This is the paper's headline rate result.
- *"When the temperature of the shredded cabbage is between 65° and 75° the acidity increases rapidly
  during the first part of the fermentation, after which it increases slowly. When the temperature is
  below 65° the rise in acidity is more gradual and uniform throughout the entire process."*
- pH: the paper contains **Table 4 (Changes in pH values)** and concludes *"Titratable acidity is a much
  better measure of the degree of fermentation than are pH determinations."* The pH table columns are
  OCR-mangled in both the djvu text and the PDF text layer, so **I could not reliably extract a
  pH-vs-time-vs-temperature grid from this paper.** Stated plainly: **no reliable pH grid retrieved.**
- Quality: *"The best sauerkraut was obtained from the vats at the intermediate temperatures; at higher
  temperatures the product was more or less pink and of poor quality … Presumably the fermentation at
  these temperatures went on too rapidly, affording conditions favorable for the growth of pink yeasts."*
- Summary: *"Sauerkraut of the best quality was produced when the maximum temperature of fermentation
  was about 65° F."*
- Also documented: heat is generated during fermentation (self-heating).

## 3. USDA ARS — Fleming, Breidt, Kyung, McFeeters: what exists and what does **not**

I pulled the ARS Fermented & Acidified Vegetables Unit's complete numbered publication list
(**462 entries**) and filtered every sauerkraut/cabbage item.

- **Source:** USDA ARS, Food Science and Market Quality and Handling Research Unit, Raleigh NC —
  *Fermented & Acidified Vegetables Bibliography*:
  <https://www.ars.usda.gov/southeast-area/raleigh-nc/fsmqhru/docs/fermented-acidified-vegetables-bibliography/>
- **Confidence: strong** (list retrieved and parsed).

### 3.1 Explicit negative finding (important)

**There is no dedicated ARS/Fleming/Breidt publication on the effect of temperature on sauerkraut
fermentation rate.** The ARS sauerkraut programme (Fleming, Breidt, Kyung, McFeeters, Johanningsmeier,
Plengvidhya, Barrangou, Yoon, Lu) published on: starter cultures and nisin, bacteriophage ecology,
*Leuconostoc fallax*, DNA fingerprinting of the microbiota, malolactic activity, salt reduction,
cabbage antimicrobial sulfur compounds, and chemical/sensory characterisation. **None of the 24
sauerkraut entries in the 462-item list is a temperature-rate study.** If the brief's expectation was
that ARS ran the 7.5/18/32 °C work, that expectation is wrong — that work is **Pederson & Albury at
Cornell/Geneva**, not ARS.

### 3.2 ARS work that *does* contain temperature-dependence modelling (not sauerkraut)

- **Dougherty, D.P., Breidt, F. Jr., McFeeters, R.F. & Lubkin, S.R. (2002).** "Energy-based dynamic
  model for variable temperature batch fermentation by *Lactococcus lactis*." *Appl. Environ.
  Microbiol.* **68**(5): 2468–2478. <https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.68.5.2468-2478.2002>
  Full text retrieved. **Confidence: strong** that the paper contains **no Arrhenius activation energy
  and no Q10** — I searched the full text for "Arrhenius", "activation energy", "Q10", "kJ/mol",
  "kcal/mol" and found none. Growth was measured at **10, 20 and 30 °C** in cucumber juice and modelled
  with a dynamic-energy-budget (ATP quota) formulation, with a fitted *energy cost for transient
  temperature adjustment* of 1.60 × 10⁻³ °C⁻¹. This is a mechanistic alternative to Arrhenius, not a
  source of Ea.
- **Passos, F.V., Fleming, H.P., Ollis, D.F., Felder, R.M. & McFeeters, R.F. (1994).** "Kinetics and
  modeling of lactic acid production by *Lactobacillus plantarum*." *Appl. Environ. Microbiol.*
  **60**(7): 2627–2636. Abstract retrieved via Europe PMC. **The abstract reports no temperature/Ea
  terms** (growth-associated coefficient 51.9 ± 4.2 mmol/g cells; non-growth-associated 7.2 ± 0.9
  mmol/g cells·h; NaCl death-rate equation). PDF is a scan without a text layer, so the
  temperature-dependence section, if any, was not retrievable.

**Bottom line for item 2 of the brief:** the "Fleming strain" work (FS-3 etc.) is real and extensive,
but it is a *starter-culture / microbial-ecology* programme, not a temperature-kinetics programme.
The "Sodium chloride, temperature, and pH effects on sauerkraut fermentation" title in the brief does
**not** correspond to any paper I could locate in the ARS list, Crossref, or Europe PMC. I could not
find such a paper. Treat that citation as unverified.

### 4.2 Derived values from retrieved primary data **[DERIVED — not reported by the authors]**

I computed these from the Parmele et al. (1927) 0.5 %-acid times above, taking rate ∝ 1/time.
**These are my calculations, not published values.** Treat as indicative only.

- Q10 (6.7 → 11.7 °C): **3.4**
- Q10 (11.7 → 18.3 °C): **2.8**
- Q10 (18.3 → 24.4 °C): **6.0**
- Q10 overall 6.7 → 24.4 °C: **3.9**
- Arrhenius regression of ln(rate) vs 1/T across all four vats:
  **Ea ≈ 91 kJ/mol, R² = 0.981** (slope −10,940 K)

**Caveats that matter:** (a) only four points, one vat each; (b) "initial temperature," not isothermal
— vats self-heated 3–5 °F, which flattens the apparent slope at the warm end; (c) at 24.4 °C the
"1 day" figure is a coarse 24-hour observation, so the 18.3→24.4 °C Q10 of 6.0 is the least reliable.

A **second derivation** from Pederson & Albury's Bulletin 824 Table 1 (*L. mesenteroides*, % of max
acidity at 1 day at 10/15/20/25 °C = 3/19/50/77) gives Q10 values of **40** (10→15 °C), **6.9**
(15→20 °C) and **2.4** (20→25 °C). The very large low-temperature Q10 reflects the near-total
growth arrest of all species at 10 °C, and the numbers are strongly influenced by the normalisation
to "percentage of maximum acidity". **I would not quote these without the caveat.** They do, however,
agree qualitatively with the classic literature: **the temperature sensitivity is steepest below
~15 °C**, which is exactly why 7.5 °C fermentation effectively stalls.

### 4.3 Honest statement of the gap

**I could not find any published Q10 or Arrhenius Ea specifically for sauerkraut (or cabbage-brine)
fermentation rate**, in Europe PMC (open-access full text), Crossref, web search, or the ARS
bibliography. Searches run included "sauerkraut activation energy", "sauerkraut Q10", "temperature
coefficient lactic fermentation vegetables", and kimchi/vegetable-LAB kinetic-model queries. What
exists is (i) storage-decay Ea (above), and (ii) mechanistic temperature models without Ea
(Dougherty et al. 2002). Stating this plainly rather than substituting a number from another
fermentation is the correct answer.

## 7. Where popular home-fermentation advice conflicts with the measured data

Popular advice (and two of the three Extension pages above) says **"ferment at 70–75 °F (21–24 °C)
for 3–4 weeks."** The measured literature disagrees on both numbers:

1. **The temperature recommendation is too warm for best quality.** Every primary source that
   measured quality says the optimum is **60–65 °F (15.6–18.3 °C)**, not 70–75 °F:
   - Parmele et al. 1927: *"Sauerkraut of the best quality was produced when the maximum temperature
     of fermentation was about 65° F."*
   - Marten et al. 1929: *"the most favorable temperature for fermentation was between 60° and 65° F"*,
     and 70 °F kraut was scored only *"Fair"* (jars) or *"Poor"* (barrels) while 55–60 °F kraut was
     *"Good"* in every instance.
   - Pederson 1931 (Bulletin 595): *"The temperature of 65° to 70°F … is probably the most desirable"*;
     Pederson & Albury 1969: kraut fermented at 55–65 °F *"will be superior in quality to kraut
     fermented at the higher temperatures of 75°F and above."*
   - Pederson & Albury 1969: at 32 °C the kraut is fast but *"the flavor of the kraut will be inferior;
     it may be likened to acidified cabbage"*, and at ≥75 °F the product darkens readily and is prone
     to pink yeast spoilage.
   - **The 70–75 °F advice is essentially the ~80 °F "heat the vat room" doctrine of Round/LeFevre
     (1910s–20s), which Pederson explicitly says was based on flawed early USDA bacteriology and
     *"should be changed to agree with later observations."*** The modern Extension pages have
     inherited the warm end of that range.

2. **"3–4 weeks" is only right at ~21 °C, and it is a range, not a constant.** Measured curing times
   to a defined endpoint:
   - 24.4 °C: 1 day to 0.5 % acid; ~8–10 days to 1.0–1.5 %; ~1 month complete.
   - 18.3 °C: 3 days to 0.5 % acid; complete in ~1 month.
   - 11.7 °C: 6 days to 0.5 % acid.
   - **6.7 °C: 11 days to 0.5 % acid; 30 days to only 0.8–0.9 %; not complete for 6 months or more.**
   - Commercial vats (Bulletin 614) ranged from **14 to 80 days** to reach 1.6 % purely on weather.
   - So at cellar temperature (say 12–15 °C) "3–4 weeks" is **too short** to reach a 1.5–2 % kraut;
     at 21 °C it is about right; and below ~10 °C the fermentation will effectively stall near 1 %
     acid for months, which home advice almost never mentions.

3. **"The warmer the room the faster" is true but the advice omits the quality cliff.** Both the
   1927 and 1929 papers tie rapid warm fermentation directly to **soft, pink, poor-quality kraut and
   pink-yeast growth**, and Pederson & Albury tie 32 °C fermentation to an acidified-cabbage flavour.
   Speed and quality trade off in opposite directions across the whole range studied.

4. **The "pH 4.6 in X days" safety framing is absent from the classic literature** and only appears
   in modern work. At 15 °C the measured time to pH 4.6 is ≈ 4–5 days (Tlais et al. 2022), and pH 3.5
   takes ≈ a month — so the *acid-production* endpoint that the classic authors cared about
   (1.5–1.6 % titratable acidity) and the *pH* endpoint regulators care about are reached on very
   different timescales. Pederson and Parmele both warned explicitly that pH is a poor measure of
   degree of fermentation.
