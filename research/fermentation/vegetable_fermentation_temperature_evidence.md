# How fermentation TEMPERATURE changes the CHARACTER of vegetable lactic acid fermentations

**Scope:** sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles/cucumbers, pepper mash.
**Question:** what does temperature change *qualitatively* (organism, end-products, texture, colour, aroma, failure modes) — not just how fast it goes.

**Method / honesty statement.** Every source below was actually retrieved during this research (via `web_search`, `web_fetch`, publisher/PMC/FAO PDFs, Crossref, Semantic Scholar, PubMed E-utilities, or — where noted — a delegated sub-researcher). Nothing is cited from memory. Where a claim could not be verified against a retrieved document it is marked **NOT VERIFIED**. Two items were retrieved by delegated sub-researchers and are labelled as such; I did not re-fetch those primaries myself.

**Confidence key**
- **strong** — primary peer-reviewed paper or official standard/monograph retrieved (text or PDF read directly), and the specific claim appears in it.
- **moderate** — retrieved but abstract-only, a review, a conference abstract, or a secondary/tertiary teaching text quoting a primary.
- **weak** — venue quality unverifiable, or claim rests on a chain of secondary citation.

## 1. Heterofermentative → homofermentative succession, and how temperature shifts or breaks it

### 1.1 The baseline succession (and its temperature envelope) — **strong**
**Breidt, F., McFeeters, R.F., Pérez-Díaz, I. & Lee, C.-H. (2013).** "Fermented Vegetables," ch. 33 in *Food Microbiology: Fundamentals and Frontiers*, 4th ed. (Doyle & Buchanan, eds.), ASM Press. doi:10.1128/9781555818463.ch33
Retrieved PDF: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p380.pdf

- *"L. mesenteroides* grows more rapidly than most other LAB **over a range of temperatures (5 to 35 °C) and NaCl brine concentrations (0 to 5%)**."
- *"L. mesenteroides* carries out a heterolactic fermentation of vegetable sugars… and produces carbon dioxide and acids (lactic and acetic)."
- The high acidity produced *"inhibits the growth of these heterofermentative microbes in favor of more acid-tolerant homofermentative LAB."* Homofermentative *Lb. plantarum* *"will eventually outcompete other LAB because of its superior acid tolerance."*
- **The succession can be broken by SALT, not only temperature:** *"In brined vegetables with initial NaCl concentrations of 6 to 12%, such as pickled cucumbers and olives, Lactobacillus species, primarily Lb. plantarum, dominate the fermentation **from the start**, with little or no evidence of heterolactic species present."*
- Kinetics at the reference condition: *"After about 1 week of fermentation, the heterofermentative LAB, which may grow to 9 log CFU/ml or greater, die off. They are replaced by the more acid-tolerant homofermentative microorganisms."*
- **Reference condition for high-quality sauerkraut without starter:** *"the equilibrated NaCl concentration is adjusted to 2% and the temperature is maintained at 18 °C."*
- End products: *"mannitol and acetic acid (about 1% each) and lactic acid, which may exceed 2%."*
- Kimchi/refrigerator practice: domestic *"kimchi refrigerators"* provide *"an initial 18 °C fermentation period of a few days, followed by very cold refrigeration (1 to 2 °C). This procedure allows the initial heterolactic stage of fermentation to occur but delays the onset of the homolactic stage of fermentation, keeping kimchi from becoming too sour."* Optimum taste at **pH 4.0–4.5 and acidity 0.5–0.6%**.

### 1.2 The succession as a *timed* event at 18 °C / 2% salt — **strong**
**Fleming, H.P. (1987).** "Considerations for the controlled fermentation and storage of sauerkraut." In *1987 Sauerkraut Seminar*, N.Y. State Agric. Exp. Sta. Special Report No. 61:26–32 (USDA-ARS).
Retrieved (scanned PDF, read as page images): https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p201.pdf

- Raw cabbage load: total aerobes 1.3 × 10⁵, **Enterobacteriaceae 3.9 × 10³, lactic acid bacteria only 4.2 × 10¹ per g** — LAB are outnumbered ~3000:1 at the start and *"predominated within about 4 days after the shredded cabbage was slated."*
- **"Up to about 8 days, most of the lactic acid bacteria were of the gas-forming (heterofermentative) type, typical of *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*. Beyond 8 days, most of the lactic acid bacteria were of the nongas-forming (homofermentative) type, typical of *Lactobacillus plantarum*."**
- Ferment divided into a **"gaseous"** (0–8 d) and **"nongaseous"** phase. Mannitol, acetic acid and ethanol were *"produced rapidly during the gaseous stage and did not change appreciably thereafter"*; **lactic acid continued to rise throughout** (to ~2.2% acidity, pH ~3.5).
- Dissolved CO₂ peaked at roughly **150–160 mg/100 mL brine at ~4 days**.
- Author's framing: *"These microbiological changes are consistent with the observation of Pederson and Albury (1969) and others for sauerkraut that contains about 2% salt and is held at about 65 °F"* (= **18.3 °C**).

### 1.3 The decisive modern experiment: temperature flips which genus wins — **strong**
**Qiao, N.-Z. & Gänzle, M. (2026 / online 2025).** "From phyllosphere to fermentation: Impact of fermentation scale and temperature on sauerkraut fermentation." *Int. J. Food Microbiol.* **447**:111571. doi:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2025.111571 (PMID 41365134)
Retrieved abstract via Semantic Scholar / PubMed API for that DOI.

- White cabbage, **2.5% (w/w) NaCl**, fermentations at **10 °C and 20 °C**, scales 0.8 g / 8 g / 30 g.
- **"At the 30 g scale, *Lactiplantibacillus* dominated at 20 °C regardless of cabbage source, whereas *Leuconostoc* or *Rahnella* remained dominant at 10 °C."**
- *"The organic acid concentrations and the metabolism of phenolic compounds differed depending on whether Lactiplantibacillus, Leuconostoc or Rahnella were the predominant fermentation microorganisms."* ← **character, not speed.**
- Failure mode at small scale: *"Fermentation of 0.8 g cabbage SF with 20 replicates per group supported growth of **Enterobacterales but not of LAB**."*
- Authors' conclusion: *"critical roles of Lactiplantibacillus at higher temperatures and Leuconostoc at lower temperatures."*

### 1.4 Same directional result in Chinese fermented vegetables — **strong**
**He, Z., Chen, H., Wang, X., Lin, X., Ji, C., Li, S. & Liang, H. (2020).** "Effects of different temperatures on bacterial diversity and volatile flavor compounds during the fermentation of suancai…" *LWT* **118**:108773. doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2019.108773
Retrieved abstract via Semantic Scholar API.

- 10 / 15 / 20 / 25 °C. **"Leuconostoc predominated the fermentation at 10 °C and 15 °C, while Weissella and Lactococcus predominated at 20 °C and 25 °C."**
- *"With the increase of the fermentation temperature, the kinds of VFCs were also increased."*
- *"20 °C could accelerate the maturation **and improve the sensory quality** of suancai."*
- Note the non-monotonic message: at 20 °C the *character* changed (different dominant genus) and sensory quality was judged better — temperature is not simply "colder = better."

**Wang, D.-D., Chen, G., Tang, Y., Li, H., Shen, W., Wang, M., Liu, S.-L. & Qin, W. (2020).** "Effects of temperature on paocai bacterial succession revealed by culture-dependent and culture-independent methods." *Int. J. Food Microbiol.* **317**:108463. doi:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2019.108463
Retrieved abstract via Semantic Scholar API.

- 10 / 15 / 25 / 35 °C, 56 samples, 1,964,231 reads, 405 OTUs, 213 genera.
- **10 °C:** *Lactococcus* at the start → *Leuconostoc* + *Weissella* mid → *Lactobacillus* + *Leuconostoc* dominate late.
- **15 °C:** *Lactococcus* start → *Leuconostoc* mid → *Lactobacillus* dominant late.
- **25 °C:** *Lactococcus* start → ***Weissella* + *Lactobacillus*** mid → *Lactobacillus* dominant late. (**Leuconostoc has dropped out of the mid-stage relative to 10/15 °C.**)
- **35 °C:** *Lactococcus*, *Weissella* **and** *Lactobacillus* all present *from the start*; *Weissella* + *Lactobacillus* mid; *Lactobacillus* dominant late.
- *"The bacterial diversity decreased with the progression of fermentation, and **some spoiled samples had an increased diversity**."*
- Also confirms: *"increasing the fermentation temperature in a certain range is beneficial for rapid paocai acid production and shortening of the maturity period."*

### 1.5 Independent confirmation in a commercial-style trial — **moderate** (conference abstract, not a full paper)
**Kuldjärv, R., Aus, E., Vaikma, H., Junusova, M., Meikas, A. & Kazantseva, J. (2023).** "Influence of fermentation temperature on the chemical, microbial and sensory profile of sauerkraut and kimchi." Abstract, 14th Symposium on Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB2023).
https://zonderzorg.registraid.com/LAB2023/index.php?pntType=ConPagina&pntHandler=IndieningPage&conIndieningId=711&pntScd=d&pntRef=

- 4 kg batches at **15, 20 and 25 °C**; sampled days 0, 4, 7, 14.
- **"Sugars were depleted for the 14th day at temperatures 20 and 25 °C, but not at 15 °C."**
- **"Sensory analysis showed also clear differences between temperatures 15 °C, and 20/25 °C."**
- **"Sauerkraut had a higher proportion of *Leuconostoc*, especially at lower temperatures… the warmer the environment, the less *Leuconostoc* and *Latilactobacillus* occur."**

### 1.6 Kimchi-specific (relayed from delegated sub-research; abstracts/full texts retrieved by that sub-researcher)
- **Kim, E., Yang, S.M. & Kim, H.Y. (2021).** *Foods* **10**(5):1068. doi:10.3390/foods10051068 (PMC8151656) — **strong.** *"LAB, such as Levilactobacillus (Lb.) brevis and Lactiplantibacillus (Lpb.) plantarum associated with rancidity and tissue softening, proliferated faster at higher temperatures than at low temperature"*; *"Latilactobacillus (Lat.) sakei and Leuconostoc (Leu.) mesenteroides, which produce beneficial substances and flavor, were mainly distributed in kimchi fermented at 4 °C."* **This is the single cleanest "character not speed" citation for kimchi.**
- **Lee, H.Y., Haque, M.A. & Cho, K.M. (2020).** *J. Appl. Biol. Chem.* **63**(4):429–437. doi:10.3839/jabc.2020.056 — 8/15/25 °C; endpoint pH 3.92/3.79/3.48 and acidity 1.12/1.35/1.54%. *Weissella koreensis* first appeared day 12/9/6. (Caveat flagged by the sub-researcher: this paper did **not** measure mannitol; mannitol appears only in its literature review.)
- **Jung, S., Hwang, I.M. & Lee, J.H. (2024).** *Heliyon* **10**(5):e27174. doi:10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e27174 (PMC10926072) — 4/10/15 °C; **week-4 acidity 1.51% / 2.32% / 4.07%** (more than doubles), pH 4.37/4.02/3.63; species richness (wk4) 11 / 20 / 19.
- **Mheen, T.-I. & Kwon, T.-W. (1984).** *Korean J. Food Sci. Technol.* **16**(4):443–450 — https://koreantk.com/ktkp2014/thesis/thesis-view.view?ctrlNo=SPGHB5-1984-v16n4-443&tempLang=en — **moderate (abstract only; full text login-gated).** *"the main microorganism responsible for Kimchi fermentation was Leu. mesenteroides and Lac. plantarum was the main acidifying organism."* *"The ratio of volatile to non-volatile acids reached its maximum at the optimum ripening time of Kimchi and decreased thereafter."* And the counter-intuitive one: **"While the total aerobic bacteria and fungi decreased during Kimchi fermentation, the yeast increased significantly at lower temperature."** No numeric pH/acidity/temperature values are in the abstract — **do not attribute numbers to this paper.**

**Answer to Q1:** The classic *Leuconostoc* → *Lactiplantibacillus* succession is real and is set by acid tolerance at a reference of ~18 °C and ~2% NaCl. It **shifts** in time (2–3× faster from 8→25 °C) and **breaks qualitatively** at three edges: (a) **≥20–25 °C** *Lactiplantibacillus* (*Lb. plantarum*) or *Weissella* takes the early/mid stage that *Leuconostoc* would otherwise hold; (b) **≤10 °C** *Leuconostoc*/*Rahnella*/*Latilactobacillus* hold the ferment to the end and the homofermentative finish may never properly occur; (c) **high salt (6–12% NaCl)** abolishes the heterofermentative phase entirely regardless of temperature.

Sathyabama Institute of Science and Technology, *Food & Nutraceuticals SBTA7013*, Unit I (M.Tech Biotechnology course material), sauerkraut section. https://sist.sathyabama.ac.in/sist_coursematerial/uploads/SBTA7013.pdf

**At 2.25% salt, from Pederson & Albury (1969) as reproduced:**

| Temperature | What happens to the microbiology | What happens to the product |
|---|---|---|
| **18.3 °C (65 °F)** — stated optimum | *L. mesenteroides* initiates → *Lb. brevis* → *Lb. plantarum* most active in final stages | Final total acidity **1.7–2.3%** (as lactic); **acetic/lactic (volatile:nonvolatile) ratio ≈ 1:4**; complete in **1–2 months** |
| **23 °C (73.4 °F)** | faster | Brine acidity **1.0–1.5% in 8–10 days**; fully fermented in **~1 month** |
| **32 °C (89.6 °F)** | **"the growth of *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* was retarded and *Lactobacillus brevis* and *Lactobacillus plantarum* dominated"**; the fermentation **"became essentially a homofermentation dominated by *Lactobacillus plantarum* and *Pediococcus cerevisiae*"** | **1.8–2.0% acid in 8–10 days**. **"quality attributes of flavor and aroma deteriorated and the kraut was reminiscent of acidified cabbage because of the large quantity of lactic acid and little acetic acid"**; kraut **"would darken readily"** and should be canned as quickly as possible |
| **7.5 °C (45.5 °F)** | *L. mesenteroides* grows at lower temperature than the other LAB; *Lactobacillus* and *Pediococcus* *"grow poorly if at all at 7.5 °C"* | Ferment **still succeeds**: acidity **0.4% in ~10 days**, **0.8–0.9% in <1 month**; CO₂ saturation + anaerobiosis preserves the mass; completion waits for warming and **"may require 6 months or more"**; **"Such kraut is generally of superior quality."** |

**Institutional recommendation from the same source:** *"Precedent for the recommendation by Pederson and Albury that sauerkraut be fermented at **not over 18.3 °C (65 °F)**."*

**⚠ Direct institutional contradiction to flag.** USDA home-canning guidance recommends **70–75 °F (21–24 °C)** as the *good* sauerkraut temperature (see 2.2), while the classical commercial research recommendation is **≤ 65 °F (18.3 °C)**. The USDA figure is a compromise for home kitchens (fast enough to outrun spoilage without a controlled room) — not a quality optimum. Any "temperature changes character" argument should use the Pederson/Fleming line, not the USDA line.

  - **Pink kraut (safe unless slimy):** *"Yeast growth due to excess or uneven salt distribution or improper coverage"* **OR** *"Natural pigments in cabbage enhanced by acidity."*

- **McIntyre, L.   - **CCP table (verbatim):** Ferment — *"Initial ferment should begin within 48 hrs. Observe formation of bubbles. **Time: up to 4 weeks. Temp: 10 °C to 25 °C.**"*

  - Sauerkraut: *"Fermentation temperature is at 20–25 °C in the first phase and needs to be lowered then to 14–18 °C."* 4–6 weeks; salt 2–2.5%; *"Optimal acidity is 1.0–1.8% and pH value 4.1 or lower."* Storage: *"below +15 °C"* for 12-month shelf life.

### 2.3 What actually causes the defects (three mechanisms, usually conflated in folk advice)

**Slimy / ropy sauerkraut — two entirely different things:**
1. **Dextran from *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, NOT a defect.** Breidt et al. (2013): *"This core of the cabbage contains sucrose, which can lead to dextran formation by L. mesenteroides, resulting in a slimy or stringy texture."* The Sathyabama text (attributed to the Pederson line) is blunter: *"The fermenting kraut may become very slimy during the intermediate stage of fermentation but with additional time the dextrans are utilized by other lactic acid bacteria. Thus, it is imperative to distinguish between dextran induced slimy kraut and permanently slimy kraut caused by pectolytic activity. **The former condition certainly is not a defect but should be considered a normal step in a natural progression.**"* (moderate — tertiary text)
2. **Pectolytic molds/yeasts → permanent slime + softening.** Same source: *"slimy, softened kraut caused by pectolytic activity of these same molds and yeasts."* BCCDC names yeast pectinase. This one *is* spoilage.

**Pink/red sauerkraut — three mechanisms:**
- **Yeasts (the usual folk answer, and it is correct but incomplete):** **Fred, E.B. & Peterson, W.H. (1922).** "The Production of Pink Sauerkraut by Yeasts." *J. Bacteriol.* **7**(2):257–269. doi:10.1128/jb.7.2.257-269.1922 (PMC378966, PMID 16558953) — **strong**, retrieved via PubMed. Also **Pederson, C.S. & Kelly, C.D. (1938).** "Development of pink colour in sauerkraut." *Food Research* **3**:538 (reference verified; full text not retrieved).
- **A red pigment produced by *Lactobacillus brevis* itself:** **Stamer, J.R., Hrazdina, G. & Stoyla, B.O. (1973).** "Induction of Red Color Formation in Cabbage Juice by *Lactobacillus brevis* and Its Relationship to Pink Sauerkraut." *Appl. Microbiol.* **26**(2):161–166. doi:10.1128/am.26.2.161-166.1973 (PMC379744, PMID 16349964) — **strong**, abstract retrieved via PubMed. Key detail: the water-soluble red pigment forms in the **post-logarithmic phase**, is **pH dependent (5.2 to 6.3)**, and *"can be suppressed by chemical reductants or anaerobic conditions of growth."* Practical implication: **normal sauerkraut is too acid for this**, so *L. brevis* red pigment should not normally occur — it is a risk if alkali is accidentally added. Note this is a *different* phenomenon from the pink from cabbage pigments enhanced by acidity that OSU Extension describes.
- **Cabbage's own pigments, intensified by acid** (OSU Extension SP 50-745).

**Rancid/oxidised off-flavours and vitamin C loss in storage — oxygen and light, not temperature:**
Fleming (1987), Table 3: kraut juice in 50-mL beakers at room temperature — ascorbic acid **43.6 mg/100 mL** at day 0; held under **air**: 33.8 (d1), 26.4 (d3), **0.0 (d6)**; under **air + UV (254 nm)**: **0.0 by day 1**; under **nitrogen**: 43.6 (d1), **44.4 (d3)**, 38.5 (d6). *"In the absence of oxygen, fully fermented sauerkraut seems to be microbiologically stable."*

**Zapatera / secondary fermentation in stored pickles — a warm-weather event:**
Breidt et al. (2013): *"Occasionally, fermented cucumbers undergo an undesired secondary fermentation during storage, which is characterized by an increase in pH, the disappearance of lactic acid, and the formation of propionic and butyric acids. **The incidence of fermented cucumber spoilage tends to increase at the beginning of the spring season, when the ambient temperature increases.** … may include the growth of spore-forming bacteria such as clostridia when the pH increases above 4.6."*

**Texture loss vs temperature (directly measured):**
**Ge, L., Huang, Y.-L., Li, X., Wang, N., Liu, J., Liu, M., Mei, Y. & Yang, M. (2024).** "Temperature-driven divergence in molecular distribution and microbial invasion and the associated texture softening during dual-phase fermentation of Paocai." *Food Chemistry* **457**:140171. doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2024.140171 — **strong** (abstract retrieved via Semantic Scholar API).
> *"…as affected by fermentation temperatures of **10 °C, 20 °C and 30 °C**. Results demonstrated that, compared with 10 °C and 30 °C, **20 °C attenuated softening of Paocai by restraining microbial invasion and suppressing pectinolysis**. Moreover, at 20 °C, a balanced molecular distribution and microbial community were achieved… **By contrast, 10 °C and 30 °C promoted nonfermentative microbial genera, retarding fermentation.**"*
> This is a genuinely important nuance: **both** too-cold and too-hot promote non-fermentative organisms. There is an interior optimum, not a monotonic "colder is better."

## 3. CO₂ / gas production vs temperature; "bloater" damage in pickles

### 3.1 The headline temperature experiment — **strong**
**Etchells, J.L., Fleming, H.P., Hontz, L.H., Bell, T.A. & Monroe, R.J. (1975).** "Factors influencing bloater formation in brined cucumbers during controlled fermentation." *J. Food Sci.* **40**(3):569–575. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1975.tb12530.x
Retrieved as scanned PDF (read as page images): https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p124.pdf
Conditions: size No. 3 pickling cucumbers, 55-gal drums, controlled fermentation predominated by *Lactobacillus plantarum*, incubated in controlled-temperature rooms *"maintained at 27 °C (80.6 °F) or 32 °C (89.6 °F)."*

| | **32 °C (89.6 °F)** | **27 °C (80.6 °F)** |
|---|---|---|
| Total bloaters, **controlled** fermentation (Table 3) | **34.2 %** | **24.5 %** |
| Total bloaters, **natural** fermentation (Table 3) | **93.5 %** | **90.0 %** |
| Brine acid / pH (Table 3) | 1.32 / 3.40 | 1.30 / 3.38 |
| Max CO₂, mg/100 mL brine (Table 3) | 68.5 | 66.5 |
| Total bloaters by brine depth 27 / 18 / 9 in (Table 4) | **48.0 / 21.0 / 12.0 %** | 24.5 / 17.0 / 14.5 % |

- **Brine depth interacts with temperature:** *"the average increase in percent total bloaters is 5 % for each 9-inch increase in depth; **at 32 °C this increase averages 18 %**."*
- **ANOVA (Table 5):** temperature significant at p < 0.01 (mean square 69.84); bloat type × temperature 352.67; depth (linear) 29.39. Bloat type × depth was **not** significant.
- **CO₂ kinetics:** *"At both 32 and 27 °C, concentrations of CO₂ reached a maximum at the greatest brine depth at about 6 days, and the values were similar, about 66 mg/100 ml brine."*
- **Circulation/purging works:** brine circulation (started day 0, 1, 2 or 3) reduced CO₂ accumulation versus a non-circulated control.
- Sugar→acid conversion in these controlled fermentations was *"usually within 7–10 days."*

### 3.2 Normal vs excessive CO₂ — **strong**
**Fleming, H.P. (1979).** "Purging carbon dioxide from cucumber brines to prevent bloater damage — A review." *Pickle Pak Science* **6**(1):8–22. ARS PDF (scanned; read as images): https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p141.pdf
- *"Bloater damage that normally occurs in unpurged cucumbers, which varies among companies and geographically. **Increases in yields of 10 to 50 % or more in sound brine stock have been reported.**"* Damage is worst for cucumbers **1.5 inches or more in diameter**.
- **CO₂ has three sources, two of them previously ignored:** *"the non-gas-forming lactic acid bacteria and the cucumbers themselves."* *"Although *Lactobacillus plantarum* is loosely classified by microbiologists as a 'non-gas-former,' it produces sufficient CO₂ in cucumber fermentations to be significant."* Yeasts are the third (aerial) source.
- Fig. 2: CO₂ accumulation in brined cucumbers to **~100–170 mg/100 mL brine over 13 days**, **higher at lower NaCl** (0–2% > 5–7%). *"BRINE TURBIDITY FIRST NOTED AT THIS POINT."*
- Historical: *"the yeasts were the cause of bloating"* was the earlier theory; later work showed *L. plantarum* alone could cause it.
- Fig. 5: standardized **bloater evaluation chart** — types **balloon / lens / honeycomb**, degrees **none / slight / moderate / advanced**, convertible to a *"bloater index."* *"In extreme cases, the entire seed area is pressed against the outer skin and flesh, leaving a completely hollow seed area."*

### 3.4 The Enterobacteriaceae / Bacillus angle — **strong**
**Zhai, Y., Pagán-Medina, C.G. & Pérez-Díaz, I.M. (2023).** "CO₂-mediated bloater defect can be induced by the uncontrolled growth of *Enterobacteriaceae* in cucumber fermentation." *Food Science & Nutrition* **11**(10):6178–6187. doi:10.1002/fsn3.3557 (PMC10563668, PMID 37823135) — full text retrieved.
- Seven cucumber-indigenous *Enterobacteriaceae* produced **7.22–171.5 mM CO₂** in cucumber juice medium at 30 °C; *Enterobacter cancerogenus* 171.58 ± 42.96 mM, *E. cloacae* 170.89 ± 0.83 mM, *E. nimipressuralis* only 16.85 ± 6.53 mM.
- In the model cucumber fermentation: **bloater index 25.4** (*E. cancerogenus*) and **17.4** (*E. nimipressuralis*); **no defect in the uninoculated control**. Up to **2 % hydrogen** produced.
- **Bloater defect costs the pickling industry 3 %–40 % of production** (citing Fleming et al. 1973b and processor communication, 2022).
- Commercial brine baseline: **1.06 M (6 %) NaCl, 15 mM acetic acid, 25 mM CaCl₂**; *"The fermentation, led by the indigenous lactic acid bacteria (LAB), proceeds to completion in **21–30 days depending on the ambient temperature**."*
- **Bacillus isolated from the control jars:** *Bacillus tequilensis*, *Lysinibacillus fusiformis*, *Bacillus subtilis*, and *Bacillus* spp. (16S rDNA, NCBI MT416150–MT416160). Useful because it documents *Bacillus* growing in a cucumber fermentation system when the LAB/Enterobacteriaceae balance is disturbed.

### 3.5 Practical CO₂ control levers (all from retrieved sources)
- **Purge with N₂** (Fleming 1979; Etchells et al. 1975).
- **Circulate brine** to strip CO₂ (Etchells et al. 1975, Fig. 7). Breidt et al. (2013): *"some of the excess brine formed from the salted cabbage may be removed from the tanks during the first week"*; *"The addition of acid can help remove excess CO₂."*
- **Keep the headspace/depth shallow** — depth is a first-order driver of bloater severity (Etchells et al. 1975).
- **Air-purging trade-off:** Breidt et al. (2013) — *"Potassium sorbate (~0.04 %) or 0.16 % acetic acid can be used as processing aids to limit the growth of aerobic microorganisms in air-purged cucumber fermentations, particularly molds and yeasts. **Excessive growth of aerobic microorganisms may also be controlled by stopping air purging for several hours each day.**"*
- **Lower temperature (27 vs 32 °C) roughly cuts controlled-fermentation bloater incidence by a third** (34.2 % → 24.5 %, Etchells et al. 1975).

## 4. Colour, texture, vitamin C and aroma vs temperature

### 4.1 Kimchi: 4–10 °C vs 20–25 °C, with numbers
*(relayed from delegated sub-research; full texts/abstracts retrieved by that sub-researcher)*

**Kim, J.Y., Kim, B.S., Kim, J.H., Oh, S.I. & Koo, J. (2020).** *Foods* **9**(8):1075. doi:10.3390/foods9081075 (PMC7465714) — **strong**, full text.
- **Texture (puncture hardness):** initial **14.7 N** → **7.8 N after 8 days at 20 °C**; 7.8 N after **16 days at 10 °C**; still **18.6 N after 35 days at 5 °C**. *"There was a rapid decrease of hardness at higher fermentation temperatures."* Attributed to soluble pectin increasing during ripening.
- **pH:** initial 5.93 → **4.43 in 2 days at 20 °C**, 3.83 by day 10; 10 and 5 °C reached 4.14–4.24 at 10 and 20 days; 0 °C stayed 5.93–6.06 until day 14, 4.22–4.37 by day 63.
- **Acidity:** 0.24 % → **1.11 % within 7 days at 20 °C**; 0.95–0.96 % after 14 days (10 °C) and 35 days (5 °C). *"the maximum acidity value of Kimchi is affected by temperature."*
- **Colour (Hunter):** redness **a** at 20 °C rose **14.78 → 21.12 in 4 days**; 10 °C reached 20.08 by day 16; 5 °C 20.35 by day 25; 0 °C 20.02 by day 56. Lightness **L** barely changed; yellowness **b** rose early at all temperatures then plateaued with no significant difference. Authors: Hunter values *"were not an adequate quality index."*
- **Sensory (20 trained assessors, 9-point):** softness / sour smell / sour taste increased significantly until **day 5 (20 °C)**, **day 16 (10 °C)**, **day 30 (5 °C)**, **day 56 (0 °C)**; crunchiness decreased until day 5 (20 °C) and day 12 (10 °C) then stabilised, but kept falling at 5 and 0 °C.
- **Industry setpoints (verbatim):** *"The temperature of the workshop where Kimchi is manufactured is kept below 10 °C, and the storage warehouse where it is kept until dispatch is typically 0–2 °C."*
- **Threshold in use:** *"According to the Codex Alimentarius … the composition of acidity for fermented kimchi should not be more than 1 %. Accordingly, the end of storage for Kimchi was generally selected to be the time for the acidity to approach the value of 1 %."*
- *"Leuconostoc spp. grows well even at low temperatures, [so] it was the most-observed bacteria on all storage temperatures."*

**Hong, S.P., Lee, E.J., Kim, Y.H. & Ahn, D.U. (2016).** "Effect of Fermentation Temperature on the Volatile Composition of Kimchi." *J. Food Sci.* **81**(11):C2623–C2629. doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13517 (PMID 27750382) — **moderate** (abstract via PubMed).
- 4 °C vs 20 °C. **Optimum ripening: 35 days at 4 °C vs 2 days at 20 °C.** pH at optimum ripening **4.97 (4 °C) vs 4.41 (20 °C)**; titratable acidity **0.59 % vs 0.76 %**.
- 40 volatiles identified. *"Organic acids, esters, and nitriles were detected **only** in kimchi fermented at 20 °C."* Dimethyl disulfide, methyl-2-propenyl disulfide and di-2-propenyl disulfide at 20 °C were **more than 2× the 4 °C** levels. The strong pungent odour at 20 °C is attributed to *"the high amount of organic acids (low pH) and sulfur compounds."*

**Park, S.-e., Seo, S., Kim, E.-J., Na, C.-S. & Son, H.-S. (2018).** "Effects of different fermentation temperatures on metabolites of Kimchi." *Food Bioscience* **23**:100. doi:10.1016/j.fbio.2018.03.009 — **moderate** (abstract via Semantic Scholar).
- 4 / 12 / 20 °C; GC-MS metabolomics. Distinct metabolite trajectories by temperature; discriminating metabolites identified as *"alanine, propylene glycol, fumaric acid, malic acid, citric acid, and galactaric acid."*

**Laksana, A.J., Choi, Y.-M., Kim, J.-H., Kim, B.-S. & Kim, J.-Y. (2022).** *Foods* **11**(15):2323. doi:10.3390/foods11152323 (PMC9368639) — **strong** (relayed from sub-research).
- 0/5/10/20 °C. LAB at 20 °C *"drastically increased to 8.34 ± 0.12 log cfu/g in less than 24 h."* 32 volatiles detected; the 20 °C kimchi *"produced VOCs separately compared to the Kimchi stored at 0 °C, 5 °C, and 10 °C, which gave a strong ethereal, sour, acetic, sweet, alcohol aroma."*
- **Carbonation/gas in packages:** **Hong, S.-I., Lee, M.-K. & Park, W.-S. (2000).** "Gas Composition within Kimchi Package as Influenced by Temperature and Seasonal Factor." *Korean J. Food Sci. Winter cabbage produced more gas, faster, than summer cabbage. **Numeric CO₂ values are NOT in the abstract.**

### 4.2 Over-ripening, pectin and the enzymatic-vs-microbial question
*(relayed from delegated sub-research)*
- **Definitions are operational, not canonical.** Codex ceiling is **1.0 % total acidity as lactic acid**; Kim et al. 2020 uses "acidity approaching 1 %"; **Oun, A.A., Roy, S., Shin, G.H., Yoo, S. & Kim, J.T. ## 5. Threshold temperatures, who takes over, and official guidance

### 5.1 The retrieved temperature envelope, side by side

| Source | Lower limit | Upper limit | What it governs |
|---|---|---|---|
| BCCDC (McIntyre 2024), sauerkraut CCP | **10 °C** | **25 °C** | *"above 25 °C or below 10 °C … favor the growth of spoilage organisms, affect flavour and may also result in poor LAB activity"* |
| USDA / NCHFP (AIB 539, rev. 2015) | **< 60 °F may not ferment** | **> 75 °F kraut may become soft** | Home sauerkraut; recommends 70–75 °F |
| Pederson & Albury (1969) via tertiary text | **7.5 °C works** (slowly; 6+ months) | **≤ 18.3 °C recommended**; 32 °C degrades flavour/aroma | Commercial sauerkraut at 2.25 % salt |
| Breidt et al. (2013) | *L. mesenteroides* grows **5–35 °C** | same | Pure-culture envelope of the initiator |
| FAO Bull. 119 (Dauthy 1995) | sauerkraut second phase **14–18 °C**; cucumbers **20–30 °C** | storage **< 15 °C** | Industrial process setpoints |
| Etchells et al. (1975) | 27 °C (80.6 °F) tested | 32 °C (89.6 °F) tested → **34.2 % bloaters vs 24.5 %** | Controlled cucumber fermentation |
| Ge et al. (2024) | **10 °C retards** (promotes nonfermentative genera) | **30 °C retards** (promotes nonfermentative genera) | Interior optimum at 20 °C for paocai texture |

**Consensus:** for sauerkraut-style cabbage ferments there is a **workable window of roughly 10–25 °C**, with a **quality optimum around 18 °C** and **quality/defect degradation above ~25 °C and below ~7–10 °C**. **NOT VERIFIED:** I found **no** peer-reviewed source establishing a hard *failure* temperature (a temperature above which the LAB fermentation simply cannot proceed) for cabbage. The USDA "< 60 °F may not ferment" is a practical home caveat (slow initiation → spoilage wins), and it is explicitly *contradicted* by Pederson & Albury's 7.5 °C success. The two are reconciled by **inoculum load and anaerobiosis**: at 7.5 °C the *Leuconostoc* already present plus CO₂ saturation hold the substrate until it warms.

### 5.2 What grows instead (retrieved identifications)
- **Yeasts (pink/white colonies, film, off-odour, pectinase):** *Rhodotorula* → pink kraut (BCCDC 2024; and the Pederson line as reproduced in the Sathyabama text). ***Pichia membranaefaciens*** *"voraciously oxidize[s] lactic acid contained in salt brines"* (Sathyabama text, **moderate**). In over-ripened kimchi: ***Pichia kudriavzevii*** (major, with high PG activity), *Kazachstania servazii*, *K. exigua*, *K. bulderi*, *Rhodotorula mucilaginosa* (Moon et al. 2014, **moderate**).
- **Kimchi white-colony-forming yeasts — the temperature/pH/salt tolerance grid:** **Kim, M.J., Kang, S.E., Jeong, C.H., Min, S.G., Hong, S.W., Roh, S.W., Jhon, D.Y. & Kim, T.W. (2021).** *Foods* **10**(3):645. doi:10.3390/foods10030645 (PMC8003234) — **strong** (relayed from sub-research). All five WCFY (*Kazachstania servazzii*, *Candida sake*, *Debaryomyces hansenii*, *Pichia kudriavzevii*, *Hanseniaspora uvarum*) grew at **4, 10 and 20 °C**; **three grew at 0 °C**; **all grew at pH 3, 4 and 5**; **all survived 20 % (w/v) NaCl**; colonies formed **anaerobically**. *"the limitation of oxygen exposure was not enough to inhibit the appearance of WCFY on kimchi."*
- **Enterobacteriaceae:** dominate the first days of every vegetable ferment (Fleming 1987: 3.9 × 10³/g on cabbage; Breidt et al. 2013). They are also the main CO₂/bloater contributors if they are not out-competed (Zhai et al. 2023). **Crucially, high salt does not exclude them in pepper mashes** (see §6).
- ***Bacillus* / *Lysinibacillus*:** isolated from the *uninoculated control* jars of a pasteurized, acidified cucumber fermentation model (Zhai et al. 2023) — *B. tequilensis*, *Lysinibacillus fusiformis*, *B. subtilis*, *Bacillus* spp.
- **Clostridia / zapatera:** *"growth of spore-forming bacteria such as clostridia when the pH increases above 4.6"* — responsible for the propionic/butyric, malodorous spring spoilage of stored cucumber brines (Breidt et al. 2013).
- **Molds:** surface growth in warm conditions (OSU Extension SP 50-745; BCCDC 2024).

### 5.3 Official guidance — and the important negative finding
- **Codex Alimentarius CXS 260-2007, *Standard for Pickled Fruits and Vegetables*** (adopted 2007, amended 2015) — retrieved full text: http://www.fao.org/input/download/standards/10746/CXS_260e_2015.pdf
  - §2.1(b): *"processed or treated to produce an acid or acidified product preserved through **natural fermentation** or acidulants."*
  - §2.1(d): *"…to ensure an **equilibrium pH of less than 4.6**."*
  - §3.2.1.2: *"The percentage of salt in the covering liquid or the acidity of the media shall be sufficient to ensure the keeping quality and proper preservation of the product."*
  - **There is NO temperature provision anywhere in CXS 260-2007.** — **strong**
- **Codex CXS 223-2001, *Standard for Kimchi*** — confirmed as an adopted Codex standard, CCPFV, in the official FAO standards list: https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/codex-texts/list-standards/en/ (the adopted PDF itself was Cloudflare-blocked on every route I tried). **The sub-researcher retrieved the FAO-hosted *Proposed Draft* (Step 5)** at https://www.fao.org/4/w7974e/w7974e0p.htm, which specifies: kimchi is fermented *"to ensure the proper ripening and preservation of the product by lactic acid production **at low temperatures**"*; **total acidity (as lactic acid) not more than 1.0 % m/m**; **salt 1.0–4.0 % m/m**; texture *"reasonably firm, crisp, and chewy"*; colour *"red colour originating from red pepper"*; sampling storage 0–4 °C. — **moderate** (proposed draft, not the adopted text). ⚠ Note: the often-quoted designation "CXS 223-2003" is **wrong**; it is **CXS 223-2001**.
- **Codex CXS 279-1971, *Standard for Pickled Cucumbers (Cucumber Pickles)*** — confirmed in the FAO standards list (adopted text not retrieved).
- **The regulatory bottom line:** Codex standards for these commodities regulate **composition (pH, acidity, salt)**, not **process temperature**. The only document retrieved that sets an explicit fermentation-temperature CCP for a vegetable ferment is **BCCDC's 10–25 °C**. FAO Bull. 119 gives process setpoints but as technology guidance, not as a standard.
- Also note **USDA/FDA-relevant safety framing**: BCCDC §3.1/3.2 recommends fresh (unpasteurised) sauerkraut be refrigerated and held a minimum of two weeks once pH drops. And there is a counter-intuitive safety finding from kimchi: **acid-mediated pathogen kill is FASTER at higher temperature** — **Choi, Y. et al. (2018).** *J. Food Prot.* **81**(6):942–946. doi:10.4315/0362-028X.JFP-17-459 — *E. coli* death-phase shoulder fell from **208.18 h at 4 °C to 8.25 h at 25 °C**; *Salmonella* from **79.91 h to 0.97 h**. "Warmer = more dangerous" is not supported for this mechanism, even though it is supported for spoilage quality.

Salt % is 8 %, not company-documented.
- **Awad, Abdul Rahman (1986).** *Chemical and Physical Characteristics of Pepper Mash and Hot Pepper Sauce*, PhD dissertation, LSU. doi:10.31390/gradschool_dissertations.4217 — **strong** (full 188-page text read by sub-researcher). **This is the key temperature source.** Barrels *"were stored in a large warehouse at **ambient temperature**"* — **no numeric value, uncontrolled.** Three salt treatments **6 %, 8 %, 10 %** in 55-gal oak and plastic barrels, **two years** (Oct 1982–Oct 1984). Industry practice stated as *"mixed with **8 to 20 percent salt** and aged… **several weeks to three years**."* Salt effect: **6 % gave the greatest acidity** (*"enhanced the activities of most of the pepper mash microorganisms"*); **10 % inhibited most of the microflora except salt-tolerant microorganisms**, with a lag phase while the microorganisms adapted. **Temperature effect (verbatim):** *"The acidity was reduced sharply during those hot months even though the concentration of salt remained constant… the **high temperatures during aging encouraged the rapid growth of acid utilizing bacteria and scum yeasts**, thereby causing a sharp reduction in the acidity"*; June heat caused *"an unusual sharp increase in the pH value of all samples"*; spoilage noted as *"a noticeable **putrefied odor**, especially in the plastic samples."* Colour: 8 %-salt oak mash showed *"less color changes."*
  → **The most useful mechanistic finding for temperature control in pepper mash: summer heat caused the mash to lose acidity and go putrid, via acid-consuming bacteria and scum yeasts.** That is a spoilage-prevention argument for temperature control — not a LAB-optimum argument.
- **Watts, C., Janes, M., Prinyawiwatkul, W., Shen, Y., Xu, Z. & Johnson, C. (2018).** *Int. J. Food Sci. Technol.* **53**(8):1816–1823. doi:10.1111/ijfs.13792 — **moderate** (abstract via Europe PMC). Barrel fermentation *"from 2 weeks up to 3 years"*; 18-month natural fermentation; *"LAB and yeast presented a **symbiotic association** throughout"*; *"There were **minor changes in the colour of the mash**"*; six volatile compounds rose significantly during the first 60 days then declined significantly after 300 days. Salt % and temperature **not** in the abstract.

### 6.2 The one real temperature head-to-head — **strong for data, with a journal-tier caveat**
**Alberto, M.R., Perera, M.N. & Arena, M.E. (2013).** "Lactic Acid Fermentation of Peppers." *Food and Nutrition Sciences* **4**(11A):47–55. doi:10.4236/fns.2013.411A007
Full text: https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/455f/63e8f54dbe75e5813e8ccd3a33f816e4390b.pdf

- *Capsicum annuum grossum*; **fermentations at 22 °C and 30 °C**; medium **2 % or 0.2 % glucose, 4 % NaCl**; strains *Lactobacillus plantarum* N8, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* L, *Pediococcus pentosaceus* 12p.
- *"Sugar consumption, pH reduction and acid production were **higher at 30 °C** than at 22 °C."* Minimum pH < 3.0 at both. Maximum growth between 2 and 5 days.
- **The trade-off (the actual character finding):** after 30 days LAB survival was *"nearly 5 log ufc/ml"* and *"**survival was higher at the lower temperature**."* **At 22 °C a creamy white surface layer identified as yeasts appeared (day 5 natural, day 10 non-heating/inoculated); at 30 °C yeasts were not detected.**
- Best sensory result = mixed *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* + *Pediococcus pentosaceus*; lactic/acetic ratio ≈ 3.
- Caveat: SCIRP journal (lower tier), and the matrix is a glucose/NaCl solution rather than a high-salt mash.

### 6.3 Other retrieved pepper temperature/duration data points
- **Kádár, C.B. et al. (2022).** *Plants* **11**(8):1080. doi:10.3390/plants11081080 (PMC9025496) — **strong** (full text). Anaerobic spontaneous fermentation of pure chili paste, **21 days at 20 °C**. Capsaicinoids *"very stable."* Total ascorbic acid loss **19.01 %**; phenolics not significantly lost. LAB 50–3700 CFU/g → **3.8 × 10⁶ – 6.2 × 10⁸ CFU/g** at d21. Lactic acid **0.02 % → 1.13 %**; pH < 4 reached in **3–5 days**.
- **Torán-Pereg, P., Deba-Rementeria, S., Estrada, O., Pardo, J.E. & Vázquez-Araújo, L. (2023).** *Foods* **12**(19):3536. doi:10.3390/foods12193536 (PMC10572888) — **strong** (full text). Green Espelette mash, **21–24 °C, 15 days**; spontaneous vs inoculated *L. plantarum*. pH 5.22 → 4.83 (spontaneous) vs **3.66** (inoculated). Colour ΔE* 11.92 (spontaneous) vs 5.66 (inoculated) — **inoculation, not just temperature, drives colour retention.**
- **Di Cagno, R., Surico, R.F., Minervini, F., De Angelis, M., Rizzello, C.G. & Gobbetti, M. (2009).** *Int. J. Food Microbiol.* **130**(2):108–116. doi:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2009.01.019 — **moderate** (abstract). **35 °C for 15 HOURS** in 1 % (w/v) brine, then 85 °C/15 min, then 30 days room-temperature storage. ⚠ Torán-Pereg et al. cite this as "35 °C during 15 **days**" — the primary abstract says 15 **hours**. **Flag this discrepancy; do not propagate "15 days."** Started batches reached pH < 3.7 with enterobacteria and yeasts inhibited, and had significantly higher firmness and colour after 30 days; unstarted batches stalled at pH ~4.87 and were contaminated.

### 6.4 The premise correction that matters most — **high salt does NOT select for LAB**
- **Li, Y., Dong, L., Zhao, Y. & Zhu, Y. (2020).** "Metagenomic insights into the changes in microbial community and antimicrobial resistance genes associated with different salt content of red pepper (*Capsicum annuum* L.) sauce." *Food Microbiology* **85**:103295. doi:10.1016/j.fm.2019.103295 — **strong for the abstract.** *"While the family **Enterobacteriaceae dominated in high-salt (15–25 %) samples**, **Lactobacillaceae quickly became the dominant population in place of Enterobacteriaceae after 24 days in 10 % salt samples**."* Also: **0.05** antibiotic-resistance genes per cell on average in the 10 % salt sample vs **16.6 ARGs** in high-salt samples, hosts mainly Enterobacteriaceae (*Enterobacter*, *Citrobacter*, *Escherichia*, *Salmonella*, *Klebsiella*), multidrug resistance predominant. ⚠ Temperature and duration are **NOT VERIFIED** (full text blocked).
- **Aryee, A.N.A., Owusu-Kwarteng, J., Senwo, Z. & Alvarez, V.B. (2022).** "Characterizing fermented habanero pepper (*Capsicum chinense* L)." *Food Chemistry Advances* **1**:100137. doi:10.1016/j.focha.2022.100137 — **strong for the abstract.** Salt **5–25 % (w/w)**, sampled d0/7/14/30/50. LAB rose only from ~1.33–1.83 to **3.80–4.97 log CFU/mL** with pH stalling at **4.47–4.78**. *"The presence of **Enterobacteriaceae throughout the fermentation period** reiterates the need to develop and implement starter cultures."* Clustering was salt-dependent with low-salt (0 %, 5 %) separated from high-salt (10–25 %). Temperature **NOT VERIFIED**.
- **Etchells, Jones & Hoffman (1943)**, "Brine preservation of vegetables," *Proc. Inst. Food Tech.* 176–182 — **NOT RETRIEVED**; quoted second-hand inside Koh (2005): *"At or above 15 percent salt, little or no growth of acid bacteria occurred."* — **weak/secondary.**

→ **Bottom line:** no retrieved evidence supports *"8–10 % NaCl selects for LAB in pepper mash."* LAB dominate at ≤ 5 %, and at 10 % only after a ~24-day lag; at 15–25 % Enterobacteriaceae persist. **No retrieved source states a numeric NaCl tolerance ceiling for LAB in pepper mash.**

### 6.5 Colour / carotenoid / capsaicin in pepper — evidence gap
Capsaicinoids are robust across two independent studies (Koh 2005: unchanged over 24 months; Kádár et al. 2022: unchanged over 21 days at 20 °C). Watts et al. (2018, 18 months): *"minor changes in the colour of the mash."* Torán-Pereg et al. (2023): ΔE* 11.92 spontaneous vs 5.66 inoculated. Awad (1986): 8 % salt + oak gave less colour change. Janiszewska-Turak et al. (2022), *Molecules* **27**(23):8637 — chlorophyll → pheophytin degradation; red bell peppers most stable, green least.
**NOT VERIFIED: no retrieved study quantifies carotenoid or browning change as a function of pepper-mash FERMENTATION TEMPERATURE.** This is a genuine evidence gap.

## 7. Where home-fermentation folk wisdom contradicts the retrieved evidence

| Folk claim | What the retrieved evidence says |
|---|---|
| **"Warmer fermentation is just faster."** | **Contradicted.** Endpoint composition differs qualitatively: acidity 1.12/1.35/1.54 % at 8/15/25 °C (Lee et al. 2020); 1.51/2.32/4.07 % at 4/10/15 °C (Jung et al. 2024). Organic acids, esters and nitriles detected **only** at 20 °C, three sulfur compounds > 2× the 4 °C level (Hong et al. 2016). Dominant organism changes (Qiao & Gänzle 2026; He et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020; Kim E et al. 2021). Metabolic inventory changes (3,048 vs 2,853 metabolites, Jung et al. 2024). Exopolysaccharide *type* changes (dextran-only vs dextran+levan, Fernandez de Ullivarri et al. 2025). |
| **"Above 18 °C it's still fine, USDA says 70–75 °F."** | **Partly contradicted.** USDA home guidance does say 70–75 °F, but the classical commercial research recommendation from the same tradition is **≤ 18.3 °C (65 °F)** (Pederson & Albury 1969, via the retrieved tertiary text), and Breidt et al. (2013) also use **18 °C**. The USDA figure is a home-kitchen compromise, not a quality optimum. |
| **"Slimy sauerkraut means it's spoiled, throw it out."** | **Contradicted for one common case.** Dextran from *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* on cabbage-core sucrose produces transitory rope/slime that other LAB then consume; the retrieved text says it *"certainly is not a defect but should be considered a normal step in a natural progression."* OSU Extension nonetheless says discard *slimy* kraut (it distinguishes slimy from merely soft) — **so the institutions themselves disagree**. The safe discriminator is **permanent** slime + softening + off-odour (pectolytic molds/yeasts) vs **transient** rope that clears. |
| **"Pink kraut = yeast, always."** | **Incomplete.** Three mechanisms: *Rhodotorula* yeasts (BCCDC 2024; Fred & Peterson 1922), a pH-dependent **red pigment made by *Lactobacillus brevis* itself** (Stamer et al. 1973, only at pH 5.2–6.3, suppressed by reductants/anaerobiosis), and **cabbage's own pigments intensified by acid** (OSU Extension) — the last is harmless and needs no action. |
| **"Yeast is a warm-weather problem; keep it cold and you're safe from yeast."** | **Contradicted.** Mheen & Kwon (1984): *"the yeast increased significantly at **lower** temperature."* All five kimchi white-colony yeasts grew at 4, 10 and 20 °C, three at 0 °C, all at pH 3–5, all at 20 % NaCl, and all anaerobically (Kim MJ et al. 2021). *P. kudriavzevii* softened kimchi and produced off-odours **at 10 °C over 21 days** (Moon et al. 2014). Kumar et al.'s yeast appeared after 50 days in non-starter kimchi held at 7 °C → −1 °C (Chang & Chang 2010). Warmth does make *visible* colonies appear sooner (Suzuki et al. 2018: none until 35 d at 4 °C vs rapid at 10/15/25 °C), but cold is not protection. |
| **"Fizz/CO₂ means it's working, so more fizz is better."** | **Overstated.** CO₂ volume is largely a **temperature artefact**: greater and faster CO₂ at higher temperature, with rising CO₂ tracking falling pH (Hong et al. 2000). CO₂ is also the direct cause of **bloater damage** in pickles — 24.5 % vs 34.2 % bloaters at 27 vs 32 °C in controlled fermentations (Etchells et al. 1975), and 10–50 % yield losses in unpurged commercial stock (Fleming 1979). In kimchi, CO₂ generation can burst polyethylene packaging and constrained the storage endpoint (Kim JY et al. 2020). Good "carbonated mouthfeel" was associated with a **cold, controlled, yeast-free** fermentation, not a warm one (Chang & Chang 2010). |
| **"Softening is all microbial."** / **"Softening is all plant enzymes."** | **Both, at different stages.** Plant PG/PE from the cabbage dominate early and are progressively inactivated below pH ~4.0 (Baek et al. 1989; Park et al. 1991). The **late** PG rise is attributed to microorganisms (Park et al. 1991), and *P. kudriavzevii* has directly demonstrated high polygalacturonase activity causing softening at 10 °C (Moon et al. 2014). |
| **"Colder is always better."** | **Contradicted.** Ge et al. (2024): paocai at both **10 °C and 30 °C** showed **more** texture softening and **promoted nonfermentative microbial genera**, versus an interior optimum at 20 °C. Qiao & Gänzle (2026): at 10 °C *Leuconostoc*/*Rahnella* may hold the ferment and the homofermentative finish never properly occurs. BCCDC (2024) puts the floor at 10 °C; USDA says < 60 °F (15.5 °C) *"kraut may not ferment."* | 
| **"There is one correct 'ripe' pH."** | **Contradicted.** Optimum-ripening pH measured at 4 °C was **4.97** vs **4.41** at 20 °C in the same study (Hong et al. 2016). Ripeness is a different point on a different trajectory depending on temperature. |
| **"Warmer is always less safe."** | **Not supported for acid kill.** *E. coli* death-phase shoulder fell from 208.18 h at 4 °C to **8.25 h at 25 °C**; *Salmonella* from 79.91 h to **0.97 h** (Choi et al. 2018). Warm fermentation is a spoilage-quality problem, not uniformly a pathogen problem. |
| **"Tabasco ferments 3 years in oak at 8–10 % salt."** | **Partly wrong.** Oak barrels and "up to three years" are company-documented (McIlhenny 2019); **"8–10 % salt" is not a McIlhenny statement** (they say only *"a small amount of salt"*) — 8 % is from an LSU MS thesis, and the 1986 LSU dissertation states industry practice as **8–20 %**. The 1870 founding patent describes a **~6-week** process that added bisulphite **"for preventing fermentation."** |
| **"8–10 % salt selects for LAB in pepper mash."** | **Contradicted.** Enterobacteriaceae dominated at **15–25 %** salt; Lactobacillaceae took over at **10 %** salt **only after 24 days** (Li et al. 2020). Habanero mash at 5–25 % salt reached only 3.80–4.97 log CFU/mL LAB with pH stalling at 4.47–4.78 (Aryee et al. 2022). |

## 9. One-paragraph synthesis

Temperature is not a rate knob on vegetable lactic acid fermentations; it is a **selector**. At the classical sauerkraut reference condition (~18 °C, ~2 % NaCl) the ferment runs *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* → *Lb. brevis* → *Lb. plantarum*, ends at 1.7–2.3 % total acidity with an acetic:lactic ratio near 1:4, and tastes like sauerkraut. Raise it to 32 °C and *Leuconostoc* is retarded, the ferment becomes an essentially **homofermentative** *Lb. plantarum*/*Pediococcus* fermentation with 1.8–2.0 % acid in 8–10 days, a large excess of lactic over acetic acid, poor aroma, and kraut that darkens and is *"reminiscent of acidified cabbage"* — while in cucumbers the same shift raises bloater incidence from 24.5 % to 34.2 %. Drop it to 7.5 °C and the ferment still succeeds, but only via *Leuconostoc* plus CO₂ saturation, taking six months or more. Modern culture-independent work confirms the same edge behaviour in kimchi, suancai, paocai and sauerkraut: at 20–25 °C *Lactiplantibacillus*/*Weissella* take the niche that *Leuconostoc*/*Latilactobacillus* hold at 10 °C, and the volatile, organic-acid and metabolite profiles diverge qualitatively — esters, nitriles and sulfur compounds appear at 20 °C that are absent at 4 °C. The floor is soft rather than hard (USDA says < 60 °F may not ferment; Pederson & Albury fermented successfully at 7.5 °C), and the ceiling is a spoilage/quality ceiling rather than a LAB-growth ceiling: above ~25 °C, and also below ~10 °C, non-fermentative genera and pectinolytic yeasts gain ground. The single most useful institutional artefact is BCCDC's CCP: **10–25 °C, up to 4 weeks, fermentation visibly started within 48 h** — because Codex standards for these products regulate composition (kimchi ≤ 1.0 % acidity; pickled vegetables equilibrium pH < 4.6) and specify **no fermentation temperature at all**.
