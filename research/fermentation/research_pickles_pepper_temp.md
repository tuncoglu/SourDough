## PART A — CUCUMBER PICKLES / BRINED CUCUMBERS

### A.1 The single most important quantitative finding: rate vs. temperature (21 / 27 / 32 °C)

**Etchells, J.L., Costilow, R.N., Anderson, T.E. and Bell, T.A. (1964). "Pure Culture Fermentation of Brined
Cucumbers." *Applied Microbiology* 12(6): 523–535.** — **CONFIDENCE: STRONG** (full text read).

Working URL (full text PDF, free): https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC1058172?pdf=render
PMC landing page: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1058172/
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1128/am.12.6.523-535.1964

Exact wording, p. 528 (their emphasis structure preserved):

> "The incubation temperature greatly affected bacterial growth and acid production in pure culture fermentations
> (Fig. 6). However, the greatest effect was on the time required for maximal levels of both growth and acid
> production to be attained, rather than on the maximal levels reached. Thus, **a temperature decrease from 32 to
> 27 C or from 27 to 21 C extended the time required to attain maximal cell populations by about 1 day, and had a
> similar delaying effect on acid production.** Differential counts run on these fermentations demonstrated that the
> temperature affected each of the three species of lactic acid bacteria similarly, and did not result in a
> selection of one species over another."

Abstract wording (same paper):

> "The rates of growth and acid production in fermentations with a mixture of *P. cerevisiae*, *L. plantarum*, and
> *L. brevis* increased as the incubation temperature was increased from 21 to 27 to 32 C; however, the maximal
> populations and acidities attained were essentially the same for fermentations at each temperature."

Experimental conditions as reported: cucumbers brined to equilibrate at **5.4–5.6 % NaCl** (w/w), pure-culture
inoculation with *Pediococcus cerevisiae* + *Lactobacillus plantarum* + *L. brevis*, incubation at **21, 27 and
32 °C**, sampling over 1, 2, 3, 4, 10 and 20 days (x-axes of Figs. 2, 3, 5, 6). Also reported in the same paper:
pure-culture mixed inoculum reached maximal cell populations **within ~2 days** at 32 °C (Fig. 2A/2B), and the
3-species pure culture reached maximal populations **about 1 day earlier** than the natural (uninoculated)
fermentation of the same cucumber lot (Fig. 5).

**Practical translation (my arithmetic, not a quote):** over 21 → 32 °C (11 °C span) the *time to maximal
acidity* shortened by only about **2 days total** in this system — i.e. this is a much weaker temperature
sensitivity than a Q10≈2 rule of thumb would predict, because the endpoint acidity, not the rate, dominates the
process. **This is the key quantitative answer to "how much does temperature change the speed".**

**What this paper does NOT give:** a table of "days to reach 0.4 / 0.6 / 0.8 / 1.0 % titratable acidity at each
temperature". The rate data are in Figs. 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 (line graphs, time axis 1–20 days). I read the text and
tables but the figures are scanned images; I did **not** digitise them. If exact days-to-target-acidity per
temperature is essential, Figures 1 and 6 of this PDF are the place to digitise.

### A.2 Temperature vs. bloater damage and CO2 — the classic controlled-fermentation study

**Etchells, J.L., Fleming, H.P., Hontz, L.H., Bell, T.A. and Monroe, R.J. (1975). "Factors Influencing Bloater
Formation in Brined Cucumbers During Controlled Fermentation." *Journal of Food Science* 40(3): 569–575.**
— **CONFIDENCE: STRONG** (full text of the scanned journal issue read, including all tables).

DOI (paywalled at Wiley): https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1975.tb12530.x
Wiley abstract page: https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2621.1975.tb12530.x
**Full text I actually read (free scan of the whole JFS vol. 40 no. 3 issue):**
http://lib3.dss.go.th/fulltext/scan_ebook/j.food_sci_1975_v40_n3.pdf

Conditions: Size No. 3 pickling cucumbers (1½–2 in. dia.), 55-gal epoxy-lined drums, 25° salometer brine
(≈6.7 % NaCl at equilibration), acetic acid + sodium acetate buffer to pH 4.7 ± 0.2, inoculated with
*Lactobacillus plantarum* (~4 × 10⁹ cells/gal), **incubated in walk-in controlled-temperature rooms at 27 °C or
32 °C**, no N2 purge.

**Table 3 (their data; brine analyses after 10 days' fermentation; CO2 is the maximum, usually 4–6 days after
inoculation; values are means of duplicates):**

| Treatment | Total bloaters (%) | Brine pH | Acid (% as lactic) | Residual sugar (%) | Max. CO2 (mg/100 ml brine) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Controlled, **32 °C** | **48.0** | 3.34 | **1.32** | 0.02 | **68.5** |
| Controlled, **27 °C** | **24.5** | 3.38 | **1.30** | 0.00 | **66.5** |
| Natural, **32 °C** | 93.5 | 3.26 | 0.65 | 0.26 | 79.5 |
| Natural, **27 °C** | 90.0 | 3.28 | 0.62 | 0.08 | 91.5 |

**Table 4 (controlled fermentations, brine analyses after 12 days; means of duplicates, triplicates at 9-in.
depth). Brine-cucumber-mass depth × temperature:**

| Incubation | Depth (in.) | Total bloaters (%) | Acid (%) | pH |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32 °C | 27 | 48.0 | 1.32 | 3.34 |
| 32 °C | 18 | 21.0 | 1.25 | 3.35 |
| 32 °C | 9 | 12.0 | 1.18 | 3.36 |
| 32 °C | **average** | **27.0** | **1.25** | 3.35 |
| 27 °C | 27 | 24.5 | 1.30 | 3.38 |
| 27 °C | 18 | 17.0 | 1.22 | 3.35 |
| 27 °C | 9 | 14.5 | 1.14 | 3.35 |
| 27 °C | **average** | **18.7** | **1.22** | 3.36 |

*(Reducing sugars were < 0.1 % in all cases at day 12.)*

**Table 5 (analysis of variance, their numbers):** Temperature effect on bloater formation, mean square =
**69.44, significant at P < 0.01**; Temp × depth (linear) = **112.67, P < 0.01**; depth (linear) = 352.67,
P < 0.01. Degrees of freedom: temperature 1, pooled error 18 (error MS 12.33).

Narrative statements from the paper (verbatim):

> "Furthermore, bloater damage was less at 27 °C than at 32 °C for both controlled and natural fermentations."
> "At 27 °C the average increase in percent total bloaters is 5 % for each 9-in. increase in depth; at 32 °C this
> increase averages 18 % (Table 4)."
> "At both temperatures, concentration of CO2 reached a maximum at the greatest brine depths after about 6 days,
> and the values were similar, about 66 mg/100 ml brine. Over the next 6 days, however, the CO2 concentration
> remained higher at 27 °C than at 32 °C."
> "Optimum temperature for growth of the culture might contribute to bloater formation. Also, higher temperatures
> reduce the solubility of CO2 (Quinn and Jones, 1936); and, according to Boyle's gas law, gaseous CO2 would
> either occupy more space and/or create a greater pressure at higher temperatures. Thus, CO2 present inside the
> cucumber might be forced from solution, increase gas pressure and cause a bloated area."

**Fermentation speed statement at 32 °C (verbatim, p. 571):**

> "In controlled fermentations of brined cucumbers incubated at 32 °C and 25° salometer, fermentable sugars were
> **rapidly and completely converted to acid, usually within 7–10 days** (Fig. 1 and 2). ... After completion of
> fermentation, the pH was 3.3–3.4 ... Criteria used to determine completion of fermentation were: absence of
> reducing sugars and of changes in titratable acidity and pH over a 2-day interval between analyses."
> "In natural fermentations, times required for conversion of sugars to acid were longer and unpredictable in
> comparison with controlled fermentations."

**Take-away for the temperature question:** in this system the *endpoint acidity at 10–12 days was almost
identical at 27 and 32 °C (1.30 vs 1.32 %; 1.22 vs 1.25 %)*, while **bloater incidence roughly doubled at the
higher temperature** (24.5 % → 48.0 % at full depth) and the temperature effect was statistically significant.

### A.6 Extension / home-preservation guidance (days vs. temperature) — usable but non-primary

**National Center for Home Food Preservation (Univ. of Georgia), "Dill Pickles"** — **CONFIDENCE: moderate**
(authoritative extension consensus, adapted from USDA *Complete Guide to Home Canning*, AIB-539, rev. 2015;

Verbatim: "Store where temperature is between **70 ºF and 75 ºF for about 3 to 4 weeks** while fermenting.

**NC State Extension, "How to Make Fermented Pickles"** (Candice Christian, May 2020, rev. Dec 2024) —
**CONFIDENCE: moderate** (mirrors the NCHFP text; cites NCHFP 2018 as its source).

Working URL: https://foodsafety.ces.ncsu.edu/news/how-to-make-fermented-pickles/

Identical numbers: 70–75 °F ≈ 3–4 weeks; 55–65 °F ≈ 5–6 weeks; avoid >80 °F.

**Rough ratio for extension guidance (my arithmetic):** 5–6 weeks at 55–65 °F vs 3–4 weeks at 70–75 °F
≈ **1.5–1.8× longer** for roughly a 10–15 °F (≈6–8 °C) reduction. This is a *practitioner rule*, not
experimental data.

### A.8 What I could NOT find for cucumbers (stated plainly)

1. **No retrievable source documents the specific "controlled ~18–21 °C vs ambient 24–27 °C" contrast**
   described in the brief. The USDA/NC State temperature-gradient work I could actually read used **21 / 27 /
   32 °C** (Etchells et al. 1964) and **27 vs 32 °C** (Etchells et al. 1975). Treat the 18–21 vs 24–27 framing as
   unverified.
2. **No source retrieved gives a table of "days to reach 0.4 %, 0.6 %, 0.8 %, 1.0 % titratable acidity at each
   temperature"** for brined cucumbers. The nearest quantitative anchors are: complete sugar→acid conversion in
   **7–10 days at 32 °C, 25° salometer** (Etchells et al. 1975, p. 571), endpoint acidity **1.30–1.32 % at
   10 days** and **1.14–1.32 % at 12 days** (27–32 °C range), and the **"≈1 extra day per 5 °C cooling step"**
   rate rule (Etchells et al. 1964, p. 528).
3. **No primary data retrieved on "low-temperature brining at ≤10 °C"** for cucumbers. I searched USDA-ARS
   "Pickle Pubs" (300 reprints downloaded; see below), Europe PMC, Crossref, AGRIS and the open web. The
   11 °C-vs-23 °C *Molecules* 2021 study is a modern model system, not classic low-temperature brining.
4. **Etchells & Jones 1940s–1950s Food Technology / Applied Microbiology papers:** the full set of **abstracts**

## PART B — PEPPER MASH / CHILLI MASH FERMENTATION

### B.1 The best controlled-temperature comparison I could retrieve

**Alberto, M.R., Perera, M.F. and Arena, M.E. (2013). "Lactic Acid Fermentation of Peppers." *Food and Nutrition
Sciences* 4(11A): 47–55.** — **CONFIDENCE: STRONG** (open-access full text read, tables read).

Working URLs: DOI https://doi.org/10.4236/fns.2013.411A007 · free full PDF:
https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/bitstream/handle/11336/6886/CONICET_Digital_Nro.9067_A.pdf?sequence=2&isAllowed=y ·
record: https://ri.conicet.gov.ar/handle/11336/6886

System: *Capsicum annuum* var. *grossum* peppers, heat-treated, **4 % NaCl**, 2 g/L or 20 g/L glucose,
inoculated singly and in combination with *Lactobacillus plantarum* N8, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* L and
*Pediococcus pentosaceus* 12p, vs. spontaneous. Incubated at **22 °C and 30 °C**. Sampled at 0, 1, 2, 5, 10,
20, 30 days.

Verbatim results (Section 3.4, pH Variations):

> "From an initial pH of 5.0, reduction in pH in the spontaneous fermentation was slower than in inoculated
> samples and the final pH was higher at both temperatures."
> "**At 30 °C in media with 2 g/l glucose, after one day incubation in the inoculated samples, the pH decreased
> nearly 1.5 units**, with the exception the sample inoculated with *Lactobacillus plantarum*, in this case the
> pH values diminished 1.0 unit. In NF [natural fermentation] the pH diminution was 0.4 units. **At 22 °C in all
> the cases the diminution was lower than at 30 °C, and less than a unit.**"
> "**At 30 °C, after 2 days of incubation average pH was 3.5. At room temperature (22 °C), the same value was
> reached after 5 days.** ... **Final pH at 22 °C was reached between 10 and 20 days.**"

Also from the abstract: "Sugar consumption, pH reduction and acid production were higher at 30 °C than at 22 °C.
... Maximum growth was reached between 2 and 5 days of fermentation in all the samples assayed. After 30 days of
incubation in presence of 2 % glucose the survival of LAB was nearly 5 log cfu/ml [and] was higher at the lower
temperature assayed."

**Temperature-effect summary (their data):** reaching pH 3.5 took **2 days at 30 °C vs 5 days at 22 °C** —
a factor of **~2.5× faster** for an 8 °C increase (≈Q10 ≈ 3 over that interval, if you assume a simple
exponential; *that Q10 is my calculation, not the authors'*). One-day pH drop: **~1.0–1.5 units at 30 °C vs
<1.0 unit at 22 °C.**

Reported organic-acid endpoints after 30 days (Tables 4 and 5, g/L): with 2 g/L glucose the natural fermentation
reached **0.61 g/L lactic acid at 22 °C vs 0.88 g/L at 30 °C**; mixed *Lc. mesenteroides* + *P. pentosaceus*
reached **1.18 g/L at 22 °C vs 2.38 g/L at 30 °C** (table values, 2 g/L glucose series). Final pH < 3.0 in all
cases with 2 % glucose.

**Caveat to carry:** this is a *model* system (4 % NaCl, added glucose), not a 8–15 % salt Tabasco-style mash.

### B.2 Pepper mash is, in practice, a very slow (months-to-years) fermentation

  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2009.01.019 (Unpaywall says **not** open access).
- **Aryee et al. (2022), *Food Chem. Adv.* 1: 100137** — via Torán-Pereg et al.: habanero (*C. chinense*)

## PART D — GAPS, EXPLICITLY

1. **Cucumber: no "days to 0.4 / 0.6 / 0.8 / 1.0 % titratable acidity at temperature X" table was found.** The
   classic USDA rate data are in figures, not tables. Digitising Figs. 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of the free Etchells
   et al. 1964 PDF (https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC1058172?pdf=render) and Figs. 1–2 of Etchells et al. 1975
   (http://lib3.dss.go.th/fulltext/scan_ebook/j.food_sci_1975_v40_n3.pdf) is the concrete next step and would
   produce exactly the requested numbers.
2. **Cucumber: the "18–21 °C controlled vs 24–27 °C ambient" contrast in the brief is not supported by anything
   I could read.** The documented USDA contrasts are 21/27/32 °C and 27/32 °C.
3. **Cucumber: no primary low-temperature (≤10 °C) brining rate data found.**
4. **Pepper: no controlled-temperature pepper *mash* study (high salt, months) was retrievable.** The one solid
   controlled-temperature pepper fermentation I found (Alberto et al. 2013) is a 4 %-salt model system.
5. **Pepper: the "1 month high-temperature vs 1 year ambient" statement and any Q10/Arrhenius value are
   unverified.** They may exist in the LSU theses (repository.lsu.edu — DNS-blocked here), the McGill thesis
   "Fermentation of the hot red pepper (*Capsicum frutescens*)" (escholarship.mcgill.ca — download blocked
   here), or in 1950s–60s *Food Research* / *Applied Microbiology* volumes that are not in the scanned library
   I could reach.
6. **Environment limitations encountered:** `repository.lsu.edu`, `ia-pub-fts-api.archive.org` and
   `escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/*` did not resolve or returned error pages from this sandbox; Wiley
   (`ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com`) returned HTTP 403 (Cloudflare); OpenAlex and Google Books APIs were
   rate-limited/quota-exceeded; no OCR binary (tesseract) is installed, so the ~290 image-only USDA-ARS reprint
