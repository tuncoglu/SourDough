# Salt Type Effects on Lactic Acid Fermentation of Vegetables — Literature-Evidence Review

Compiled for a food-science model. Every claim below carries a source with a URL and an explicit
confidence label (**strong / moderate / weak-or-contested**). Where nothing real exists, this file
says "no experimental evidence found" rather than repeating folklore.

Scope: sauerkraut, kimchi, cucumber pickles, pepper mash (and closely adjacent evidence from
pickled vegetables generally, plus dairy where the vegetable literature is silent).

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — the six things that matter most

1. **"Never use iodized salt for fermenting" is folklore.** No controlled study shows iodized salt
   inhibits LAB or delays acidification in any vegetable fermentation. Four sauerkraut studies
   (1955, 1958, 1998, 2018), two pickle studies (1996, 2004), one 16S-sequenced cucumber study
   (2020) and ~100 years of dairy work (1926–2022) found no such effect. The authoritative US source
   (USDA/NCHFP, 2015) explicitly permits iodized salt. Confidence: **strong**.
2. **US and EU iodized salts are chemically different and must not be conflated.** US "iodized salt"
   is legally **potassium or cuprous iodide (I⁻)**, ~45 mg I/kg measured. German/EU Jodsalz is
   **potassium iodate (KIO₃)**, ~15–25 mg I/kg. Iodide cannot oxidise anything; iodate is a strong
   oxidant. **Conflating them is the single biggest error in this literature.** Confidence: **strong**.
3. **The one real iodine effect is cosmetic, iodate-specific, and about pickles:** KIO₃ at 40 mg I/kg
   significantly darkened **and** softened brined pickles (Amr & Jabay 2004), while KI at the same
   dose did not. Taste was unaffected. Confidence: **moderate**.
4. **If iodine does anything microbiologically at brine-realistic doses, it hits YEASTS, not LAB.**
   Two independent hints: a significant yeast reduction in iodized olive brine (Lanza 2020) and a
   p = 0.06 yeast/mould effect in spontaneous sauerkraut (Müller 2018). Confidence: **moderate**.
5. **The rock-solid density numbers:** Diamond Crystal kosher **2.8 g/tsp**, Morton kosher
   **4.8 g/tsp**, table salt **6.0 g/tsp**, and — counter-intuitively — **Morton Canning & Pickling
   Salt is also 6.0 g/tsp**, identical to table salt. The "1.4 g per ¼ tsp Diamond Crystal" variant is
   a 2× transcription error. Confidence: **strong**.
6. **Unrefined salt really does change kimchi — but via Mg and Ca, not K, and not the rate.**
   ICP-MS: Mg 262 mg/kg (purified) vs 5,150–12,545 mg/kg (solar); K is actually *highest* in purified
   salt. Mineral-rich sea salt increased *Leuconostoc* and decreased the *Lactobacillus*/*Leuconostoc*
   ratio (Kim 2017) and shifted solar-salt kimchi toward *Leuconostoc*/*Weissella* vs *L. sakei* with
   purified salt (Lee 2022). No rate effect was found. Confidence: **moderate-to-strong**.

# PART A — IODIZED SALT

## A1. What is actually in "iodized salt"

### Finding A1.1 — The citation "21 CFR 137.150" is wrong; the correct rule is 21 CFR 100.155

**Claim.** There is no 21 CFR 137.150 for salt. 21 CFR Part 137 is *Cereal flours and related
products*. The rule that names and defines "iodized salt" is **21 CFR 100.155, "Salt and iodized
salt"** (Source: 42 FR 14306, Mar. 15, 1977; amended 48 FR 10811, Mar. 15, 1983 and 49 FR 24119,
June 12, 1984).

The regulation states: *"the term iodized salt or iodized table salt is designated as the name of
salt for human food use to which **iodide** has been added in the form of **cuprous iodide or
potassium iodide** permitted by §§ 184.1265 and 184.1634 of this chapter."*

- Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2002-title21-vol2/html/CFR-2002-title21-vol2.htm (see § 100.155)
- Confidence: **strong** (primary law; verified against the GPO CFR text).

**Why this matters:** US "iodized salt" is legally **iodide (I⁻)**, not **iodate (IO₃⁻)**. Iodide is
the fully reduced form and is not an oxidant. This single fact undercuts most of the folk reasoning
about iodized salt.

### Finding A1.2 — Exact US permitted levels and the correct ppm conversion

| Additive | CFR cite | Max level in table salt | Iodine delivered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potassium iodide (KI) | 21 CFR 184.1634(d) | 0.01 % (= 100 mg KI/kg) | 76.5 mg I/kg (max) |
| Cuprous iodide (CuI) | 21 CFR 184.1265(c) | 0.01 % (= 100 mg CuI/kg) | 66.6 mg I/kg (max) |
| Potassium iodate (KIO₃) | 21 CFR 184.1635(d) | **Not permitted in table salt.** GRAS only as a *dough strengthener* in bread, ≤0.0075 % of flour weight | — |
| Potassium iodide (as a food additive generally) | 21 CFR 172.375 | not a salt concentration limit; caps *total dietary iodine* (225 µg/day adults, etc.) | — |

- Sources: https://ecfr.io/Title-21/Section-184.1634 · https://ecfr.io/Title-21/Section-184.1265 · https://ecfr.io/Title-21/Section-184.1635 · https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/172.375
- Confidence: **strong** (primary law).

**Correction to the brief's premise.** The brief said "0.006–0.01 % ⇒ ~15–45 mg/kg as I". That is
wrong at the low end. Because KI is 76.4 % iodine by mass (126.9/166.0):
- 0.006 % KI = 60 mg KI/kg = **45.9 mg I/kg**
- 0.01 % KI = 100 mg KI/kg = **76.5 mg I/kg**
So the US legal band is roughly **46–76 mg I/kg**, and the industry label value is 45 mg I/kg — not
15–45 mg/kg.

### Finding A1.3 — Measured iodine in real US iodized salt (this is the key number)

**Dasgupta PK, Liu Y, Dyke JV. "Iodine nutrition: iodine content of iodized salt in the United
States." *Environmental Science & Technology* 2008;42(4):1315–1323.** doi:10.1021/es0719071

Numbers:
- USFDA recommendation: **60–100 mg KI/kg salt = 46–76 mg I/kg salt**.
- All US iodized salt is **labelled 45 mg I/kg**.
- Measured in 88 freshly opened containers (ICP-MS): **median 44.1 mg I/kg; mean 47.5 ± 18.5 mg/kg;
  range 12.7–129 mg I/kg**.
- **47 of 88 samples fell below** the FDA-recommended level; **6 exceeded** it.
- **Within a single can**, iodine varied by **1.2× to 3.3×** between depths sampled
  (CV 8.3 %–49.3 %) — the iodine is *not* homogeneously distributed.
- Iodine is **significantly lost on high-humidity storage**; light or dry heat has little effect.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18351111/ (abstract retrieved via Europe PMC)
Confidence: **strong** (direct measurement, n=88).

**Practical implication for a fermentation model:** the iodine dose delivered by a scoop of US
iodized salt is both lower than the legal maximum and *variable by up to ~3× within one can*. Any
"iodine effect" would have to be robust across ~13–130 mg I/kg to be detectable in home practice.

### Finding A1.4 — EU / German iodized salt: different form, about half the dose

- **Germany:** Jodsalz contains **15–25 mg iodine (as iodate) per kg salt**. In 2022 the German
  Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) and the German Society for Endocrinology proposed
  raising this to **30 mg/kg**. German law: *Verordnung zur Änderung der Vorschriften über jodiertes
  Speisesalz*, 19 June 1989 (BGBl. I S. 1123).
- **Switzerland:** iodized salt uses **iodide**, not iodate.
- **Austria:** mandatory 15–20 mg potassium iodide **or** iodate per kg salt (1999 amendment to the
  Speisesalzgesetz).
- **WHO definition of "adequately iodized salt": 15–40 ppm iodine.**

Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodiertes_Salz · https://apps.who.int/nutrition/landscape/help.aspx?menu=0&helpid=377
Confidence: **moderate** for the German/Austrian figures (encyclopedic + national law citation, not
read in the original Bundesgesetzblatt); **strong** for the WHO 15–40 ppm definition.

**So:** German/EU iodized salt is typically **KIO₃ at ~15–25 mg I/kg** — roughly **half** the US
iodine level but in the **oxidising** form. US salt is **KI at ~45 mg I/kg** in the **non-oxidising**
form. These are not equivalent inputs to a model.

### Finding A1.5 — Iodate vs iodide: the chemistry

Iodate (IO₃⁻) is the **oxidised** form of iodine and is a genuine oxidant — it is the standard
oxidant used in iodometric titration. Iodide (I⁻) is the **fully reduced** form and cannot act as an
oxidant. Directly relevant food consequence: Amr & Jabay observed that iodate-treated brines lost
~50–60 % of their iodine over 12 days (4 mg/L → 1.6–2.1 mg/L), which is consistent with iodate being
consumed by reductants in the vegetable matrix (ascorbate, phenolics) before it ever reaches
bacteria.

- Sources: https://www.wlfpublisher.com/admin_1992/pdf/articles/2004_issue2_f27.pdf (Amr & Jabay 2004, Figures 5–6 and text) · https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodiertes_Salz
- Confidence: **strong** for "iodate is the oxidant, iodide is not" (definitional); **moderate** for
  the "quenched by vegetable reductants" inference (it is the reviewers' reading of their data, not
  a measurement the authors made).

Standard reduction potentials (**chemistry strong; sourcing moderate** — taken from tabulated
textbook values cross-checked on the web, not read from a primary CRC Handbook entry in this review):

| Couple | E° |
|---|---|
| IO₃⁻ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → I⁻ + 3H₂O | **+1.085 V** |
| 2IO₃⁻ + 12H⁺ + 10e⁻ → I₂ + 6H₂O | **+1.195 V** |
| I₂ + 2e⁻ → 2I⁻ | +0.5355 V |
| *(contrast)* Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ | +0.771 V |
| *(contrast)* O₂/H₂O | +1.229 V |

So iodate is a genuinely strong oxidant, comparable to oxygen; iodide is its fully reduced form and
cannot oxidise anything. **KI-iodized salt and KIO₃-iodized salt are not interchangeable inputs** —
which is exactly what Amr & Jabay observed empirically (Finding A2.4).

**Independent observational support that iodate is chemically consumed in a real brine:**
Lanza et al. 2020 found that adding KIO₃ to an olive brine significantly *lowered* Mg and Fe in the
olive flesh, attributed to formation of Mg(IO₃)₂ and Fe(IO₃)₃ — i.e. the iodate anion reacted with
matrix cations rather than persisting as free IO₃⁻.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7142713/
Confidence: **moderate**.

**Order-of-magnitude estimate (an estimate, not a cited finding):** at 45 mg I/kg salt in a 5 % brine
the total iodine is ≈ **2.25 mg/kg brine ≈ 17.7 µM** iodate; at Lanza's conditions (37 mg/kg, 8 %
brine) ≈ 23 µM. Fresh cabbage carries ascorbate at roughly **1.7–2.8 mM** (300–500 mg/kg) plus
glutathione and polyphenols — a reductant pool ~2 orders of magnitude larger. Iodate should therefore
be titrated out by the vegetable matrix long before it could exert a dose-dependent antimicrobial
effect, *provided* the reaction is fast on a fermentation timescale. That kinetic proviso was **not**
verified in this review (the ascorbate–iodate rate-law paper, Samios & Karayannis,
*Z. Phys. Chem.* 1977;104(4–6):189, doi:10.1524/zpch.1977.104.4-6.189, could not be opened).
Confidence: **weak** — label as a plausible mechanism only.

## A2. Does iodized salt actually inhibit LAB or delay acidification?

### Finding A2.1 — DIRECT SAUERKRAUT EVIDENCE (the decisive modern study)

**Müller A, Rösch N, Cho G-S, Meinhardt A-K, Kabisch J, Habermann D, Böhnlein C, Brinks E, Greiner R,
Franz CMAP. "Influence of iodized table salt on fermentation characteristics and bacterial diversity
during sauerkraut fermentation." *Food Microbiology* 2018;76:473–480.** doi:10.1016/j.fm.2018.07.009
(PMID 30166176)

Design: 1.0 % table salt, iodized vs non-iodized; with *Lactobacillus plantarum* + *Leuconostoc
mesenteroides* starters (≈1 × 10⁷ cfu/mL) vs spontaneous; metagenomic (16S) community analysis.

Key numbers:
- With starters: LAB reached **1 × 10⁹ cfu/mL within 24 h**, pH dropped **below 4.0 within 24 h**.
- Without starters: LAB rose more slowly from **1 × 10⁵ to 1 × 10⁹ cfu/mL**, and pH fell below 4.0
  only **after 3 days**.
- Community: spontaneous fermentations were more diverse (enterobacteria and pseudomonads in the
  first days, lactococci later). With starters, lactobacilli predominated; leuconostocs occurred at
  much lower sequence abundance.
- **"The use of iodized salt did not statistically significantly influence microbial populations in
  the fermentation."**
- **"Thus, there is no basis for the popular held belief that the use of iodized salt inhibits the
  growth of the bacteria important for the sauerkraut fermentation."**
- Fermentation did **not** change the iodine concentration.
- One caveat the authors raise themselves: a **near-significant effect (p = 0.06) on yeasts and
  moulds** in the *unstartered* fermentations. Since commercial/home sauerkraut is usually
  spontaneous, they say this should be further investigated.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30166176/ · https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fm.2018.07.009 · https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df6b926eef00c2cea2bf26
Confidence: **strong** — this is the single best-controlled, most directly on-point experiment found.

### Finding A2.1b — DIRECT CUCUMBER PICKLE EVIDENCE, with 16S community analysis

**Stoll DA, Müller A, Meinhardt A-K, Dötsch A, Greiner R, Kulling SE, Huch M. "Influence of salt
concentration and iodized table salt on the microbiota of fermented cucumbers." *Food Microbiology*
2020;92:103552.** doi:10.1016/j.fm.2020.103552 (PMID 32950146; Max Rubner-Institut, Germany)

Design: cucumber cv. Potomac, **5 % brine with and without iodine**, **8 weeks at 20 °C**, sampled
every 2 weeks; culture-dependent plating plus 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing.

Result, verbatim from the abstract: *"the fermentation process of cucumbers and the involved
microbiota is influenced by the **concentration** of table salt and **not** by the use of iodized
table salt. Therefore, we conclude that the use of iodized table salt **does not negatively affect
the fermentation process**."* pH fell significantly during the first two weeks **regardless of iodine
content**; the microbiota was fully developed within 2 weeks. Iodine permeated the cucumbers by
diffusion, reaching satisfactory concentrations in the final product.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32950146/
Confidence: **strong** (controlled, culture + sequencing, peer-reviewed).

### Finding A2.2 — DIRECT SAUERKRAUT EVIDENCE, with a full acidification curve (1958)

**El-Wakeil FAE-S. "Effects of iodized salt and other iodine compounds on the quality of processed
vegetables." PhD dissertation, The Ohio State University, 1958** (advisor W. A. Gould). OhioLINK ETD,
accession osu148656241580449.

Design: 6 lb shredded cabbage (cv. Danish Ballhead) + **68 g salt = 2.5 % salt** in 40 glazed
porcelain crocks; Morton **iodized** salt (stated as "maximum 112 ppm KI" ≈ **86 mg I/kg**) vs
ordinary non-iodized salt; plus **added KI at 1.0, 1.5, 10, 25, 100 and 200 ppm** in the cabbage;
plus an iodine–iodide mixture and the iodophor "Iosan" at 1–200 ppm; plus sodium thiosulfate at 50
and 100 ppm. Titratable acidity measured weekly for 5 weeks, expressed as % lactic acid.

**Table 50 — % lactic acid by week (iodized vs non-iodized salt, same batch):**

| Treatment | wk 1 | wk 2 | wk 3 | wk 4 | wk 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iodized salt (Morton) | 1.16 | 1.54 | 1.88 | 1.93 | **1.94** |
| Ordinary non-iodized salt | 0.90 | 1.63 | 1.89 | 1.95 | **1.96** |

Final acidity differs by **0.02 percentage points**. If anything the iodized batch fermented *faster*
in week 1 (1.16 vs 0.90).

**KI spiked directly into the cabbage, up to 200 ppm — still no effect.** Highest KI dose (200 ppm):
1.03 / 1.71 / 1.84 / 1.91 / 1.94 % lactic acid (pure thiosulfate series) vs 1.96 % for the
non-iodized control. The author's conclusion: *"potassium iodide, iodine-iodide mixture (Battelle) or
'Iosan' in the concentrations applied did not affect either the fermentation process or the final
total acidity produced after 5 weeks of fermentation."* And: *"there were no significant differences
during the fermentation of cabbage treated with either ordinary non-iodized or (Morton) iodized
salts."* The **thiosulfate stabiliser** (50 and 100 ppm) also had no effect.

Source: https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/etd/r/1501/10?clear=10&p10_accession_num=osu148656241580449 (full text PDF; Tables 50–52, pp. 148–151)
Confidence: **strong** for the numbers as recorded; **moderate** on study quality (1958, single lab,
duplicate titrations only, no modern statistics). Note the iodine dose here (~86 mg I/kg in the salt)
is **above** today's US level, which strengthens the negative result.

### Finding A2.3 — Earlier sauerkraut work reaching the same conclusion

**Kojima N, Brown HD. "The effects of iodized salt in processed fruits and vegetables." *Food
Technology* 1955;9:103–107.**
Reported (via the systematic review in A2.6, and as summarised inside El-Wakeil's 1958 thesis):
iodized salt **and** iodates in bulk and canned sauerkraut **did not affect the different stages of
the fermentation process or the final total acidity**; no effect on taste, colour, texture or odour
after 32 days' fermentation. Also no effects in tomato juice, green beans, olives and sweet corn at
**120 ppm** iodine in salt — and in one series at **1200 ppm**.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6098777/ (Table 1) · https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/etd/r/1501/10?clear=10&p10_accession_num=osu148656241580449
Confidence: **moderate** (original 1955 paper not read; two independent secondary accounts agree).

**Doman M, Korsak D, Lekowska-Kochaniak A, Popowski J. "New possibilities of the utilization of the
iodinated salt in the fermentation process" / effect of iodinated salt on the cabbage fermentation
process." *Polish Journal of Food and Nutrition Sciences* 1998/1999;8/49(2):201–208.** (Also indexed
as *Przemysl Fermentacyjny i Owocowo-Warzywny* 42(5):23–25, 1998.) Studied KI at concentrations
comparable to iodized salt in cabbage fermentation and its effect on *Lactobacillus plantarum*,
*L. brevis*, *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* and *Saccharomyces*. Summarised in the 2018 systematic review
as KI **6 ppm**, with **no effect on quality after 90 days' storage and no effect on lactic acid
production or microflora composition**.
Sources: https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/64723073e17b74d2224e7585 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6098777/ (Table 1)
Confidence: **moderate** (Polish-language; accessed only via AGRIS abstract + review table).

### Finding A2.4 — DIRECT PICKLE EVIDENCE (the one real positive effect — and it is iodate, not iodide)

**Amr AS, Jabay OA. "Effect of salt iodization on the quality of pickled vegetables." *Journal of
Food, Agriculture & Environment* 2004;2(2):151–156.** University of Jordan.

Design: cucumbers, turnips, carrots, cauliflower and olives pickled in **100 g/L (10 %) brines** made
from crude and refined Dead Sea salt and wet-mined salt, each with and without **40 mg/kg iodine as
either KIO₃ or KI**; 12 days at room temperature; 10 trained panellists; Duncan's Multiple Range Test.

Results with numbers:
- **Potassium iodate caused significant (P<0.05) darkening/discolouration** of most pickled
  vegetables regardless of salt source. Example colour scores (10-point hedonic), crude Dead Sea
  salt, cucumber: **control 6.9ᵃ, KI 7.0ᵃ, KIO₃ 5.4ᵇ**. Wet-mined crude salt, cucumber:
  **control 6.8ᵇ, KI 8.0ᵃ, KIO₃ 4.2ᶜ**. Cauliflower (Dead Sea crude): **7.1ᵃ / 7.2ᵃ / 4.1ᵇ**.
- **Potassium iodate caused significant (P<0.05) softening.** Texture scores, cucumber (Dead Sea
  crude): **7.2ᵃᵇ / KI 7.9ᵃ / KIO₃ 6.8ᵇ**. The softening was **less pronounced with the high-calcium
  refined wet-mined salt**.
- **Potassium iodide produced none of these effects** — KI scores were statistically
  indistinguishable from controls, and often nominally better.
- **Taste: no significant effect of iodization with either form** (P<0.05).
- **Salt penetration rate: no significant effect** of iodine form or salt source; pickles and brines
  reached equilibrium at **4–5 % salt after ~12 days** in all treatments.
- **Iodine mass balance:** brine iodine fell from **~4 mg/L at day 0 to 1.6–2.1 mg/L at day 12**;
  pickle iodine reached **1.6–1.8 mg/kg** (higher in cucumbers and turnips — thin skins — than in
  carrots and cauliflower).
- **Vitamin C and vitamin A: no effect of iodization** (pickling itself destroyed 62.5 % of vitamin C
  in cucumbers to 93.5 % in turnips).
- The authors hypothesise the softening arises from **oxidation of pectin methyl groups by iodate,
  with accompanying CO₂ production** ("more gas production").

Source: https://www.wlfpublisher.com/admin_1992/pdf/articles/2004_issue2_f27.pdf
Confidence: **strong** for this experiment (controlled, replicated, statistically analysed);
**moderate** for generalisation (10 % brine, only 12 days, no pH/LAB counts measured, and 40 mg I/kg
is at/above the top of the US range while being typical of warm-climate KIO₃ programmes).

**Critical reading:** this is a **colour and texture** effect caused by **iodate**, in **pickles** —
not an inhibition of lactic acid bacteria, and not something iodide does.

### Finding A2.5 — Second direct pickle study: no effect at all (with KI)

**Badran O, Qaraqash W, Gamah S. "Possible effects of iodized salt on the taste, colour and
consistency of traditional pickles." *Eastern Mediterranean Health Journal* 1996;2(2):219–223.**
(WHO EMRO; funded by WHO)

Design: randomised **crossover** — 20 women, each preparing two identical batches with salt A and
salt B (one iodized, one not; allocation blinded to the investigators); **80 jars total** (40
cucumber, 40 mixed vegetables); 2 months' storage; 30 judges (15 per pickle type, incl. 5 food
technologists); paired Student *t*-test.

Result: **no statistically significant difference** (α = 0.05) between iodized and non-iodized salt
for **taste, colour, consistency or overall acceptability**, for either food technologists or
untrained panellists, in either cucumbers or mixed vegetables.
Source: https://www.emro.who.int/emhj-volume-2-1996/volume-2-issue-2/article5.html
Confidence: **strong** for the sensory conclusion (good design, adequate n, crossover).

### Finding A2.6 — Systematic review of the whole question

**Blankenship JL, Garrett GS, Khan NA, De-Regil LM, Spohrer R, Gorstein J. "Effect of iodized salt on
organoleptic properties of processed foods: a systematic review." *Journal of Food Science and
Technology* 2018;55(9):3341–3352.** doi:10.1007/s13197-018-3277-9 · PMC6098777

**34 studies, 38 processed foods.** Conclusion: *"There is no evidence that the use of iodized salt in
production of processed foods or condiments causes adverse organoleptic changes that will affect
consumer acceptability or product quality."*

Its own extracted table nonetheless records, for pickled vegetables, exactly the Amr & Jabay result:
"KI and KIO₃, 40 ppm … No objective effect on sensory properties with KI. **KIO₃ had no effect on
taste but darkening and softening of the pickles.** No effect on vitamin C or A content for either KI
or KIO₃." It also records **Mencinicopschi et al. 2004** (KIO₃ at 37 and 56 ppm; cucumber, green
tomatoes, marinated sweet peppers): no effect on colour, texture, taste or smell except a slight
taste change in cucumber, and **no effect on vitamin C or lactic acid**; and **CNSIC 2004** (Sichuan
pickles, 10.5–56 ppm): no effect on taste, texture, fragrance, colour or quality after 3 months.
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6098777/
Confidence: **strong** as a survey of the literature; note the internal tension — the review's
abstract says "no evidence of adverse change" while its own data table records iodate-induced
darkening and softening. Read the table, not just the abstract.

### Finding A2.4b — ONE real microbial effect, and it is on YEASTS not LAB (olive brine)

**Lanza B, Di Marco S, Simone N, Di Marco C, Gabriele F. "Table Olives Fermented in Iodized Sea
Salt Brines: Nutraceutical/Sensory Properties and Microbial Biodiversity." *Foods* 2020;9(3):301.**
doi:10.3390/foods9030301 (PMC7142713)

Design: naturally fermented table olives (cvs. Carolea, Leucocarpa) in **8 % brines** made with PGI
"Sale marino di Trapani" sea salt vs the same salt **+ 0.006 % KIO₃ (= 37 mg I/kg salt)**; **8 months**;
plate counts (PCA total microflora, MEA yeasts, MRS anaerobic LAB) plus mineral, colour and sensory
analysis.

Results:
- Iodine **significantly reduced the microbial load**, and that load was **"represented only by
  yeasts"** in both cultivars, with *Candida* the dominant genus.
- Iodine in the flesh reached **109 µg/100 g** (Carolea) and **38 µg/100 g** (Leucocarpa).
- **Sensory and colour were not significantly influenced** by iodized brining (only Carolea differed
  in the b* parameter and hence chroma C).
- KIO₃ addition significantly **lowered Mg and Fe** in the flesh, attributed to Mg(IO₃)₂ and
  Fe(IO₃)₃ formation.

Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7142713/
Confidence: **moderate**.

**Read this carefully — it is easy to over-claim.** The recoverable microflora in these brines was
**yeasts only, in both the iodized and the non-iodized arms**; no LAB population was recovered to be
inhibited. So this study does **not** show iodine suppressing LAB. It shows iodine suppressing
**yeasts**. That direction is independently echoed by Müller et al. 2018's near-significant
(p = 0.06) effect of iodine on **yeasts and moulds** in spontaneous sauerkraut. **Two independent
hints that if iodine does anything microbiologically at brine-realistic doses, it hits yeasts more
than LAB.** Confidence: **moderate**.

### Finding A2.6b — A mis-citation to avoid

**Viander B, Mäki M, Palva A. "Impact of weak acid salts and salt on the growth of lactic acid
bacteria." *Food Microbiology* 2003;20(4):391–395.** doi:10.1016/S0740-0020(02)00150-8 — frequently
cited in "salt quality" discussions, but it **does not test iodized salt**. Its "different salt
quality" arm is **mineral salt (28 % KCl, 57 % NaCl)**, i.e. a sodium-reduction study: LAB reached
10⁸ cfu/mL on day 3 (1.2 % NaCl) vs day 4 (0.5 % NaCl) vs **day 6** (0.5 % mineral salt), with lower
lactic acid at 2 weeks for mineral salt. **Citing Viander for iodized salt is an error.** (Relevant
instead to Part B5 on KCl.)
Confidence: **moderate**.

### Finding A2.8 — MIC of iodine/iodate against LAB: NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND

Searched specifically for minimum inhibitory concentrations of iodine, iodide, iodate or iodophor
against *Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, *Pediococcus* and *Lactococcus*.

**No experimental evidence found.** No MIC values were located. This is a genuine gap: the
"iodine kills the bacteria" claim has no dose–response anchor in the LAB literature.

What does exist nearby:
- **Iodate reduction by marine aerobic bacteria** (Yamamura S. et al.) — bacteria exist that reduce
  iodate, i.e. detoxify it; evidence that microbial iodate reduction is a real phenomenon.
  https://search.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-39360326
- **Iodophors** (e.g. "Iosan", used in El-Wakeil 1958) are commercial sanitising agents — but
  sanitising concentrations are orders of magnitude above 15–76 mg/kg, and El-Wakeil tested Iosan at
  1–100 ppm in kraut with **no effect on fermentation**. The gap between "iodophor sanitiser" and
  "45 mg I/kg in salt" is the whole argument.
Confidence: **the absence of evidence is itself a confident finding** — stated as such.

### Finding A2.9 — Kimchi and pepper mash: NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND

Searched for iodized vs non-iodized salt in **kimchi** and in **pepper mash / chilli mash**.
**No experimental evidence found** for either. All direct vegetable-fermentation evidence located is
for **sauerkraut** (1955, 1958, 1998, 2018) and for **brined mixed pickles** (1996, 2004). Kimchi
literature is extensive but appears to address salt *concentration*, salt *type* (see Part B) and

## A3. Dextrose and anti-caking agents in US iodized salt

### Finding A3.1 — What is actually on the label

Product-label ingredient lists (Open Food Facts, transcribed from retail packaging):

| Product | Ingredients, in label order |
|---|---|
| Morton Iodized Salt | **SALT, CALCIUM SILICATE (an anticaking agent), DEXTROSE, POTASSIUM IODIDE** |
| Morton Iodized Salt (alt. listing) | Salt, calcium silicate (an anti-caking agent), dextrose, potassium iodide |
| Morton Salt Lite | Salt, potassium chloride, calcium silicate, magnesium carbonate, dextrose, potassium iodide |
| Morton Canning & Pickling Salt | **SALT** (nothing else) |

Confidence: **strong** for composition (crowd-sourced but transcribed from actual US labels, and
consistent across multiple entries and retailers).

### Finding A3.1b — Morton's own statement of what the dextrose is for

Morton's archived consumer FAQ states: *"Morton® Iodized Salt contains potassium iodide, **dextrose to
stabilize the iodide** and calcium silicate which is an anti-caking agent."*
Source: https://web.archive.org/web/20081225045630/http://www.mortonsalt.com/faqs/food_salt_faq.html
(archived Morton FAQ); ingredient list corroborated at
https://api.politifact.com/factchecks/2024/apr/22/facebook-posts/this-claim-isnt-worth-its-salt-table-condiment-has/
Confidence: **strong** — manufacturer's own statement of function.

So the dextrose is a **stabiliser for the iodide** (a reducing sugar that limits oxidation of I⁻ to
I₂), not a fermentation additive. The ferrocyanide/"yellow prussiate of soda" question belongs to
*other* salts — Morton Coarse Kosher Salt and Morton's industrial grades — **not** to Morton iodized
table salt. See Part B2.

### Finding A3.2 — Does the dextrose matter? No, and here is the arithmetic

- US ingredient labelling requires descending order by weight. Dextrose is listed **third of four**,
  i.e. **less than the calcium silicate** and **more than the potassium iodide**. Since KI is capped
  at **0.01 %**, the dextrose is on the order of **~0.01–0.05 % of the salt**, i.e. **tens to a few
  hundred mg/kg of salt**. Morton does not disclose the exact figure.
- At a 2.5 % salt dose in a dry-salted kraut, that contributes on the order of **≈2.5–12 mg dextrose
  per kg of cabbage** — far below any microbiologically meaningful sugar concentration, and it is a
  *fermentable substrate*, not an inhibitor.
- The same reasoning applies to **calcium silicate** and **sodium silicoaluminate** anti-caking
  agents: they are insoluble mineral particulates present at ~0.5–2 % of the salt at most and are
  not osmotic or antimicrobial actors.

**No experimental evidence found** that the dextrose (or the anti-caking agents) in US iodized salt
affects vegetable fermentation rate, LAB growth, or flavour. The claim is harmless but unfounded.
Confidence: **moderate** — the composition is strong, but the "it doesn't matter" conclusion is
arithmetic inference, not a controlled trial.

### Finding A3.3 — Stabilisers were tested directly in sauerkraut, and were inert

El-Wakeil (1958) added **sodium thiosulfate at 50 and 100 ppm** (the classic KI stabiliser in
iodized salt) to kraut, in both pure and commercial grades, and reported: *"the application of sodium
thiosulfate, both pure and commercial, as the stabilizer for the iodine compound (potassium iodide)
used as the source of iodine did not seem to affect the process of fermentation. Also there was no
difference in effect between the 50 and 100 p.p.m. concentrations used."*
Source: https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/etd/r/1501/10?clear=10&p10_accession_num=osu148656241580449
Confidence: **moderate** (single 1958 study, but a direct test of the actual additive class).

## A4. Where does "never use iodized salt for fermenting" come from, and is it supported?

### Finding A4.1 — The authoritative US source does NOT say this

**National Center for Home Food Preservation (University of Georgia), "Salts Used in Pickling"**
(adapted from the USDA *Complete Guide to Home Canning*, Agriculture Information Bulletin No. 539,
revised 2015) states verbatim:

> *"Use of canning or pickling salt is recommended. **Fermented and non-fermented pickles may be
> safely made using either iodized or non-iodized table salt.** However, **non-caking materials**
> added to table salts **may make the brine cloudy**. Flake salt varies in density and is not
> recommended for use."*
> *"Caution: Use of reduced-sodium salt in fermented pickle recipes is not recommended."*

Source: https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/pickle/general-information-pickling/salts-used-in-pickling/
Confidence: **strong** (the primary US extension authority; note it blames **anti-caking agents**,
not iodine, for cloudiness — the opposite attribution from the folklore).

### Finding A4.2 — The folklore, with its exact wording and its missing citation

**University of Missouri Extension, HES 149, "Using Salt in Home Preserved Foods"** (republished by
UW–Madison Extension, Nov. 2022) states:

> *"**Table salt** is safe for canning, but it contains anticaking ingredients that do not dissolve
> and cause cloudiness. **The iodine in iodized salt can cause pickles to darken, discolor, or be
> spotty. Iodine can cause cauliflower to turn pink or purple.**"*
> *"**Kosher salt** and sea salt can measure differently from canning and pickling salt. Sea salt
> contains various minerals which can cause discoloration or off-flavors. **Do not use these for
> pickling or fermentation.**"*

Source: https://winnebago.extension.wisc.edu/2022/11/04/using-salt-in-home-preserved-foods/
Confidence: **weak-or-contested**. These are assertions with **no citation, no dose, no experiment,
and no distinction between iodide and iodate**. They are directly contradicted by Finding A4.1 and by
every controlled study in A2. Note this source also condemns kosher salt and sea salt, which Part B
shows is likewise unsupported as a general rule.

### Finding A4.2b — Two more extension sources repeating the claim, also uncited

- **Ohio State University Extension, HYG-5342, "Making Fermented Dill Pickles" (2016):** *"Use
  non-iodized canning or pickling salt; **iodine can prevent the bacterial fermentation** and may
  darken pickles."* — **no citation given.** This is the *strongest* form of the claim (actual
  fermentation inhibition) and is precisely the claim refuted by Müller et al. 2018 and Stoll et al.
  2020.
  https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/HYG-5342
- **Illinois Extension, "Fermenting FAQ" (current):** *"iodized salt may lead to slower fermentation
  and browning of vegetables"* — **no citation given.**
  https://extension.illinois.edu/food-preservation/fermenting-faq

Confidence: **weak-or-contested** for the claims; **strong** for the observation that these
institutions attach no evidence to them.

### Finding A4.2c — The warning is carried by pickle/cucumber guidance, not by sauerkraut guidance

No contemporary extension bulletin located in this review singles out **sauerkraut** as requiring
non-iodized salt. The claim appears in **pickle and cucumber** material (A4.2, A4.2b) and in general
"salts used in pickling" pages. Consistent with that, all four controlled **sauerkraut** studies
(1955, 1958, 1998, 2018) found no effect.
Confidence: **moderate** (a negative search result across the sources retrieved).

### Finding A4.3 — The traceable origin: "some unpublished reports"

Badran et al. (1996) — the WHO-funded crossover trial that found no effect — explicitly identifies
where the concern came from:

> *"There have been some suggestions that iodized salt, when used in pickling, may affect the
> consistency of the pickles. **This has never been confirmed.**"*
> *"The aim was to study the possibility that iodized salt used in pickling of vegetables produces
> changes in the colour and texture, **as suggested in some unpublished reports**."*

Source: https://www.emro.who.int/emhj-volume-2-1996/volume-2-issue-2/article5.html
Confidence: **strong** (direct quotation). **The documented origin of the warning is unpublished
reports — there is no traceable experimental paper that originates it.**

### Finding A4.4 — A citation chain that mis-cites its own sources

Amr & Jabay (2004) document the folklore's citation chain and it does not hold up:

- They report: *"Iodine addition was reported by some workers to adversely affect the properties of
  foods prepared with iodized salt to the extent that **some workers recommended that no iodized salt
  should be used in food processing** [ref 4]."* **Ref 4 is El-Wakeil 1958** — the very thesis whose
  Tables 50–52 (Finding A2.2) show **no** effect of iodized salt or of KI up to 200 ppm on sauerkraut
  fermentation.
- They attribute "*discoloration of pickles*" to **ref 5 = Kojima & Brown 1955**. But Kojima & Brown
  found **no** effect on pickles, olives or other processed vegetables (Finding A2.3). The 1955 paper
  is being cited for an effect it did not report.
- Their own new data show a real effect — but for **KIO₃**, not KI, and only for **colour and
  texture**, not fermentation.

Source: https://www.wlfpublisher.com/admin_1992/pdf/articles/2004_issue2_f27.pdf (Introduction and reference list)
Confidence: **moderate-to-strong** (based directly on reading the paper's reference list against the
systematic review's extracted findings for those same references).

### Finding A4.6 — Pre-1995 origin: NOT ESTABLISHED

USDA Farmers' Bulletin No. 1438, *Making fermented pickles* (LeFevre, orig. Aug 1924, rev. Apr 1927,
reprinted 1940), is digitised at UNT (https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc6462/) but is
**page-image only**; its full-text search endpoint returned a bot challenge. **It could not be
confirmed whether it mentions iodine at all.** HathiTrust/Google Books attempts yielded no retrievable
pre-1995 statement. The most valuable unopened document is **West CE, Merx RJHM, *Effect of iodized
salt on the colour and taste of food*, UNICEF, New York, 1995** — a grey-literature report this review
knows only second-hand (via Blankenship 2018, which records that it used an extreme **400 ppm** iodine
in salt on boiled rice and potatoes with no flavour or appearance effect).

**Honest conclusion: the warning was already circulating as unpublished/unofficial material by 1995;
its ultimate origin was not located.** Do **not** attribute it to a specific USDA bulletin or to
**Pickle Packers International** — no evidence linking PPI to this claim was found.
Confidence: **strong as a negative finding** (stated as such rather than filled with a guess).

### Finding A4.7 — Consolidated verdict on Part A

1. For **US iodized salt** — legally potassium or cuprous **iodide** at ~45 mg I/kg — the "never use
   iodized salt for fermenting" warning is **not supported by any experimental evidence**, and is
   contradicted by four sauerkraut studies (1955, 1958, 1998, 2018), two pickle studies (Badran 1996;
   Amr & Jabay 2004 for KI), and one cucumber study with 16S sequencing (Stoll 2020).
   Confidence: **strong**.
2. The defensible residue of the warning is narrow and different: **potassium iodate** at ~40 mg I/kg
   darkens and softens light-coloured brined pickles (Amr & Jabay 2004). This is **cosmetic**, not a
   fermentation failure. Confidence: **moderate** (one strong primary study).
3. For **EU/German** salt, which *is* KIO₃-iodized at 15–25 mg I/kg, that cosmetic caveat applies in
   principle; the one KIO₃ olive-brine trial at 37 mg I/kg found **no significant sensory penalty**
   (Lanza 2020). Confidence: **moderate**.
4. If a model must encode one iodine effect, encode **yeast suppression**, not LAB inhibition
   (Lanza 2020 significant; Müller 2018 p = 0.06 in the same direction). Confidence: **moderate**.
5. There is **no MIC for iodine or iodate against any LAB genus** in the accessible literature.
   Confidence: **strong as a negative finding** — do not synthesise a number.

**Pickles vs sauerkraut — where the folklore actually lives:**

- The cloudiness/darkening/pinking claims appear in **pickle** guidance (A4.2). The same extension
  material's kraut guidance does not carry an iodine warning.
- Every controlled **sauerkraut** study found no effect: **1955** (Kojima & Brown), **1958**
  (El-Wakeil), **1998** (Doman et al.), **2018** (Müller et al.).
- The one **pickle** study with a real positive finding (Amr & Jabay 2004) found it for **iodate**
  only, and it was colour/softening, not fermentation failure. A second pickle study (Badran 1996,
  iodide) found nothing.

**Verdict on A:** the "never use iodized salt for fermenting" warning is **not supported** for US
iodized salt, which is legally potassium or cuprous **iodide**. The defensible, evidence-backed
residue of the warning is narrow and different from the folklore: **potassium iodate** at
~40 mg I/kg can darken and soften **light-coloured brined pickles**, and does not stop the
fermentation. Confidence: **strong** for the "not supported" verdict; **moderate** for the
iodate-pickle caveat (one strong primary study plus one secondary report).

(<212 µm) salt pours at 0.90–1.04 g/mL while the coarse cubic granular salt pours at 1.14–1.30 g/mL.
Particle morphology and size distribution, not "fineness", control the packing. A model that assumes
"finer salt = denser salt = more salt per spoon" gets this backwards.

**Cross-check (arithmetic, not a source).** Dividing label g/tsp by 4.93 mL (a US teaspoon) gives
scooped densities of: Diamond Crystal **0.57**, Morton kosher **0.97**, table salt **1.22**,
canning & pickling **1.22**, Maldon **1.22 g/mL**. Morton TFC Purex free-pour (1.14–1.30) brackets
table salt's 1.22; Morton Flour Salt free-pour (0.90–1.04) brackets Morton Coarse Kosher's 0.97. Two
independent routes agree. **But note these are different quantities**: a teaspoon figure is a
scooped/settled measurement; a PDS "loose (pour)" figure is free-fall. Do not treat them as
interchangeable.

**Tapped vs untapped density of NaCl: NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND.** No tapped (tap-density /
Hausner) value was located from a handbook, pharmacopoeia, FCC/USP monograph or manufacturer spec
sheet. Only Morton's "loose (pour)" ranges exist in the accessible record. **Do not present a
tapped/untapped pair as sourced.**

### Finding B1.3 — Flake salt density: contested

Maldon Sea Salt Flakes at **6.0 g/tsp** comes only from label-mirror databases
(https://www.recipal.com/ingredients/50808-nutrition-facts-calories-protein-carbs-fat-maldon-sea-salt-flakes

**The safe modelling rule for flake salt is "use mass, not volume" — not a specific g/tsp.**

### Finding B1.4 — Dissolution rate: the measurement exists, the numbers do not

### Finding B1.5 — Does dissolution speed matter for dry-salting vs brining? NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND

Searched for studies relating salt particle size, salt dissolution rate or salt crystal form to
sauerkraut or brine fermentation kinetics. **None found.** Every sauerkraut salt study located varies
salt **concentration**, not salt **form**. There is a genuine mechanistic difference — in
**dry-salting**, salt must dissolve in the juice it draws out, so surface area and dissolution rate
are plausibly rate-limiting in the first hours, whereas in **brining** the salt dissolves into a large
stirred water volume — but **stating that as established would be unsupported**. Confidence:
**strong as a negative finding**.

## B2. Do anti-caking agents affect fermentation rate, brine clarity or flavour?

### Finding B2.1 — The premise needs correcting: Morton table salt uses CALCIUM SILICATE, not ferrocyanide

**Morton Iodized Table Salt ingredients: salt, potassium iodide, dextrose, calcium silicate.**
Morton's own FAQ: *"Morton® Iodized Salt contains potassium iodide, dextrose to stabilize the iodide
and **calcium silicate** which is an anti-caking agent."*

https://api.politifact.com/factchecks/2024/apr/22/facebook-posts/this-claim-isnt-worth-its-salt-table-condiment-has/
Confidence: **strong**.

Yellow prussiate of soda (**YPS**, sodium ferrocyanide, E535) is *not* in Morton iodized table salt.

**Actionable:** do **not** treat "kosher salt" as additive-free. Morton kosher contains an anti-caking

### Finding B2.4 — Does anything here affect LAB or fermentation? NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND

Searched specifically for effects of **calcium silicate, sodium silicoaluminate, magnesium carbonate,

fermentation rate, brine clarity or flavour. **No such study was found** — for any of them.
"Inert with respect to LAB" is a reasonable inference from their chemistry and use levels (insoluble
minerals at ≤2 %), **but it is not an evidenced claim**. Do not present it as one.

The **only** ferrocyanide–microorganism experiment located:

production of ethyl alcohol from molasses by *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*." *Biotechnology and

**stimulated** ethanol production, with **261 ppm** giving the maximum effect (**+3.0 %** vs control);
added during yeast *propagation* it depressed ethanol production by up to **4.0 %**; added during the
fermentation stage it had **no significant effect**.
https://www.osti.gov/biblio/7050038
Confidence: **moderate** that the study says this; **weak** for any extrapolation — 261 ppm is
**20–29× the legal maximum in salt**, it is yeast not LAB, and it is molasses not vegetables.

**Toxicology, for completeness (do not mis-cite):** EFSA ANS Panel, "Re-evaluation of sodium
ferrocyanide (E 535), potassium ferrocyanide (E 536) and calcium ferrocyanide (E 538) as food

### Finding B2.5 — What YPS actually does (real crystal physics)

- **Bode AAC, Vonk V, van den Bruele FJ, Kok DJ, Kerkenaar AM, Mantilla MF, Jiang S, Meijer JAM,
  van Enckevort WJP, Vlieg E. "Anticaking Activity of Ferrocyanide on Sodium Chloride Explained by

  reduction of surface free energy", with a marked increase in three-dimensional nucleation and
  branching.
https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5465512

**Mechanistic read (a hypothesis, not a cited finding about fermentation):** YPS works by modifying

Confidence: **moderate** for the mechanism, **weak** for the extrapolation.

**Historical note:** Morton's 1911 anticaking agent was **magnesium carbonate** — *"In 1911, Morton
created free-flowing salt by adding magnesium carbonate as an absorbing element"* (Morton FAQ,
https://www.mortonsalt.com/faq/). "The anticaking agent" is not one chemical across brands or across
history, so any model field called "anticaking agent" is under-specified.

### Finding B2.6 — The dextrose, quantified

**Morton's own figure: iodized table salt contains 0.04 % dextrose = 40 mg per 100 g = 400 ppm.**

Source: https://web.archive.org/web/20081225045630/http://www.mortonsalt.com/faqs/food_salt_faq.html

**Does it matter?** Arithmetic (flagged as arithmetic, not evidence): at a **2.5 % salt** dose,

### Finding B2.7 — The one real experiment on table salt in a vegetable fermentation (restated for Part B)

Müller et al. 2018 (full detail in Finding A2.1) is the only controlled trial that used **actual
iodized table salt** — i.e. salt carrying iodine *and* its anti-caking agent — in a vegetable
fermentation. Result: **no statistically significant** effect on the fermentation microbiota.
Three caveats to carry: (i) it isolates *iodine*, not anti-caking agents as such; (ii) the salt level
was **1.0 %**, low for sauerkraut (typical 2.0–2.5 %), so it does not test the concentrations home
fermenters use; (iii) it did **not** measure brine turbidity or colour.
Confidence: **strong** for what it measured; it **directly refutes** the "iodized salt slows
fermentation" half of the Illinois/UMaine claim.

## B5a. KCl partial substitution for NaCl

### Finding B5a.1 — The maximum KCl fraction: ~30 % of total salt is the best-supported level; 50 % is the sensory ceiling

**Definition warning that must be carried in any model:** the literature uses **two different
conventions** and they are not interchangeable.
- "50 %" in **Choi 1994** (kimchi) and **Li 2022** (sauerkraut) = KCl as a share of the NaCl / brine
  salt, i.e. a NaCl:KCl weight ratio.
- **Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013** describes its treatment as "40 % sodium replacement" but KCl is only
  **30 % of total salt weight** (4.5 of 15 g/kg) because MgCl₂ + CaCl₂ make up the rest.
- **Li 2025 (npj)** and **Huang 2025** define K30/K50 as **% of total salt replaced by KCl**.
Always store which convention a number uses. Confidence: **strong**.

**Evidence for 30 %:**
- **Wolkers-Rooijackers M, Thomas M, Nout MJR. "Effects of sodium reduction scenarios on
  fermentation characteristics and microbiota composition during sauerkraut fermentation." *LWT –
  Food Science and Technology* 2013;54(2):383–388.** doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2013.07.002 (free full text
  https://edepot.wur.nl/291541). 100 kg pilot, white cabbage cv. Atria, 12 °C for 6 wk then 9 °C for
  6 wk. A = 15 g/kg NaCl (control); B = 9 g/kg NaCl; C = 9 g/kg NaCl + 4.5 g/kg KCl + 0.75 g/kg MgCl₂
  + 0.75 g/kg CaCl₂ (isotonic to A; **KCl = 30 % of total salt**, CaCl₂ = 5 %).
  - pH: day 2 = 4.5 / 4.4 / 4.3; day 4 = 4.1 / 4.1 / 4.0; day 9 = 3.9 all; **final day 37 = 3.6 (A) /
    3.7 (B) / 3.5 (C)**. pH < 4 within 9 days in all.
  - Titratable acidity day 37 (g/kg as lactic acid): **A 15.0ᵃ, B 13.0ᵇ, C 15.0ᵃ**.
  - LAB 8.0–8.5 log CFU/g in all; Enterobacteriaceae undetectable from day 4.
  - **Texture (Kramer shear maximum force, N): 6 wk → A 74, B 56, C 136; 12 wk → A 98, B 52,
    C 123.** The KCl/CaCl₂ mix was dramatically *firmer* than full-salt control.
  - Sensory (1–5): texture A 3.5→3.8, B 2.2→2.4, **C 4.4→4.4**; taste A 3.4→3.6, B 1.8→2.8,
    C 3.7→3.8. B (salt reduction alone) significantly worse (P≤0.05); **A and C not different.**
  - **Mechanism — important:** softening in B was **not enzymatic** (PME undetectable, exo-PG ~15 mU/g
    equal across A/B/C, no endo-PG products). It was **lower osmotic/ionic strength**. The authors
    state CaCl₂ at 5 % is too small to explain the firming via an "egg-box" mechanism.
    **→ KCl substitution is substantially an IONIC-STRENGTH substitution, not a taste swap.**
  Confidence: **strong** (full text read).
- **Li Y, Gu Y, Cheng W, et al. "…" *npj Science of Food* 2025;9:156.**
  doi:10.1038/s41538-025-00528-2 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12304172/). Radish paocai,
  starter *Levilactobacillus brevis* PL6-1 at 7.00–7.50 log CFU/g, total salt 1.5 %, 15 °C, 14 d;
  K00/K10/K30/K50 = 0/10/30/50 % KCl of total salt.
  - LAB 7.40 log CFU/g → 8.31–8.47 on day 1 → peak 8.75–8.87 on day 5. *"Partial replacement of NaCl
    with KCl showed no significant impact on LAB growth dynamics."*
  - pH 5.95 → **3.61 by day 5** (all groups); TTA 0.06 % → >0.3 % by day 5 with *no significant
    variation* between groups.
  - **K50 had the highest bitterness intensity**; K30 gave the highest sourness, sweetness, umami and
    overall acceptability. K30 also had the highest mannitol (5.60 g/kg at day 5).
  Confidence: **strong** (full text read).
- **Huang R, Fang Y, Zhong Y, Lu W, Deng Y. *International Journal of Biological Macromolecules*
  2025;322(Pt 4):147107.** doi:10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.147107 — Yunnan suancai, KCl + κ-carrageenan at
  30/40/50 %: *"the 30 % KCl/CG replacement preserved suancai texture and acceptability"*; 40 % and
  50 % gave significantly different flavour profiles. Confidence: **moderate** (abstract).
- **Cen Y, Li Y, Sun X, et al. *International Journal of Food Microbiology* 2026;461:112012.**
  doi:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2026.112012 — 30 % KCl (K30), 30 % MgCl₂, 15/15 KMg. *"All low-sodium
  groups maintained comparable LAB growth, acidification, and overall acceptability."*
  **30 % MgCl₂ significantly reduced radish hardness** — so MgCl₂, not KCl, is the texture risk.
  Confidence: **moderate** (abstract).

**Evidence for 50 % as the ceiling:**
- **Choi SY, Beuchat LR, Perkins LM, Nakayama T. "Inhibition of Escherichia coli O157:H7 and
  Salmonella typhimurium on kimchi." *International Journal of Food Microbiology*
  1994;21(4):335–340.** doi:10.1016/0168-1605(94)90063-9. Brine water 15 % salt; NaCl:KCl ratios
  1:0 (control), 5:1, 2:1, 1:1 wt:wt = 0 %, 17 %, 33 %, 50 % KCl.
  - Total acidity reached the acceptable range **0.4–0.6 % (as lactic acid)** and **pH 4.4–4.7 after
    13 days at 13 ± 1 °C**.
  - **5:1 and 2:1 (NaCl:KCl) gave FASTER growth of LAB** and total aerobic microorganisms than the
    100 % NaCl control.
  - Saltiness, bitterness, sourness, hotness and texture were judged **acceptable at ALL ratios
    tested, including 1:1 = 50 % KCl**.
  Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
- **Li Z, Xie S, Sun B, Zhang Y, Liu K, Liu L. *Journal of Food Processing and Preservation*
  2022;46(9):e16622.** doi:10.1111/jfpp.16622 — sauerkraut. KCl proportion significantly affected
  fermentation kinetics (TTA, reducing sugar); malic/acetic/lactic/succinic acid differed
  significantly (p<.05); LAB number affected by KCl; QDA found *"the substitution ratio of KCl was
  **no more than 50 %** to avoid the unacceptable taste and low overall liking."*
  **Gap: the actual KCl percentages, total salt level, pH/TTA values and LAB counts could not be
  retrieved (Wiley 403). Only "≤50 %" is quotable.** Confidence: **moderate** (abstract only).
- **Lee YN, Lee SM, Kim KO. *Journal of Food Science* 2019;84(10):2961–2972.**
  doi:10.1111/1750-3841.14784 — kimchi, n=167 consumers, 2.0 % NaCl control, 25 % and 50 % sodium
  reduction × KCl at 0/0.47/0.93 %. Healthiness perception improved with sodium reduction; there was
  an unfavourable prior notion about salt replacers. **Sensory only — no pH/TTA/LAB data.**
  Confidence: **moderate**.

### Finding B5a.2 — Does KCl inhibit LAB? Yes, weakly — and always less than an equal amount of NaCl

**Ndiaye A, Fliss I, Filteau M. *Frontiers in Microbiology* 2024;15:1328416.**
doi:10.3389/fmicb.2024.1328416 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10904479/). 31 LAB strains
plus 93 pairwise co-cultures, on PCASL solid medium at 3 % NaCl, 5 % NaCl and 3 % KCl.
- Monocultures (community average): growth **−15 % at 3 % KCl (p<0.0001)**. Acidification: **−14 % at
  5 % NaCl (p<0.0001)**, −3 % at 3 % NaCl (p<0.02), and **no statistically significant effect at
  3 % KCl (p=0.18)**.
- Co-cultures: growth −23.9 % (3 % NaCl), −57.4 % (5 % NaCl), −14.9 % (3 % KCl), all p<0.0001.
  Acidification reduced significantly **only** for 3 % NaCl (−3.6 %, p=0.02) and 5 % NaCl (−15.1 %,
  p<0.0001) — **not** for 3 % KCl.
- Some strains **grew better** at 3 % KCl (e.g. *Lactococcus lactis* subsp. *lactis* 74310, +23 %,
  p=0.01). Co-cultures were more resilient: at 3 % KCl only 5 % lost their significant salt effect vs
  41.7 % at 3 % NaCl and 58.8 % at 5 % NaCl.
- Authors: *"KCl reduces the growth of the majority of our LAB strains, but not as much as NaCl …
  and may not drastically alter their acidification capacity."*
- **Scale caveat: 3 % KCl ≈ 0.40 M K⁺, far above normal brine salt levels (1.5–2.5 %).**
Confidence: **strong** (full text read).

**Rank order of inhibition for LAB: CaCl₂ ≈ NaCl > KCl > MgCl₂.**
- **Bautista-Gallego J, Arroyo-López FN, Durán-Quintana MC, Garrido-Fernández A. *Journal of Food
  Protection* 2008;71(7):1412–1421.** doi:10.4315/0362-028X-71.7.1412 — dose–response of NaCl, KCl,
  CaCl₂, MgCl₂ against *Lactobacillus pentosus* and *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*; non-inhibitory
  concentrations and MICs were estimated. Verbatim: *"All chloride salts exerted a significant
  antimicrobial effect on the growth cycle; particularly, **CaCl₂ showed a similar effect to NaCl,
  while KCl and MgCl₂ were progressively less inhibitory**."*
  **The actual MIC / non-inhibitory concentrations are behind a paywall and were NOT retrievable.**
  Only the rank order is quotable. Confidence: **moderate**.
- Same rank order independently reported for Feta brine: Zonoubi & Goli 2020, *Food Science &
  Nutrition*, doi:10.1002/fsn3.2050.
- NaCl tolerance reference point for cucumber LAB: **Etchells JL, Costilow RN, Anderson TE, Bell TA.
  *Applied Microbiology* 1964;12(6):523–535.** doi:10.1128/am.12.6.523-535.1964 — *Pediococcus
  cerevisiae*, *Lactobacillus plantarum*, *Lb. brevis* tolerated up to **~8 % salt**; thermophilic
  lactobacilli only **2.5–4.0 %**.

**So: up to ~2–3 % KCl in a vegetable brine there is no meaningful inhibition of acidification. The
practical limit on KCl in these products is SENSORY (bitter/metallic), not microbiological.**
Confidence: **strong**.

### Finding B5a.3 — Does KCl shift the community Leuconostoc → Lactobacillus? Not demonstrated

- Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 (PCR-DGGE): treatment C showed **additional** *Lb. paraplantarum* and
  *Lb. curvatus* at day 9 relative to A and B, while *Lc. lactis* and *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*
  dominated **all three**. No shift away from *Leuconostoc*. (Strong but low-resolution: DGGE, total
  DNA not RNA.)
- **Lee M-A, Yun Y-R, Choi Y-J, et al. *Heliyon* 2022;8(11):e11360.**
  doi:10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e11360 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9663873/) — kimchi,
  6 °C, 6 weeks. Salt minerals (mg/kg): purified salt Na 386,334 / Mg 262 / K lowest; solar salt 1 yr
  Na 334,887 / Mg 7,069 / **K 1,907**; dehydrated solar salt Mg 12,545. **Solar-salt kimchi → higher
  proportion of *Leuconostoc* and *Weissella*; purified-salt kimchi → higher *Latilactobacillus
  sakei*** (over-ripening/spoilage-associated) at the end of fermentation. Mannitol highest in
  solar-salt kimchi. **Confound: the K⁺ effect is inseparable from Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ in this design.**
  Confidence: **moderate**.
- **BOTTOM LINE: no experimental study directly measured a KCl-driven *Leuconostoc*→*Lactobacillus*
  shift in sauerkraut or kimchi.** The evidence is *consistent with* mineral/K⁺-rich salt favouring
  *Leuconostoc*/*Weissella*, but the stronger driver is total ionic strength / salt **level**, not the
  Na:K identity. Label any model term for this **weak-or-contested**. Confidence: **strong as a
  negative finding**.

### Finding B5a.4 — The essential control: plain salt reduction (without K⁺ replacement) is the risky move

**Johanningsmeier SD, McFeeters RF, Fleming HP, Thompson RL. *Journal of Food Science*
2007;72(2):M116–M172.** doi:10.1111/j.1750-3841.2007.00372.x — cabbage salted at 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.0 %
NaCl ± *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* LA 81 at 10⁶ CFU/g. Natural fermentations became variable, with
softening and off-flavours as salt decreased; starter-added gave firm texture and reduced off-flavours
at **all** salt levels (P<0.05) and a more uniform pH decline → **50 % NaCl reduction is achievable
with a *Leuconostoc* starter.** Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

### Finding B5a.5 — 100 % substitution is possible for LAB but costs quality

*Molecules* 2022;27(5):1609. doi:10.3390/molecules27051609
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8911622/) — pickled green tomatoes, **100 % substitution**
of NaCl by KCl or MgCl₂, 28 d at 16–18 °C. Final LAB **4.11–4.63 log CFU** for all variants (KCl did
not prevent LAB growth); yeasts <1 log CFU/g throughout. *"NaCl addition induced the most stable
characteristics"*; *"NaCl has the lowest influence on the textural parameters."* No pH/TTA data in the
accessible text. Confidence: **moderate-to-strong**.

## B5b. CaCl₂ addition: rate, firmness and community

### Finding B5b.1 — The dose regimes (do not conflate them)

| Regime | CaCl₂ | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Texture-only addition to a normal NaCl brine | **20–40 mM (0.2–0.4 %)** | Standard industry practice |
| CaCl₂ as the *only* salt (NaCl eliminated) | **100 mM (1.1 %)** | Needs starter + potassium sorbate + controlled air purging |
| Trace-ion studies | 0.45–3.6 mM (50–400 ppm) | 1–2 orders of magnitude lower — **a different regime** |

Source for the 20–40 mM industry figure: Pérez-Díaz IM et al., "Fermented and Acidified Vegetables",
Ch. 51 in *Compendium of Methods for the Microbiological Examination of Foods* (APHA, 2013), USDA-ARS
public copy https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p390.pdf — *"calcium chloride,
ca. 0.2–0.4 %, and other calcium salts may inhibit the action of softening enzymes. Calcium chloride is
now being added to commercial cucumber brines."* Confidence: **moderate** (authoritative institutional
review chapter).

### Finding B5b.2 — CaCl₂ does NOT slow acidification in cucumbers — it accelerates it

**Pérez-Díaz IM, Dickey AN, Fitria R, Ravishankar N, Hayes J, Campbell K, Arritt F. *Journal of
Applied Microbiology* 2020;128(6):1678–1693.** doi:10.1111/jam.14597 — no salt vs **100 mmol/L
(1.1 %) CaCl₂** vs **1.03 mol/L (6 %) NaCl**, commercial and lab scale.
- **CaCl₂ and no-salt brines sustained FASTER microbial growth and FASTER pH reduction than 6 %
  NaCl.**
- Day 1 community: *Leuconostoc*, *Lactococcus*, *Weissella* dominated in no-salt and 100 mM CaCl₂;
  in 6 % NaCl it was *Weissella* + Enterobacteriaceae.
- **By day 3: *Lactobacillus* dominated ALL fermentations regardless of salt type.**
- **By day 7: *Lactobacillus* = 84–96 % of the population** (except no-salt, which stayed mixed).
Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

**McFeeters RF, Pérez-Díaz IM. *Journal of Food Science* 2010;75(3):C291–C296.**
doi:10.1111/j.1750-3841.2010.01558.x — CaCl₂ as the only salt: sugars → mainly lactic acid,
**pH fell to <3.5** (same pattern as NaCl brines); no detectable propionic or butyric acid. Firmness
retention with 100–300 mM CaCl₂ at 45 °C storage was **not significantly different** from 1.03 M NaCl
+ 40 mM CaCl₂. Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

**Matrix matters — in OLIVES the opposite happens.** Rodríguez-Gómez F, Bautista-Gallego J,
Romero-Gil V, Arroyo-López FN, Garrido-Fernández A, García-García P. *LWT* 2012;46(1):56–63.
doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2011.11.002 — Spanish green table olive fermentation, NaCl/KCl/CaCl₂ centroid
mixture design: *"the production rate of lactic acid was either decreased or delayed by the presence
of calcium chloride but adequate final conditions were always reached"*, and *"a stimulating effect of
potassium on lactic acid production."* **Do not transfer the Ca²⁺ rate slowdown to cucumbers.**
Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

### Finding B5b.3 — Firmness: large and reproducible in the lab, smaller (and negative) at commercial scale

**The classic lab evidence:**
- **Tang H-CL, McFeeters RF. *Journal of Food Science* 1983.** doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1983.tb14789.x —
  **"Calcium chloride at 20 and 40 mM prevented firmness loss compared to fresh cucumbers during
  11 months of storage."** Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
- **Fleming HP, McFeeters RF, Thompson RL. *Journal of Food Science* 1987;52(3):653–657.**
  doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1987.tb06695.x — **0.2 % CaCl₂ (≈18 mM)** with 2.6/4.2/5.8 % NaCl retained
  firmness through 1 month fermentation and 12 months storage. **With NO added NaCl, cucumbers were
  firm after fermentation but firmness fell during storage to 69 % (whole), 64 % (mesocarp), 32 %
  (endocarp) of initial.** Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
- **Guillou AA, Floros JD, Cousin MA. *Journal of Food Science* 1992;57(6):1364–1368.**
  doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1992.tb06859.x — synergy allowed good quality pickles at **5 % NaCl + 0.2 %
  CaCl₂ + 0.2 % potassium sorbate**. Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
- **Yousefi M, Arianfar A, Hakimzadeh V, Rafe A. *Foods* 2025;14(3):336.**
  doi:10.3390/foods14030336 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11817513/) — firmness in
  Newtons: fresh cucumbers ≈ **12 N**; at 100 ppm ion after 30 weeks, **NaCl control 8 N vs Ca²⁺
  ~10 N (significantly higher, p<0.05)**, MgCl₂ ~7 N. Firmness order after 6 months:
  **Ca-acetate > CaCl₂ > NaCl > KCl > MgCl₂ > AlCl₃**. Also proposes *"a pH threshold of around 3.6
  may serve as a predictive marker for acceptable firmness and taste profiles."*
  Confidence: **strong** (full text read), but **these doses are 1–2 orders of magnitude below the
  USDA 20–40 mM work** — a different regime.
- **Buescher RW, Hamilton C, Thorne J, Cho M. *Journal of Food Quality* 2011;34(2):93–99.**
  doi:10.1111/j.1745-4557.2011.00374.x — **high crispness retained over 52 weeks only in tissues with
  elevated residual Ca²⁺ (1,248 and 1,490 ppm)**; conclusion: **alum can be replaced by elevating
  residual Ca²⁺**. Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

**The commercial-scale reality check — the key contradiction:**
**McMurtrie EK, Johanningsmeier SD. *Journal of Food Quality* 2018:8051435.**
doi:10.1155/2018/8051435 — commercial scale, 6 processors, 2 years, vessels 10,000–40,000 L, storage
55–280 d, **0.1 M CaCl₂ + 6 mM potassium sorbate** + starter vs standard industrial NaCl.
- **Terminal pH 3.23 ± 0.09 (CaCl₂) vs 3.30 ± 0.12 (NaCl)** — complete lactic fermentation in both,
  CaCl₂ slightly *lower*.
- **CaCl₂ cucumbers were on average 1.8 N LESS FIRM** — significant in the finished product
  (P<0.0001).
- Colour: higher hue, lower chroma (P<0.0269), consistent with increased photooxidation.
- *"Commercial implementation of CaCl₂ brines … in open tanks variably resulted in texture and color
  defects."*
Confidence: **strong** (abstract). **The lab/jar results are optimistic relative to open-tank
reality.**

**Commercial process that works** (for the "no NaCl" case): **Pérez-Díaz IM, McFeeters RF, Moeller L,
Johanningsmeier SD, et al. *Journal of Food Science* 2015;80(12):M2827–M2836.**
doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13107 — CaCl₂ + potassium sorbate at equilibrated **100 mM and 6 mM**,
*Lactobacillus plantarum* LA0045 at **10⁶ CFU/mL**, air purged 2 × 2–3 h/day on days 1–10 and
1 × 2–3 h/day on days 11–14, in 12,490 L and 28,400 L tanks. **All fermentations completed: full
sugar→lactic acid conversion, pH decrease to 3.0, microbiologically stable ≥21 d.** Suitable for
**short-term** storage. Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

### Finding B5b.4 — CaCl₂ in sauerkraut: thin evidence

- **Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013** (see B5a.1): CaCl₂ = 0.75 g/kg = **5 % of total salt**, in the
  KCl + MgCl₂ + CaCl₂ mix. Full-rate fermentation (pH < 4 by day 9, final 3.5), TTA equal to control,
  and the **firmest** kraut (Kramer max force **136 N at 6 wk vs 74 N control; 123 N vs 98 N at
  12 wk**). But the authors state CaCl₂ at 5 % is too small to explain the firming by an egg-box
  mechanism — **attribute the firming mainly to total ionic strength**.
- **Pires-Cabral P, Mira H, Quintas C. *Fermentation* 2025;11(2):91.**
  doi:10.3390/fermentation11020091 — white cabbage + salicornia ± CaCl₂. Without vs with CaCl₂: final
  pH **4.07 vs 3.58**; acidity **0.70 vs 0.77 g lactic acid/100 mL**; LAB **6.01 vs 5.51 log CFU/g**;
  *"analogous microbial and sensorial characteristics … but with an increased hardness."*
  **Ca interfered with mineral diffusion, with Zn reduced by 37 %.** **The CaCl₂ concentration is not
  stated in the accessible abstract (MDPI 403).** Confidence: **moderate**.

### Finding B5b.5 — CaCl₂ bitterness: not a problem at ≤35 mM in pickles

**Wilson EM, Johanningsmeier SD, Osborne JA. *Journal of Food Science* 2015;80(6):S1360–S1367.**
doi:10.1111/1750-3841.12882 — cucumbers fermented/stored with 0.1 M CaCl₂ or 1 M NaCl in 3,000 gal
open-air commercial tanks, processed into hamburger dill chips containing 0.38 M NaCl.
- **50 % detection threshold of CaCl₂ in dill pickle chips = 61.8 ± 7.6 mM.**
- Consumers (n=101) showed **no significant preference difference** after 2 months (P=0.75) or
  8 months (P=0.68).
- Consumer liking of flavour (n=73) was **not** influenced by fermentation in CaCl₂ nor by **23 or
  35 mM CaCl₂** in finished products (P>0.05). **Variability in texture decreased consumer liking
  (P<0.05).**
Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

**Cross-matrix caution:** in table olives, CaCl₂ *does* increase bitterness — López-López A,
Moreno-Baquero JM, Garrido-Fernández A. *Foods* 15(1):104. doi:10.3390/foods15010104: *"CaCl₂, while
enhancing kinaesthetic properties such as hardness, crunchiness, and fibrousness, also increased
bitterness."* **Treat as weak evidence for cucumbers/sauerkraut.** Confidence: **strong for olives,
weak for other matrices**.

**KCl, not CaCl₂, is the classic bitterness risk:** *"at high concentrations, KCl imparts a bitter
metallic taste to food products, leading to lower consumer acceptability"* (Ndiaye et al. 2024, citing
Khetra et al. 2016). Li et al. 2025 measured the highest bitterness in K50. **No experimental KCl
bitter-taste detection threshold in a vegetable brine was found.**

### Finding B5b.6 — Bloater damage: CaCl₂ does NOT prevent it

**Zhai Y, Pérez-Díaz IM. *Journal of Food Science* 2017.** doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13945 — CaCl₂
cover-brine reformulation with Ca(OH)₂: Ca(OH)₂ + acetic acid gave complete fermentation with final pH
0.5 units higher; lactic acid +22 %; Enterobacteriaceae relative abundance −92 %; Leuconostocaceae
+7 %; **CO₂ +25 %**; 50 % reduction in acetic acid formation with Ca(OH)₂ + 690 mM (4 %) NaCl.
**No significant difference in bloater index**, because CO₂ stayed above the **20 mg/100 mL** threshold
needed to induce the defect. Confidence: **strong** (abstract).

**Etchells JL, Borg AF, Bell TA. *Applied Microbiology* 1968;16(7):1029–1035.**
doi:10.1128/am.16.7.1029-1035.1968 — bloaters at 5–10 % salt were associated with gaseous fermentation,
chiefly yeasts; 0.025–0.1 % sorbic acid controlled fermentative yeasts; **inoculation of
*Lactobacillus brevis* produced bloaters identical to yeast-made bloaters in ALL jars, while
*L. plantarum* and *Pediococcus cerevisiae* produced none.** Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
**No study was found showing CaCl₂ itself prevents bloater damage.**

Related: Fleming HP, McDonald LC, McFeeters RF, Thompson RL, Humphries EG. *Journal of Food Science*
1995;60(5):1063–1065. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1995.tb05662.x — blanching (3 min, 77 °C) + calcium
acetate buffer + *L. plantarum* prevented bloaters in the lab, but at pilot scale the cucumbers were
**severely bloated with unacceptable firmness after 7 months**, apparently from post-blanch microbial
recontamination.

## B5 — NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND (KCl and CaCl₂)

**KCl:**
1. **No study substituting KCl for part of the NaCl in a CUCUMBER or pickled-vegetable brine** with
   fermentation-rate or quality endpoints. Only trace-ion KCl additions (50–400 ppm) and table-olive
   work exist. **Cucumber-brine KCl substitution is a genuine evidence gap.**
2. **No numeric KCl MIC or non-inhibitory concentration for LAB** — estimated by Bautista-Gallego
   2008 but paywalled. Only the rank order is quotable.
3. **No KCl bitter-taste detection threshold** in sauerkraut, kimchi or cucumber brine.
4. **No experimental evidence that KCl substitution shifts sauerkraut or kimchi from *Leuconostoc* to
   *Lactobacillus*.**
5. **No quantitative pH/TTA/LAB numbers from Li et al. 2022** (sauerkraut KCl) — only "≤50 %".
6. **No dose–response at 60/70/80 % KCl** in sauerkraut or kimchi with sensory + rate endpoints.

**CaCl₂:**
7. **No experimental study measuring white sediment / turbidity caused by excess CaCl₂** in cucumber
   brines or sauerkraut — widely repeated in troubleshooting material, never measured. **Treat as
   folklore unless a primary source is produced.**
8. **No experimental data on CALCIUM LACTATE addition to cucumber brines or sauerkraut.** Calcium
   lactate firmness evidence exists only for table olives and for alginate spherification. **Do not
   model calcium lactate for sauerkraut/cucumber brine on the basis of evidence.**
9. **No CaCl₂ dose–response curve (0/10/20/40/80 mM) for sauerkraut or kimchi fermentation rate** with
   pH curves, TTA and LAB counts.
10. **No firmness values in Newtons for a CaCl₂ concentration series in sauerkraut.** The only N
    values found are the Kramer-shear maxima of Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013.
11. **No experimental evidence that CaCl₂ reduces bloater damage** (Zhai & Pérez-Díaz 2017 found no
    significant bloater-index difference).
12. **No CaCl₂ MIC or growth-inhibition number for vegetable-fermentation LAB** — only the qualitative
    rank order CaCl₂ ≈ NaCl > KCl > MgCl₂ for *L. pentosus*.

## B5 — Consolidated model guidance (evidence-graded)

1. Set the KCl ceiling at **~30 % of total salt for "safe by default"**; allow up to **50 % with an
   explicit bitterness penalty term**. Confidence: **moderate-to-strong**.
2. **Model KCl substitution as an ionic-strength substitution, not a taste swap.** The dominant
   effect is maintaining osmotic/ionic strength, which is what protects tissue firmness.
   Confidence: **strong** (Wolkers-Rooijackers 2013 mechanism analysis).
3. **KCl does not meaningfully slow LAB acidification at realistic brine concentrations** (up to 50 %
   KCl in paocai and kimchi, 30 % in sauerkraut; in vitro p=0.18 at 3 % KCl). Confidence: **strong**.
4. **CaCl₂ in cucumber brines: 20–40 mM for texture-only addition; 100 mM only when eliminating
   NaCl**, and then only with starter + potassium sorbate + controlled air purging. Expect terminal
   pH 3.0–3.3 in CaCl₂-only processes — slightly *lower* than NaCl. Confidence: **strong**.
5. **CaCl₂ does NOT slow acidification in cucumbers — it speeds it up** relative to 1 M NaCl. It
   *does* slow lactic-acid production in **olives**. Keep the matrices separate. Confidence: **strong**.
6. **Budget a small firmness penalty for commercial-scale Ca-only cucumber fermentation:** −1.8 N on
   average plus colour/photooxidation defects. Confidence: **strong**.
7. **Ca²⁺ side-effect:** in cabbage, CaCl₂ addition reduced K, Mg and especially **Zn (−37 %)** in the
   final product (Pires-Cabral 2025). Confidence: **moderate**.
8. **CaCl₂ bitterness is not a problem at ≤35 mM in pickles** (50 % detection threshold
   61.8 ± 7.6 mM; Wilson 2015). Confidence: **strong**.

## B3. Do natural / unrefined salts change fermentation?

**Short answer: yes, measurably — the microbial community and the metabolite profile shift. The
differences are real and statistically analysed. They are not, however, large changes in
acidification rate, and no study found unrefined salt to be unsafe or to fail fermentation.**

### Finding B3.1 — Kimchi: salt type shifts the community and the metabolome (the two key studies)

**(a) Kim DW, Kim BM, Lee HJ, Jang GJ, Song SH, Lee JI, Lee SB, Shim JM, Lee KW, Kim JH, Ham KS,
Chen F, Kim HJ. "Effects of Different Salt Treatments on the Fermentation Metabolites and Bacterial
Profiles of Kimchi." *Journal of Food Science* 2017;82(5):1124–1131.** doi:10.1111/1750-3841.13713

Design: **purified salt (PS)** vs **mineral-rich sea salt (MRS)**, "both with different mineral
profiles"; culture-dependent 16S rRNA sequencing plus mass-based metabolomics.

Result, verbatim: *"The different mineral profiles in the fermentation medium caused changes in the
bacterial profiles of the 2 kimchi products. **An increase of *Leuconostoc* species in MRS-kimchi
decreased the *Lactobacillus*/*Leuconostoc* ratio**, which led to changes in metabolites (including
sugars, amino acids, organic acids, lipids, sulfur compounds, and terpenoids) associated with kimchi
quality."* The authors conclude MRS **positively** affected mineral contents, bacterial growth and
metabolite profiles.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28440871/
Confidence: **moderate-to-strong** (abstract read; the direction is explicit and quantitative
direction — *Lactobacillus*/*Leuconostoc* ratio falls — but the numeric ratio and CFU values were not
retrievable from the abstract).

**(b) Lee M-A, Choi Y-J, Kim Y-S, Chon S-Y, Chung Y-B, Park S-H, Yun Y-R, Min S-G, Yang H-C, Seo H-Y.
"Effects of salt type on the metabolites and microbial community in kimchi fermentation." *Heliyon*
2022;8(11):e11360.** doi:10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e11360 (PMC9663873 — full text read)

Design: four salts — **solar salt aged 1 year (SS1), solar salt aged 3 years (SS3), dehydrated solar
salt (DSS), purified salt (PS)**; kimchi stored at **6 °C for 6 weeks**, sampled weekly; ICP-MS
mineral analysis, community profiling, GC-MS metabolomics, PCA.

**Measured mineral contents of the four salts (mg/kg, mean ± SD, ICP-MS):**

| Mineral | SS1 (solar 1 yr) | SS3 (solar 3 yr) | DSS (dehydrated solar) | PS (purified) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na | 334,887 ± 1,206 ᵇ | 335,830 ± 325 ᵇ | 323,811 ± 221 ᶜ | 386,334 ± 1,188 ᵃ |
| **Mg** | **7,069 ± 103 ᵇ** | **5,150 ± 121 ᶜ** | **12,545 ± 78 ᵃ** | **262 ± 2 ᵈ** |
| **K** | 1,907 ± 87 ᶜ | 1,340 ± 79 ᵈ | 2,141 ± 32 ᵇ | **2,779 ± 86 ᵃ** |
| **Ca** | 1,129 ± 17 ᵇ | **1,893 ± 7 ᵃ** | 835 ± 13 ᶜ | 465 ± 3 ᵈ |
| Mn | 1.57 ± 0.13 ᵃ | 1.26 ± 0.11 ᵇ | 1.62 ± 0.15 ᵃ | **ND** |
| Fe | 0.10 ± 0.03 ᵇ | 0.21 ± 0.06 ᵃ | 0.17 ± 0.05 ᵃᵇ | **ND** |
| Zn | 0.02 ± 0.02 ᵃᵇ | 0.02 ± 0.00 ᵃᵇ | 0.05 ± 0.03 ᵃ | **ND** |
| Sr | 21.90 ± 3.55 ᵃ | 12.02 ± 2.00 ᵇ | 6.58 ± 1.55 ᶜ | 0.52 ± 0.11 ᵈ |

Different superscript letters = significantly different (p<0.05, Duncan's test).
**Note two things that contradict intuition:** (i) **K is *higher* in purified salt** (2,779 mg/kg)
than in any solar salt; it is **Mg and Ca** that distinguish unrefined salt — **Mg by 20–48×**
(262 → 5,150–12,545 mg/kg) and Ca by 1.8–4×. (ii) Mn, Fe and Zn are **not detectable at all** in
purified salt.

**Community result:** *"The main microorganisms detected in kimchi were *Weissella koreensis*,
*Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, and *Latilactobacillus sakei*. **Leuconostoc and Weissella were mainly
present in kimchi supplemented with solar salt. However, a high proportion of *L. sakei* was present
in kimchi supplemented with purified salt and dehydrated salt.**"* Metabolites: *"the content of free
sugars, organic acids, and amino acids differed in kimchi fermented with different salt types."*
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9663873/
Confidence: **strong** (full text read).

**Why this matters biologically:** the paper notes that **Mg and Mn are essential for LAB growth and
metabolism** (citing Kim et al. 2017; Raccach 1998) and that these were **higher in the solar salts
than in purified salt**. *Latilactobacillus sakei* is the over-ripening / late-spoilage-associated
species; *Leuconostoc*/*Weissella* dominance is the "fresh, well-fermented" community. So the
mineral profile plausibly steers the succession — a real, mechanistically sensible effect.
Confidence: **moderate** (the correlation is clear; the causal mediation by Mg/Mn is the authors'
interpretation).

**(c) Related:** Yun Y-R, Choi Y-J, Kim Y-S, et al. "Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of solar
salt brined kimchi." *Food Science and Biotechnology* 2023;32(5):679–687.
doi:10.1007/s10068-022-01203-y — solar-salt kimchi showed higher antioxidant activity and greater
inhibition of NO production / inflammation-related gene expression than purified-salt kimchi.
Confidence: **moderate**.

### Finding B3.2 — Kimchi mineral-rich salt vs purified salt: the "salt type" effect is mineral-driven, not "naturalness"-driven

Both B3.1(a) and B3.1(b) point the same way and identify the same candidate mechanism: **Mg (and Mn,
Ca) in unrefined salt**, not some vitalistic property of "natural" salt. **K is not the
discriminator — purified salt had the *most* K.** Any model that encodes "unrefined salt = more K"
is wrong for these Korean salts. Confidence: **strong** (direct ICP-MS data).

### Finding B3.3 — The sauerkraut "salt quality" study is actually a KCl study (important mis-citation guard)

**Viander B, Mäki M, Palva A. "Impact of low salt concentration, salt quality on natural large-scale
sauerkraut fermentation." *Food Microbiology* 2003;20(4):391–395.**
doi:10.1016/S0740-0020(02)00150-8

Despite the title's "salt quality", this paper's "different salt quality" arm is **mineral salt
(28 % KCl, 57 % NaCl)** — a **sodium-reduction / KCl-substitution** study, **not** an iodized-salt and
not a refined-vs-sea-salt study. Design: three parallel trials, 430 kg scale, white cabbage.

Numbers:
- NaCl concentrations: trials 1A–C = **0.5 %**, trials 3A–C = **1.2 %**; trials 2A–C = **0.5 %
  mineral salt, giving 0.3 % NaCl**.
- pH fell somewhat faster at 1.2 % NaCl, but *"no significant differences could be observed after the
  sixth fermentation day."*
- **LAB reaching 10⁸ cfu/mL took 3 days (1.2 % NaCl), 4 days (0.5 % NaCl) and 6 days (0.5 % mineral
  salt).** At the end of fermentation all trials reached **10⁸ cfu/mL**.
- **Lactic acid production was clearly lower after 2 weeks** in the 0.5 % mineral-salt trials. Acetic
  acid did not differ much.
- **Sensory: the sauerkraut juice fermented with 0.5 % mineral salt was judged to have the BEST
  taste** by a trained panel.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0740-0020(02)00150-8
Confidence: **strong** (abstract).
**Do not cite this paper as evidence about iodized salt.** Confidence: **strong**.

### Finding B3.4 — NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND for several specific refuted/unsupported claims

- **No study found comparing refined vs unrefined salt in cucumber pickle or pepper-mash
  fermentation** with pH/LAB/metabolite endpoints. The kimchi work (B3.1) is the real evidence base;
  sauerkraut has only the KCl "salt quality" study (B3.3).
- **No study found showing unrefined sea salt changes the *rate* of acidification** (as opposed to the
  community composition and metabolite profile). Both kimchi studies report community/metabolite
  shifts, not rate failures.
- Extension guidance that "sea salt contains various minerals which can cause discoloration or
  off-flavors… Do not use these for pickling or fermentation"
  (https://winnebago.extension.wisc.edu/2022/11/04/using-salt-in-home-preserved-foods/) is
  **uncited**. The one relevant piece of real evidence points the opposite way: a trained panel
  preferred the mineral-salt sauerkraut juice (B3.3), and mineral-rich sea salt *improved* the kimchi
  metabolite profile (B3.1). Confidence: **strong as a negative finding**.
- **Hahn YS. "Effect of salt type and concentration on the growth of lactic acid bacteria isolated
  from kimchi." *Korean Journal of Food Science and Technology* 2003;35:743–747** — identified as
  directly on point but **not obtained** (Korean-language, not accessible in this review).
  Confidence: **not obtained** — flag as a lead.
- **Han GJ, Son AR, Lee SM, Jung JK, Kim SH, Park KY. "Improved quality and increased in vitro
  anticancer effect of kimchi by using natural sea salt without bittern and baked (Guwun) salt."
  *Journal of the Korean Society of Food Science and Nutrition* 2009;38:996–1002** — identified as
  relevant; **not obtained**.

## B4. Trace minerals: Mg, Ca, Fe — how they could matter, and what data exist

### Finding B4.1 — Measured trace-mineral contents of real salts (this is the anchor data)

**(a) Refined vs solar salt, by ICP-MS** — from Lee et al. 2022, table reproduced in Finding B3.1(b).
The headline contrast: **Mg 262 mg/kg (purified) vs 5,150–12,545 mg/kg (solar)**; **Ca 465 vs
835–1,893 mg/kg**; **Mn, Fe, Zn non-detectable in purified salt**; **K actually higher in purified
salt (2,779 mg/kg)**. Confidence: **strong**.

**(b) Gourmet/artisan salts, by ICP-MS** — **Di Salvo E, Tardugno R, Nava V, Naccari C, Virga A,
Salvo A, Corbo F, Clodoveo ML, Cicero N. "Gourmet Table Salts: The Mineral Composition Showdown."
*Toxics* 2023;11(8):705.** doi:10.3390/toxics11080705 (PMC10459412). Ten salts, 12 elements, triplicate
analysis. Selected values (mg/kg):

| Salt | Ca | Fe | Mn | Zn | Ni | Pb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Himalayan pink** | 2,926 | **2.6** | 1.3 | 246.8 | 0.4 | 5.5 |
| Maldon | 2,624 | 2.3 | 0.8 | 238.8 | 1.5 | 8.4 |
| Hawaiian pink | 2,476 | 1.4 | 2.4 | 215.8 | 1.1 | 7.9 |
| Hawaiian black | 1,861 | 6.4 | 2.6 | 156.3 | 1.0 | 8.2 |
| Guerande grey | 3,818 | 1.8 | **5.0** | 178.5 | 0.2 | 6.6 |
| Atlantic grey | 2,640 | 2.5 | 1.2 | 327.4 | 0.2 | 8.4 |
| Persian blue | **6,252** | **21.7** | 3.0 | **1,334.4** | **12.4** | 9.2 |
| Mozia | 3,438 | 10.5 | 2.9 | 642.0 | 0.6 | 7.2 |

**Reading this correctly:** the "gourmet salt is full of minerals" story is real for **Ca** (all
unrefined salts are ~1,900–6,300 mg/kg Ca, i.e. **0.2–0.6 %**) but the **Fe and Mn contents are
trivial** (Fe 1.4–21.7 mg/kg; Mn 0.8–5.0 mg/kg) — a 2.5 % brine made with Himalayan pink salt supplies
**~0.065 mg Fe per kg of brine**. Note the study itself flags that **Pb exceeded the maximum level in
all samples** and that **Ni was high in Persian blue and smoked salts** — an unrefined-salt caution
that is actually evidenced, unlike the flavour folklore. Confidence: **strong** for the numbers;
**moderate** for generalising from 10 Italian-market products (single samples per type, triplicate
instrumental replicates).
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10459412/

**(c) Crude vs refined salt, by wet chemistry** — **Amr AS, Jabay OA, *Journal of Food, Agriculture &
Environment* 2004;2(2):151–156** (Table 1, mg/kg):

| Salt | Fe | Mg | Ca | Br | SO₄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dead Sea **crude** | 26 | **1,220** | 2,500 | 358 | 3,000 |
| Dead Sea **refined** | 6 | **12** | 890 | 174 | 20 |
| Wet-mined **crude** | 50 | 420 | 3,800 | 35 | **37,000** |
| Wet-mined **refined** | 11 | 25 | 1,300 | 29 | 4,400 |

**Refining removed ~99 % of Mg** (1,220 → 12 mg/kg), ~60 % of Ca, ~50 % of Br, and ~99 % of sulfate
in the Dead Sea salt. The paper notes the Codex standard for table salt intended for iodization allows
maxima of **2,000 mg/kg Ca, 1,000 mg/kg Mg and 5,000 mg/kg sulfate**.
Source: https://www.wlfpublisher.com/admin_1992/pdf/articles/2004_issue2_f27.pdf
Confidence: **strong** (direct analysis, published methods: ASTM D3869-79 for Br, ISO 2482 for Ca/Mg,
AOAC 965.09 by AAS for Fe).

### Finding B4.2 — Magnesium: a real LAB growth requirement, but no vegetable-fermentation dose–response

- **Mg is a documented LAB growth/metabolism requirement.** Lee et al. 2022 state explicitly that
  "Mg and Mn, which are essential for LAB growth and metabolism (Kim et al. 2017; Raccach 1998), were
  higher in SS and DSS than in the PS" — and they use this to interpret their community shift.
  Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9663873/
  Confidence: **moderate** (the requirement is well established in LAB physiology; the specific
  citations were not read).
- **Mg and K are the bitter-taste cations in salt.** Lee et al. 2022 note "Mg and K, which produce a
  bitter taste (Ha and Park 1998), decreased with the aging period of solar salt" — i.e. **aged solar
  salt is less bitter because it has lost Mg and K**. Measured: Mg 7,069 (1 yr) → 5,150 (3 yr) mg/kg;
  K 1,907 → 1,340 mg/kg. This is a real, quantified sensory-relevant gradient across unrefined salts.
  Confidence: **moderate** (the bitter-taste attribution is Ha & Park 1998, a Korean-language paper
  not read; the mineral decline is measured).
- **No experimental dose–response study found** relating Mg concentration in a vegetable brine to LAB
  growth rate, acidification rate, or bitterness. **No experimental evidence found.**

### Finding B4.3 — Calcium: the best-evidenced trace mineral in this whole review

Ca is the one trace mineral with a large, replicated experimental literature — but it comes from
**added CaCl₂**, not from the Ca naturally present in salt. Full detail is in Part B5b; the key
numbers:

- **20–40 mM CaCl₂ (0.2–0.4 %) prevents firmness loss** in cucumber brines — standard industry
  practice (USDA-ARS/APHA chapter, https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p390.pdf).
- **Tang & McFeeters 1983**: CaCl₂ at **20 and 40 mM prevented firmness loss vs fresh cucumbers over
  11 months of storage**. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1983.tb14789.x
- **Fleming et al. 1987**: **0.2 % CaCl₂ (≈18 mM)** retained firmness through fermentation + 12 months;
  **without NaCl, firmness fell to 69 % / 64 % / 32 %** of initial in whole / mesocarp / endocarp.
  doi:10.1111/j.1365-2621.1987.tb06695.x
- **CaCl₂ does not slow cucumber acidification — it accelerates it** vs 1 M NaCl (Pérez-Díaz et al.
  2020, doi:10.1111/jam.14597).
- **CaCl₂ bitterness is not a problem at ≤35 mM in pickles**; the 50 % detection threshold in dill
  chips is **61.8 ± 7.6 mM** (Wilson et al. 2015, doi:10.1111/1750-3841.12882).

**Salt-derived Ca is a separate question and the answer is: too little to matter by itself.** Even a
high-Ca unrefined salt at ~6,250 mg/kg Ca (Persian blue) used at 2.5 % gives only **~156 mg Ca per kg
of product** ≈ **3.9 mM Ca** — roughly **5–10× below** the 20–40 mM texture-effective range. **A model
needs added CaCl₂, not a fancy salt, to get a firming effect.**
Confidence: **moderate** (arithmetic on strong input data).

**One piece of evidence that salt-derived Ca does matter functionally:** Amr & Jabay found that the
iodate-induced **softening was less pronounced when the high-calcium refined wet-mined salt was used**
(Ca 1,300 mg/kg) than with the low-Ca Dead Sea salt — an in-experiment demonstration that the Ca in
the salt modulated a texture outcome. Confidence: **moderate** (a single observation within a
multi-factor experiment, not a designed Ca dose–response).

### Finding B4.4 — Iron: plausible, but NO EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FOUND in fermented vegetables

- Measured Fe in salts: **non-detectable in purified salt**; **0.10–0.21 mg/kg in solar salts** (Lee
  et al. 2022); **1.4–21.7 mg/kg in gourmet salts** (Di Salvo et al. 2023 — highest in Persian blue
  at 21.7 mg/kg); **6–50 mg/kg in the Jordanian Dead Sea / wet-mined salts** (Amr & Jabay 2004).
- The mechanism is plausible: Fe(III) complexes with phenolics (chlorogenic acid, tannins) to give
  green/brown/dark discoloration, and Fe is a redox-active metal that can catalyse oxidation. **But no
  study was found that measured Fe-induced discoloration in sauerkraut, kimchi, cucumber pickles or
  pepper mash, and no dose–response was found.**
- The one relevant *negative* datum: in **table olives**, Lanza et al. 2020 found KIO₃ addition
  *lowered* Fe and Mg in the flesh (attributed to Fe(IO₃)₃ and Mg(IO₃)₂ formation) and that **colour
  and sensory properties were not significantly influenced** by the iodized brine (only the b* chroma
  parameter in one cultivar differed).
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7142713/
- **Conclusion: "Fe in unrefined salt discolours fermented vegetables" is mechanistically plausible
  and arithmetically thin (a 2.5 % brine of a 21.7 mg/kg-Fe salt delivers ~0.54 mg Fe/kg) but is
  NOT experimentally demonstrated.** Confidence: **strong as a negative finding**.
- **Also flagged for honesty:** Di Salvo et al. 2023 found **Pb above the maximum level in all 10
  gourmet salts tested**, and elevated **Ni in Persian blue and smoked salts**. That is an
  *evidenced* concern about unrefined salt — unlike the flavour/colour folklore.
