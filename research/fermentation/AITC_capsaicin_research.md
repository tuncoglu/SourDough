## 1.2 AITC MIC / MBC against LACTIC ACID BACTERIA

### The key primary study (this contradicts the "spares LAB" hypothesis)

**Regubalan & Ananthanarayan 2018**, *J Food Sci Technol* 55(9):3417–3426, doi 10.1007/s13197-018-3247-2
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6098797/

Mustard essential oil (declared **AITC 99 %**, Synthite, Mumbai). Broth dilution, ~5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL final inoculum, 37 °C / 48 h, turbidity at 625 nm + plating. All in triplicate.

| Strain | Effect | MIC (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* NCIM 2073 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus plantarum* NCIM 2084 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus casei* NCIM 2364 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Lactobacillus acidophilus* NCIM 2903 | **Biocidal** | **60** |
| *Pediococcus pentosaceus* CFR 2123 | **Biocidal** | **80** |
| *Candida versatilis* CFR 505 (yeast) | **Biocidal** | **40** |

- Mustard EO was the **only** one of 11 essential oils tested that was **cidal** rather than static against LAB. All others (asafoetida, black/white pepper, coriander, clove, cumin, fennel, garlic, ginger, parsley seed) were static at 200–3500 ppm.
- Confidence: **strong** for the numbers (single lab, 5 LAB strains + 1 yeast, triplicate, clear cidal/static discrimination). **Moderate** for extrapolation to real foods.
- Study-quality caveat: commercial EO at "99 % AITC" — other minor constituents not fully characterised; MIC method does not separate static vs cidal by MBC proper.

### Applied corroboration

- **0.1 % (≈1000 ppm) mustard EO in idli batter** (a real LAB-fermented matrix): LAB reduced by **1.11 log₁₀ CFU/g at day 3** (30 °C) and **0.87 log₁₀ at day 30** (4 °C); yeast reduced by **2.41 log₁₀** (30 °C, 5 d) and **1.84 log₁₀** (4 °C, 30 d). Shelf life extended to 5 d at 30 °C, 30 d at 4 °C. Same source as above. Confidence: strong.
- **Shofran et al. 1998** (*J Food Sci* 63(4)): AITC MIC **50–1000 ppm** against bacteria and **1–50 ppm** against yeast. Confidence: moderate — **I could not retrieve the primary paper; this is a secondary citation reproduced in Regubalan 2018.**
- **Ko et al. 2012**, *Int J Food Microbiol* 153(1–2):92–98, doi 10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2011.10.021: 0.1 % encapsulated AITC reduced *L. plantarum* and *L. mesenteroides* growth to **half of control** at 30 °C / 24 h. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22104119/ Confidence: moderate (abstract-level only; ScienceDirect full text is 403-blocked).
- **Inatsu et al. 2005**, *J Food Sci* 70(9): AITC suppressed LAB in fermented Chinese cabbage from **2.85 → 2.0 log₁₀ CFU/g after 4 days at 10 °C**. Confidence: moderate (secondary citation in Regubalan 2018).

### Bottom line for Topic 1

**AITC is clearly inhibitory — indeed biocidal — to *Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, and *Pediococcus* in vitro at 60–80 ppm.** The popular notion that mustard "spares LAB" is **not supported** by the in vitro MIC literature. AITC is *more* potent against the yeast tested (40 ppm) than against LAB (60–80 ppm) — a roughly 2× selectivity margin, not a categorical one. Any "spares LAB" effect must therefore be a *dose* effect (i.e., the AITC concentration actually achieved in a brine or pickle), not a *spectrum* effect.

## 1.6 Mustard in pickling / preserving — does it spare LAB?

This is the weakest-evidenced area of the whole dossier. What the evidence actually shows:

**Against the "spares LAB" idea:**
1. Mustard EO (99 % AITC) is **biocidal to five LAB strains at 60–80 ppm** in broth (§1.2) — a *lower* concentration than for most other essential oils tested.
2. In idli batter, 0.1 % mustard EO measurably **reduced LAB counts** (0.87–1.11 log₁₀) and **raised pH / lowered titratable acidity** versus control. Regubalan & Anantharayan 2018.
3. In kimchi, 0.2 % AITC gave **significantly higher pH** than control at 4 and 10 °C over 15 days (Ko et al. 2012, secondary citation).

**Complicating cultural evidence — mustard is itself a fermentation substrate, not only an inhibitor:**
- **Gundruk** (Nepal/India/Sikkim): *B. juncea* mustard leaves, **no salt**, pressed airtight, fermented **15–22 days**, then sun-dried 2–4 days. Dominant LAB: *L. fermentum*, *L. plantarum*, *L. casei*, *Pediococcus pentosaceus*.
- **Inziangsang** (Nagaland/Manipur): mustard leaves (*B. juncea*), 20–30 °C, **7–10 days**, LAB: *L. plantarum*, *L. brevis*, *Pediococcus*.
- **Sayur asin** (Indonesia): mustard + cabbage + salt + boiled-rice liquid; *L. mesenteroides*, *L. plantarum*, *P. pentosaceus*.
- **Khalpi** (Nepal, cucumber): fermented 3–5 days, and mustard oil is added **after** fermentation as a pickle dressing — not during.
- Source: Swain et al. 2014, *Biotechnol Res Int* 2014:250424, doi 10.1155/2014/250424, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4058509/
- **Interpretation caveat:** mustard *leaves* are low in sinigrin (sinigrin is not a major leaf glucosinolate per Lietzow 2021), so these fermentations do not constitute a strong test of AITC's effect on LAB.

**DATA NOT FOUND — explicit flags:**
- **No controlled quantitative study found** measuring mustard seed or mustard powder addition to **cucumber brine** on fermentation rate (days to pH target, LAB counts).
- **No study found** on mustard/AITC in **sauerkraut** with LAB-rate endpoints. (Shelef 1983 is repeatedly cited as claiming AITC inhibits *A. aceti*, *S. ellipsoideus*, *S. cerevisiae*, *Mycoderma vini* "in pickles and sauerkraut" — but this is a 1983 secondary review citation that I could not retrieve, and it concerns **spoilage yeasts and acetic acid bacteria, not LAB**.)
- **No data found on AITC or mustard vs *Oenococcus oeni* or malolactic fermentation in wine**, despite multiple targeted searches (Europe PMC, PubMed, general web). This appears to be a genuine gap.

## 1.7 Quantitative effect of AITC on vegetable fermentation rate

| Finding | System | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AITC suppressed LAB from **2.85 → 2.0 log₁₀ CFU/g after 4 days at 10 °C** | Fermented Chinese cabbage | Inatsu et al. 2005, via Regubalan 2018 (secondary) | Moderate |
| **0.2 % AITC** in kimchi stored at 4 and 10 °C for 15 days gave **significantly higher pH** than control | Kimchi | Ko et al. 2012, *Int J Food Microbiol* 153(1–2):92–98 (secondary) | Moderate |
| **0.1 % mustard EO** → higher pH and lower titratable acidity than control for 5 d at 30 °C and 30 d at 4 °C; effect **lost after day 5 at 30 °C** | Idli batter | Regubalan & Ananthanarayan 2018 | Strong |
| **Mechanistic caveat:** AITC is **more stable at 4 °C** than at 28–30 °C, and its antimicrobial activity is **pH-dependent, effective at neutral pH**; inhibition of *L. monocytogenes* at neutral pH was **3× higher than at pH 5.0** over 10 d at 4 °C | — | Olaimat & Holley 2016, via Regubalan 2018 | Moderate (secondary) — **this is the single most important practical caveat**: fermenting vegetables acidify rapidly, which suppresses AITC efficacy |

**Synthesis for Topic 1:** AITC does slow LAB-driven vegetable fermentation when dosed at ≥0.1 % / ≥60–80 ppm, but the effect is self-limiting because the pH drop it fails to prevent destroys its own activity. In a real pickle brine with mustard seed or powder, the achieved AITC concentration is likely well below the 60–80 ppm broth MIC unless seed is used at high loading — and I found **no study that measures that concentration directly in a pickle brine**.

# TOPIC 2 — CAPSAICIN / CAPSAICINOIDS

## 2.1 Concentrations and the SHU conversion

### The SHU-to-ppm conversion — verified, with a correction

Your proposed "1 ppm ≈ 15–16 SHU" is **correct in magnitude but the precise factor is 16.1, not 15**, and it is **compound-specific**:

| Statement | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **SHU = capsaicin (mg/kg) × 16** | Frontiers in Plant Science 2026, PMC13190488 | Strong |
| Conversion coefficient **16.1 × 10³ for capsaicin AND dihydrocapsaicin**; **9.3 × 10³ for other capsaicinoids** (concentration in g/kg) — i.e. **16.1 SHU per ppm** for cap/dihydrocap, **9.3 SHU per ppm** for the rest | *Current Research in Food Science* 2023, doi 10.1016/j.crfs.2022.100411, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9735263/ ; and *Food Chemistry: X* 2024, doi 10.1016/j.fochx.2024.101566, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11245976/ | Strong |
| **"16 SHUs is equivalent to a capsaicin content of 1 mg/kg"** | *Case Reports in Medicine* 2020, doi 10.1155/2020/3610401, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7330639/ | Moderate |
| A commonly used composite formula: **SHU = M × 0.9 × (16.1 × 10³) + M × 0.1 × (9.3 × 10³)**, where M = total capsaicinoids in g/kg (i.e. assuming 90 % capsaicin+dihydrocapsaicin, 10 % others) | *Molecules* 2024, doi 10.3390/molecules29235767, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11643616/ | Moderate |
| Pungency degree: **1° = 150 SHU** | *Curr Res Food Sci* 2023 (above) | Moderate |

**So: the correct working figure is 16 (16.1) SHU per ppm capsaicin/dihydrocapsaicin, and ~9.3 SHU per ppm of the minor capsaicinoids.** The "15" figure in circulation is a rounded approximation; it is *not* the analytical standard.

**DATA NOT FOUND — important flag:** I could **not** retrieve the authoritative primary text of **ASTA Method 21.3** or **AOAC Official Method 995.03** ("Capsaicinoids in Capsicums and their Extractives, Liquid Chromatographic Method"). The AOAC method PDF exists at https://catarina.udlap.mx/u_dl_a/tales/documentos/mqi/mendez_m_ad/apendiceC.pdf (fetch failed) and the ASTA presentation is member-gated at https://astaspice.org/resources/presentation-rethinking-heat-an-accuracy-update-to-the-scoville-heat-units-shu-methodology-asta-method-21-3 . **The 16.1 / 9.3 coefficients above are from peer-reviewed papers citing the standard, not from the standard itself.** Treat the "ASTA/AOAC authoritative" framing as unverified.

## 2.2 Capsaicin MIC against lactic acid bacteria

**EXPLICIT NEGATIVE FINDING:** after multiple systematic searches (Europe PMC REST API, NCBI PMC full-text search, PubMed, general web), **I could not find a single peer-reviewed MIC or MBC value for capsaicin against any *Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, *Pediococcus* or *Weissella* strain in µg/mL.** This is a genuine gap in the literature, not a search failure — capsaicin's antimicrobial testing has focused on pathogens.

The available evidence **indirectly and consistently indicates capsaicin does NOT inhibit LAB at realistic food concentrations**:

1. **Kimchi capsaicinoid dose–response (the best available evidence):** Kimchi prepared with capsaicinoids at **4, 12, 30.7, 40.9 and 50.3 mg/kg**, stored 4 °C for 28 days:
   - **pH and titratable acidity did NOT differ significantly among capsaicinoid concentrations.**
   - Day 0 pH 5.36–5.52; all samples except the 50.3 mg/kg one fell **below pH 4.0** by day 28.
   - LAB abundance **rapidly increased in all samples** until day 21, then decreased — LAB were *not* suppressed.
   - Total viable bacteria rose from 4.24–4.52 to a peak of **6.98–7.11 log CFU/g at day 14**.
   - Lactic acid was **highest** in the *high*-capsaicinoid kimchi; acetic acid highest in the *low*-capsaicinoid kimchi.
   - Capsaicinoid concentration did **not** change over 28 days of fermentation.
   - Source: *Heliyon* 2024, doi 10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24441, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10831603/
   - **Confidence: moderate.** ⚠️ **FLAG — internal inconsistency:** the abstract and highlights claim *"Capsaicinoids lower pH and raise titratable acidity in kimchi samples"*, but the Results section states *"pH and titratable acidity did not significantly differ with capsaicinoid concentration among kimchi samples."* I am reporting the **Results** text as the finding. Cite this paper carefully.

2. **Commercial kimchi correlation study:** across 13 commercial kimchi samples, capsaicinoid content was **positively correlated with total acidity** and **negatively correlated with pH** — i.e. higher capsaicinoid kimchi was *more* acidic, the opposite of inhibition. **Zero correlation** was found between free sugar content and capsaicinoid content. Free sugar content exerted the **greatest direct and indirect effect on perceived spiciness intensity**. Source: *Foods* 2021, 10(1):86, doi 10.3390/foods1001086, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7829842/ Confidence: moderate (n = 13, correlational, not causal).

3. **Capsaicin-tolerant LAB exist:** a Chinese-language study screened and characterised capsaicin-tolerant LAB from pickled pepper juice (https://d.wanfangdata.com.cn/periodical/... and http://sf1970.cnif.cn/EN/10.13995/j.cnki.11-1802/ts.037764), and a Chinese patent (CN116555097A) claims a *Lactobacillus plantarum* strain "capable of resisting capsaicin stress" (https://patents.google.com/patent/CN116555097A/en). Confidence: weak (titles/abstracts only; the existence of tolerant strains implies selection pressure but is not an MIC).

## 2.4 Does chilli slow or not slow kimchi fermentation? — the decisive comparison

There are two well-designed studies, and **they answer different questions**:

### Study A: red pepper POWDER (with vs without) → SLOWS fermentation

**Jeong et al. 2013**, *Int J Food Microbiol* 160(3):252–259, doi 10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2012.10.015
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23290236/ (ScienceDirect full text 403-blocked)

- Baechu (Chinese cabbage) and Mu (radish) kimchi, **with and without red pepper powder**, monitored **40 days**.
- **"The lag phases of kimchi fermentation were clearly extended by the addition of red pepper powder."**
- Bacterial diversity decreased more slowly with red pepper powder.
- Communities dominated by *Leuconostoc* and *Lactobacillus* in all kimchi. **Without** red pepper, *Weissella* abundance was **negligible**. **With** red pepper, *Weissella* proportions were **much higher**, while *Leuconostoc* and *Lactobacillus* were **evidently lower**.
- ¹H NMR metabolite analysis confirmed fermentation progressed **"a little more slowly"** with red pepper powder.
- PCA supported slowing, **especially during the early fermentation period**.
- Confidence: **strong** for the direction; **moderate** for magnitude — exact pH/day values are behind the paywall.

### Study B: capsaicinoid CONCENTRATION at constant red pepper powder → NO significant effect

**Heliyon 2024**, doi 10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e24441, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10831603/
(Detailed in §2.2 above.) Four red pepper powders were blended so that **total red pepper powder was held at 0.6 kg** in every batch, varying only capsaicinoid concentration (4 → 50.3 mg/kg). Result: **no significant pH or TA difference between treatments.** Only the LAB *community composition* changed (e.g. *W. koreensis* was highest at 4 mg/kg).

### Resolution — which effect wins?

**The two studies together suggest the fermentation-slowing effect of red pepper powder is NOT primarily a capsaicinoid effect.** In Study B, when red pepper mass was held constant and only capsaicinoids varied, the pH/TA effect vanished. Therefore the slowing seen in Study A is plausibly attributable to:
- the native microbiota carried on the red pepper powder,
- non-capsaicinoid antimicrobials in the powder,
- dilution/buffering of the cabbage by inert powder mass, or
- the physical matrix effect of the powder.

**The competing accelerating force is sugar, and it is large.** Gochugaru contains **16.76–29.92 % free sugar** (Ku 2012) and up to **19.1 %** (Choi 2000) — a substantial fermentable carbohydrate load. And *Foods* 2021 found **zero correlation** between free sugar and capsaicinoid content, meaning sugar and heat are independent variables that kimchi makers can and do decouple.

**And the tradition appears to have solved this:** the gochugaru type **preferentially used for kimchi (Youngyang) is the HIGHEST in free sugar and LOWEST in capsaicinoid** (Choi 2000). That is the opposite of what you would choose if capsaicin were the desired preservative — and exactly what you would choose if you wanted reliable, fast, well-flavoured fermentation.

**Honest bottom line:** capsaicin at realistic kimchi concentrations (4–50 mg/kg) does not measurably slow kimchi fermentation or inhibit its LAB. Red pepper *powder* does slow early fermentation, but by a mechanism that the current evidence does not support as capsaicinoid-mediated.

**DATA NOT FOUND — explicit flags:**
- **No study found** giving days-to-target-pH or days-to-target-TA for kimchi **with vs without** red pepper in a form I could extract numerically. Jeong 2013 has this data; it is paywalled.
- **No quantitative data found** on **Tabasco-style pepper mash** fermentation kinetics with vs without red pepper.
- **No quantitative data found** on **gochujang** fermentation rate vs capsaicinoid content.
- **No study found** isolating the effect of red pepper powder's **native microbiota** versus its **capsaicinoids** on kimchi fermentation — this is the obvious missing experiment.
