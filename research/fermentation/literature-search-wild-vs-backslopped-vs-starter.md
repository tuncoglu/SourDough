# Spontaneous (wild) vs backslopping vs defined starter cultures — lactic-fermented vegetables

Literature search. Every number below was retrieved from a page/PDF I actually opened; evidence
level is stated per item. No number is inferred or invented. Derived values are flagged.

---

## (a) Head-to-head studies

### A1. Johanningsmeier, McFeeters, Fleming & Thompson 2007 — sauerkraut, wild vs *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* starter
- **Source:** J. Food Sci. 72(5):M166–M172. USDA-ARS Food Science Research Unit / NC State, Raleigh NC.
  doi:10.1111/j.1750-3841.2007.00372.x — full PDF retrieved: https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/60701000/Pickle%20Pubs/p348.pdf
- **Design:** 3×2 factorial. 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.0 % NaCl (wt/wt) × no starter vs *L. mesenteroides* LA 81 (ATCC 8293) at **10⁶ CFU/g**. 1-gal jars, **18 °C**, **n = 3 jars per treatment**, 4 cabbage lots. Plate counts + HPLC + trained descriptive panel (10 panelists, 9 h training).
- **Baseline microflora of raw cabbage (Table 2):** LAB **3.0–3.6 log CFU/g**; total aerobes 4.6–5.1; Enterobacteriaceae 4.2–4.6. Starter at 10⁶ CFU/g is "approximately 2 logs higher than the natural microflora."
- **Lag phase:** not reported numerically (pH curves are figures only).
- **Time to pH 4.0 / pH 3.5:** **not reported numerically** — stated only as "the decline in pH was rapid and more uniform at all 3 salt concentrations in fermentations started by the addition of *L. mesenteroides*," vs "highly variable among jars without starter culture" during the first 6 d.
- **Total fermentation time:** sampled at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 6, 10, 17 d, then held 10 months.
- **Final pH after 10 mo at 18 °C (Table 3):** natural 0.5 % = 3.49; 1.0 % = 3.44; 2.0 % = 3.44 — starter 0.5 % = 3.47; 1.0 % = 3.46; 2.0 % = 3.42. **Not significantly different (P = 0.75).**
- **Final titratable acidity proxy — lactic acid (mM):** natural 144.1 / 149.3 / 135.3; starter 141.0 / 141.5 / 131.5. Acetic acid 69.0 / 59.8 / 48.4 vs 57.0 / 60.1 / 53.0. Mannitol 86.7 / 88.5 / 82.9 vs 99.7 / 102.1 / 94.1.
- **Days saved by starter:** **not measured / not stated.** The paper's claim is about *uniformity, firmness and off-flavour reduction*, not time saved.
- **Quality-of-evidence: STRONG** for "starter makes acidification more uniform and preserves texture at reduced salt"; **WEAK/absent** for any "days saved" number. Unfunded academic/USDA; no industry funding declared. Limitation: pH kinetics only in figures.

### A2. Liu Y. et al. 2023 — commercial northeast-China sauerkraut, starter vs no starter
- **Source:** Foods 12(6):1164. doi:10.3390/foods12061164 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC10048197).
- **Design:** 4 retail sauerkraut products sampled: **ZLL = naturally fermented, no starter**; LLS = *L. paracasei*; HX = *L. plantarum*; QM = *L. plantarum* + *L. acidophilus*. n = 3 parallel samples per product. 16S rRNA + UPLC + GC-IMS.
- **pH (endpoint, Table 2):** ZLL 3.80 (highest) | LLS 3.67 | HX 3.59 (lowest) | QM 3.62.
- **Total acid (g/kg):** ZLL 9.00 | LLS 10.98 | HX 14.06 | QM 10.44.
- **Lag phase / time-to-pH / days saved:** **not determinable** — single time point on retail products.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** Confounded: different brands, raw materials, recipes, fermentation times and temperatures. No time course. Good for "starter products ended more acidic," not for kinetics.

### A3. Hu W. et al. 2024 — northeast sauerkraut, spontaneous vs *Levilactobacillus brevis*
- **Source:** Food Chemistry: X (2024). **Open access**, full text retrieved (PMC11068551).
- **Design:** spontaneous control (CS) vs *L. brevis* inoculation (LB), 30-day fermentation, sampling at 0/5/10/20/25/30 d. 16S rRNA, organic acids, GC-IMS volatiles, sensory.
- **Total fermentation time:** 30 d monitored.
- **Time to pH 4.0 / 3.5 / lag phase:** **only in figures (Fig. 1A)** — no numeric values in text.
- **Final lactic acid:** LB **13.56 mg/g** vs CS **10.61 mg/g** (P < 0.05).
- **Nitrite:** CS peaked 11.43 mg/kg on day 10 → 0.56 mg/kg day 30; LB fell steadily to 0.14 mg/kg day 30 (LB always lower).
- **Days saved:** paper states inoculation "shortened the fermentation time" and "accelerated the acidification" — **no number given**.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** n = 3, real cabbage, full time course — but the headline kinetic claim is qualitative; the quantitative claim is a stated reduction in "fermentation time" without a value.

### A4. Zhao X., Liu L., Zhao Y., Wang D., Zhang X., Jin X., Wang L. & Liu X. 2026 — *L. mesenteroides* AA001 starter vs natural, three vegetables
- **Source:** Microorganisms 14(2):411. doi:10.3390/microorganisms14020411 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC12943457). Authorship verified against the Europe PMC record, not inferred.
- **Design:** natural fermentation (NF) vs inoculation with *L. mesenteroides* AA001 at **1.0 × 10⁶ CFU per gram fresh weight**. Three matrices: fermented Yingcai (*Lepidium* sp.), Dongbei suancai (Chinese cabbage), radish kimchi (white radish). Maturity defined objectively as **pH ≤ 4.0 AND total acidity ≥ 6 g/kg AND LAB ≥ 10⁹ CFU/g**. 10 trained assessors (ICC ≥ 0.80); triplicate.
- **Lag phase:** not reported as such. In pure MRS broth the strain reduced pH below 4.0 within 24 h (model medium, *not* the vegetable).
- **Time to pH 4.0 (the maturity criterion):** NF **9–14 days**; starter **4–7 days** (Yingcai model).
- **Time to pH 3.5:** not reported.
- **Total fermentation time:** NF 9–14 d; starter 4–7 d.
- **Days saved by the starter: 5–7 days.**
- **Sensory maturity:** starter **5–7 d** vs natural **11–14 d**.
- **Nitrite peak:** starter ≤ 10 mg/kg on days 2–3; natural 20–46 mg/kg on days 3–6 (Chinese limit 20 mg/kg).
- **Final titratable acidity:** reported as ≥ 6 g/kg at maturity (criterion, not a measured final mean). No % lactic acid figure in text.
- **Quality-of-evidence: STRONG for a single-strain study.** Explicit numeric maturity criteria, three real vegetable matrices, replicated, trained panel, IRB-approved. Caveats: one strain only; no backslopping arm; commercial/safety endpoints partly industry-relevant (Sichuan Academy of Food and Fermentation Industries is a co-author — a state research institute, not a starter supplier).

### A5. Peñas, Frias, Sidro & Vidal-Valverde 2010 — sauerkraut, natural vs induced at two salt levels
- **Source:** J. Agric. Food Chem. 58(6):3549–3557. doi:10.1021/jf903739a — **abstract only retrieved** (PubMed/Europe PMC PMID 20170112); full text paywalled.
- **Design:** white cabbage cv. Bronco (eastern Spain), **0.5 % and 1.5 % NaCl** × spontaneous vs induced fermentation with *L. mesenteroides*, *L. plantarum*, or a mixed starter. Storage at 4 °C for 1–3 months.
- **Reported values:** ascorbigen rose from 14 µmol/100 g dm (raw) to **63–137 µmol/100 g dm**; vitamin C fell from 354 to **236–277 mg/100 g dm**. *L. mesenteroides* at 0.5 % NaCl gave highest ascorbigen + most vitamin C. After storage, *L. mesenteroides* sauerkraut retained 74–82 µmol/100 g dm ascorbigen and 33–44 mg/100 g dm vitamin C.
- **Lag / time-to-pH / days saved:** **not in the abstract — not retrieved.**
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** Genuine starter-vs-natural head-to-head, but the published endpoint is micronutrients and sensory ("no differences in overall acceptability between natural fermentations and those performed with starter cultures"), not acidification speed.

### A6. Kim M.J., Lee H.W., Lee M.E., Roh S.W., Kim T.W. 2019 — kimchi, mixed starter *vs* no starter (starter SLOWED fermentation)
- **Source:** J. Microbiol. 57(4). doi:10.1007/s12275-019-9048-0 — **abstract only retrieved** (PMID 31073899).
- **Design:** mixed starter of *Lactococcus lactis* WiKim0098 + *Leuconostoc citreum* WiKim0096 vs no starter; **12 days at 10 °C**.
- **Time to pH 4.2: the starter kimchi took ~1.5-fold LONGER than the control kimchi.** (Starter kimchi had a lower initial pH and higher LAB at day 0, yet reached pH 4.2 more slowly.)
- **Total fermentation time:** 12 d monitored.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** Replicated lab kimchi, but n not stated in the abstract, and this is a **counter-example to the "starter always speeds things up" assumption** — the authors' stated goal was *extending shelf-life*.

### A7. Choi S.J., Yang S.Y., Yoon K.S. 2021 — kimchi, LAB starter vs natural at 1 % and 2.5 % salt
- **Source:** Food Microbiology 99:103868. doi:10.1016/j.fm.2021.103868 — **abstract only retrieved** (PMID 34416967).
- **Design:** *L. mesenteroides* KCTC 13374 + *L. plantarum* KCTC 33133 as starters; 1 % and 2.5 % salt; fermentation at **10 °C and 25 °C**; >15 days.
- **Titratable acidity: naturally fermented kimchi 0.65 %; starter-fermented kimchi 1.0 %.**
- **Time to pH 4.0 / 3.5 / days saved:** not in abstract.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE** (single abstract-level datum; strong journal, but time-axis values not retrieved).

### A8. Kim T.W. group 2025 — kimchi, four *Leuconostoc* starters at 10⁷ CFU/g vs saline control
- **Source:** npj Science of Food (2025). **Open access**, full text retrieved (PMC12032216).
- **Design:** *L. mesenteroides* WiKim32 / WiKim33 / WiKim0121 and *Leuconostoc* sp. CBA3628, each at **10⁷ CFU/g kimchi**; control (CTR) = 0.9 % saline. 6 h pre-ferment at room temp, then **5 °C for 33 days**; sampling at 0/3/6/11/22/33 d. **Triplicate** (15 samples total). Multi-omics.
- **Outcome:** "Higher LAB amounts in the starter samples caused greater acidification from day 0 to 22, resulting in a **faster decrease in pH than in CTR**." After day 11, LAB counts in starter samples exceeded CTR by **over 70×**.
- **pH values reported across the whole study: range 4.06–5.14** (from the heatmap scale bar). No numeric time-to-threshold.
- **Time to pH 4.0 / 3.5 / days saved:** not given.
- **Quality-of-evidence: STRONG design** (triplicate, defined dose, 5 °C, multi-omics), but the kinetic outcome is qualitative. Note the 5 °C temperature makes this a storage-stability study, not a production-speed study.

### A9. Etchells, Anderson & Bell 1964 — pure-culture (defined starter) cucumber fermentations
- **Source:** Applied Microbiology 12(6):523–535. USDA Fermentation Laboratory, Raleigh NC. **Abstract retrieved** (PMC1058172); PDF blocked (406/403 from PMC and ARS mirrors).
- **Design:** cucumbers sterilised by gamma radiation (0.83–1.00 Mrad) or hot-water blanching (66–80 °C, 5 min), then inoculated with single species. Nine species tested. Brine 5.4–5.6 % NaCl (and 2.5–3.0 % for one test).
- **Concrete values retrieved:**
  - *L. delbrueckii* in cucumbers brined at **2.5–3.0 % salt produced sufficient acid in about 30 h at 48 °C to reduce brine pH from above 7.0 to below 4.0.**
  - *P. cerevisiae*, *L. plantarum* and *L. brevis* reached the highest populations and the lowest pH at 5.4–5.6 % NaCl, and dominated in that species order when co-inoculated (matching natural succession).
  - Rates of growth/acid production increased from 21 → 27 → 32 °C, **but maximal populations and acidities were essentially the same at each temperature.**
  - Salt tolerance limit ~8 % for those three species; 2.5–4.0 % for the thermophiles tested.
- **Time to pH 4.0:** 30 h — but only for *L. delbrueckii* at 48 °C, an atypical strain/temperature, in a sterilised model system.
- **Quality-of-evidence: STRONG within its design** (controlled pure cultures, replicated), but **not a wild-vs-starter head-to-head** — the indigenous flora was killed first, so there is no spontaneous control. Historical (1964); no industry funding evident.

### A10. Nabors 1968 — sauerkraut pre-fermentation inoculation (historical negative result)
- **Source:** MS thesis, Utah State University. https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/etd/5181/ — **abstract retrieved**, PDF 403.
- **Finding quoted from the abstract:** "Sauerkraut inoculated with *L. plantarum* was **slower in fermenting and significantly lower in acidity and higher in pH**" (compared with *L. mesenteroides* inoculation). Dehydration (freeze / hot-air / microwave) then caused losses of ascorbic acid, lactic acid and volatiles and raised pH.
- **Quality-of-evidence: WEAK.** 1968 master's thesis, scanned, no replication details retrievable, no numeric times in the abstract. Listed because it is a rare explicit "starter was *slower*" result and is consistent with A6.

### A11. Wiander & Ryhänen 2005 — white cabbage, commercial starters + low-NaCl mineral salt
- **Source:** European Food Research & Technology 220:319–323. doi:10.1007/s00217-004-1080-5 — **abstract only retrieved** (Europe PMC); full text paywalled.
- **Design:** commercial starters in various combinations + mineral salt (57 % NaCl, 28 % KCl, 12 % MgSO₄, 2 % lysine·HCl, 1 % SiO₂) at **0.5 % w/w NaCl** on sliced cabbage. Both laboratory and **large-scale** trials; sauerkraut juice pressed. Taste panel.
- **Retrieved statement:** "The pH decreased rapidly in the beginning of the fermentations, ensuring an accurate start of the process"; *L. mesenteroides* + mineral salt gave the mildest juice.
- **Lag / time-to-pH / days saved / final TA: NONE in the abstract — not retrieved.** This is the key gap in the Finnish dataset.

### A12. Tolonen et al. 2002 & 2004 — Finnish sauerkraut, starter vs spontaneous
- **Sources:**
  - Tolonen M, Taipale M, Viander B, Pihlava J-M, Korhonen H, Ryhänen E-L. 2002. J. Agric. Food Chem. 50(24):6798–6803. doi:10.1021/jf0109017 — abstract retrieved (PMID 12405778).
  - Tolonen M, Rajaniemi S, Pihlava J-M, Johansson T, Saris P.E.J., Ryhänen E-L. 2004. Food Microbiology 21(2):167–179. doi:10.1016/S0740-0020(03)00058-3 — abstract retrieved.
- **2002 study:** cabbage fermented with a starter compared to spontaneous fermentation. "Glucosinolates were **totally decomposed in both fermentations during two weeks**." Total glucosinolate in raw material 3.71 µmol/g DW; kaempferol 0.9 mg/kg FW. Nitrite below detection in both.
- **2004 study:** starters tested were *L. plantarum*, *L. sakei*, *L. mesenteroides*, *P. pentosaceus*, *Lc. lactis* N8, *Lc. lactis* LAC67. *Lc. lactis* N8 produced up to **1400 IU/mL nisin in cabbage brine within 24 h**, still detectable after 13 d (**250 IU/mL**). *L. sakei* produced 2–3× the total glucosinolate breakdown products of other strains.
- **Lag / time-to-pH / days saved / final TA: NOT in either abstract — not retrieved.**
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE** for the phytochemical comparison, **zero** for fermentation-speed numbers (not published in the retrievable text).
- ⚠️ Note: the 2004 author list ends "Saris PEJ" — one database record renders it "Saris PEJ"; the commonly cited form is **Saris P.E.J.** Verify before citing.

## (b) Inoculum size / dose–response

### B1. Nguyen T.V. et al. 2026 — carrot juice, 1 % vs 2 % vs 3 % v/v inoculum (the only true dose–response found)
- **Source:** Food Science & Nutrition (2026). doi:10.1002/fsn3.71917 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC13184175).
- **Design:** *Lactiplantibacillus*/*Lactobacillus* strain TO35, standardised to **~10⁹ CFU/mL**, inoculated at **1 %, 2 %, 3 % (v/v)**; 6 TSS levels (9–24 °Brix); 30 °C; 48 h comparative endpoint plus a 12/24/36/48/60/72 h time course. **n = 3**, mean ± SD, one-way ANOVA + LSD.
- **Measured, 48 h, at 15 °Brix (Table 3):**
  | Inoculum | Lactic acid (g/L) | pH | Viable count (log CFU/mL) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 % | 12.07 ± 0.11 | 3.33 ± 0.01 | 9.76 ± 0.06 |
  | 2 % | 12.75 ± 0.27 | 3.29 ± 0.02 | 9.76 ± 0.05 |
  | 3 % | **14.10 ± 0.27** | **3.18 ± 0.02** | 9.89 ± 0.07 |
- **At 9 °Brix:** 1 % → 10.88 g/L, pH 3.53; 2 % → 10.35 g/L, pH 3.49; 3 % → 11.48 g/L, pH 3.39.
- **At 24 °Brix (osmotic stress):** 1 % → 7.65 g/L, pH 3.97; 2 % → 8.16 g/L, pH 3.86; 3 % → 9.53 g/L, pH 3.66.
- **Is the response proportional? NO — sub-proportional and non-monotonic.** Tripling the inoculum (1 → 3 %) gave only **+16.8 %** lactic acid at 15 °Brix, **+5.5 %** at 9 °Brix, and **+24.6 %** at 24 °Brix. At 9 °Brix the 2 % treatment was *worse* than 1 %. The authors state larger inocula help most where substrate/osmotic stress limits growth: "at very high TSS values (21°–24°Brix), increasing the inoculum did not fully overcome the inhibitory effects of osmotic stress."
- **Fermentation time:** 24 h gave the best sensory score; 48–72 h over-acidified.
- **Lag phase:** not reported.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** n = 3, replicated, real dose levels — but this is **carrot JUICE (a model/liquid medium), not whole fermented vegetables**, one strain, and the strain was identified only phenotypically (authors' own stated limitation). No CFU-dose (10⁵/10⁶/10⁷) series.

### B2. Zhang S. et al. 2023 — pickled chili pepper, backslopped aged brine vs fresh brine (measured backslopping effect)
- **Source:** Foods 12(1):101. doi:10.3390/foods12010101 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC9818826).
- **Design:** large-scale industrial fermentation by Yunnan Hongbin Green Food Group. Four procedures: **PFB** pool + fresh brine, **PAB** pool + aged brine, **JFB** jar + fresh brine, **JAB** jar + aged brine. **Aged brine = fresh brine : reused brine at 1:4, i.e. 80 % v/v reused brine** (the highest backslopping proportion found in any study). 8.16 % NaCl, 0.22 % CaCl₂, 0.63 % acetic acid. **20–25 °C for 30 days**; sampling at 0/1/4/7/14/30 d; **n = 3**.
- **pH at 30 d (measured):** PFB 3.96 ± 0.02 | PAB 3.92 ± 0.02 | JFB 3.79 ± 0.00 | **JAB 3.71 ± 0.01**.
- **pH fell significantly within 7 days (P < 0.05) and then stabilised** in all four.
- **First-order fractional-conversion rate constants k (measured, R² > 0.90):** PFB **0.39** | PAB **0.41** | JFB **0.62** | **JAB 0.69** (units d⁻¹ as reported).
- **Effect size (derived by me from the paper's k values):**
  - Backslopping effect, same container: pool **+5.1 %** (0.39 → 0.41); jar **+11.3 %** (0.62 → 0.69).
  - Container effect, same brine: fresh **+59 %** (0.39 → 0.62); aged **+68 %** (0.41 → 0.69).
  - → **At an 80 % v/v backslopping rate, the acceleration was modest and much smaller than the vessel effect.**
- **Time to pH 4.0 / 3.5:** **cannot be computed from retrieved text** — C₀ and C∞ appear only inside a figure box. Any "days saved" figure would be extrapolation, so none is given.
- **Final titratable acidity:** reported as organic-acid profiles; JAB had the highest total organic acids, PAB the lowest acetic acid at 30 d. No % lactic acid figure in text.
- **Sugar consumed over 30 d:** PFB 20.30 %, PAB 16.34 %, JFB 43.43 %, JAB 1.72 %.
- **Quality-of-evidence: STRONG for a backslopping comparison.** n = 3, industrial scale, four parallel arms isolating both brine and vessel, kinetic model fit reported. Caveats: aged brines were sourced from a working pickle cellar (undefined age/composition) and the reused brine itself carries pre-formed acid, so part of the pH difference is inoculation *plus* carried-over acidity; also a **direct comparison against a defined commercial starter is absent.**

### B3. Wang C. et al. 2026 — pickled chili, aged brine of 0/5/15/25/50 years
- **Source:** Foods 15(14):2564. doi:10.3390/foods15142564 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC13408934).
- **Inoculum proportion (measured):** **50 mL aged brine into a total 250 mL liquid volume = 20 % v/v reused brine** (18 g salt in 200 mL water + 50 mL aged brine), 120 g peppers, 9 % w/v salinity, 31 days at 20–25 °C, **n = 5**.
- **Reported:** brine age correlated with flavour/metabolite and bacterial-community shifts; older brine (Y25, Y50) gave lower diversity dominated by *Levilactobacillus*.
- **pH / time-to-pH / days saved: NOT measured in this study.** The paper cites prior work that aged brine "may shorten fermentation cycles" but does not test it.
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE–STRONG for the designed comparison (n = 5)**, but **it is not a fermentation-speed study.** Useful mainly as a real-world backslopping dose (20 % v/v). Limitation stated by the authors: initial brine composition was not characterised, so metabolite differences are a "brine-age-associated composite effect."

### B4. Liu Z. et al. 2020 — radish pickle, old-brine inoculation, time to pH 3.5
- **Source:** Frontiers in Microbiology 11:445. doi:10.3389/fmicb.2020.00445 — **open access**, full text retrieved (PMC7146078).
- **Design:** 3 kg radish per 10 L jar (glass GL / porcelain PO / plastic PL); **30 mL of old brine (6 % salt)** added to each jar plus cold boiled water with 6 % NaCl; room temperature 22–25 °C; **pH measured daily for the first 12 days**; **n = 3**.
- **Time to pH 3.5 (measured):** **day 4** in glass and plastic jars; **day 5** in porcelain jars.
- **Nitrite peak:** day 2; 59.73 mg/kg in porcelain (significantly higher than the other two).
- **Lactic acid at day 12:** 18.04 g/L (plastic) and 10.89 g/L (porcelain).
- **Days saved:** **cannot be stated — there is no no-backslopping control. All jars received old brine.**
- **Inoculum %:** not computable — the total brine volume per jar is not reported; only "30 mL of old brine."
- ⚠️ **This is the single best time-to-pH-3.5 value I found for a backslopped vegetable, but it is not head-to-head.**
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE.** n = 3, real radish, daily pH — but the study variable was the *container*, not the inoculum, and there is no spontaneous control.

### B5. Hu Y.-Y. et al. 2024 — Taiwanese pickled cabbage, backslopping second round vs spontaneous first round
- **Source:** J. Sci. Food Agric. 104(12). doi:10.1002/jsfa.13688 (PMID 38925544) — **abstract only retrieved**; no PMC deposit.
- **Method:** quantitative microbiome profiling (16S rRNA gene copies normalised by qPCR).
- **Measured microbial loads:** spontaneous first-round fermentation total bacteria **6.1 → 10 log copies/mL**; backslopped second-round fermentation **7.6 → 9.9 log copies/mL**. Adding backslopping starter + vinegar rapidly produced a homogeneous community with *Lactiplantibacillus* held at **~90 %** throughout; without vinegar, *Leuconostoc* dominated.
- **Implication:** the backslopped batch began at **7.6 vs 6.1 log copies/mL ≈ 30× higher initial bacterial load** than the spontaneous batch.
- **pH / time-to-pH / titratable acidity / days saved: NOT in the abstract — not retrieved.**
- **Quality-of-evidence: MODERATE** for the load/succession data (qPCR-based absolute quantification is a genuine methodological strength); **absent** for speed-of-acidification numbers.

### B6. Reference inoculum levels used across the retrieved head-to-head studies (dose landscape)
| Study | Vegetable | Inoculum level | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Johanningsmeier 2007 | sauerkraut | 10⁶ CFU/g | defined starter, ~2 logs above natural LAB (3.0–3.6 log CFU/g) |
| Zhao 2026 (AA001) | Yingcai / suancai / radish kimchi | 1.0 × 10⁶ CFU/g fresh weight | defined starter |
| Kim 2025 (npj Sci Food) | kimchi | 10⁷ CFU/g | defined starter ×4 strains |
| Nguyen 2026 | carrot juice | 1 / 2 / 3 % v/v of ~10⁹ CFU/mL | defined starter (dose series) |
| Zhang 2023 | pickled chili | 80 % v/v reused brine (1:4 fresh:aged) | **backslopping** |
| Wang 2026 | pickled chili | 20 % v/v aged brine | **backslopping** |
| Liu 2020 | radish pickle | 30 mL old brine per jar (≈3 kg radish) | **backslopping** (volume fraction unreportable) |

**Bottom line for (b):** I found **no study anywhere** that varies *backslopped brine* proportion (e.g. 1 % vs 5 % vs 10 %) and measures fermentation time, and **no study** that varies defined-starter dose on a log scale (10⁵ vs 10⁶ vs 10⁷ CFU/mL) for a vegetable and reports lag phase. The only genuine dose–response is B1 (carrot juice, 1/2/3 % v/v), and it shows **sub-proportional, non-monotonic** benefit. The published practice level for defined starters clusters at **10⁶–10⁷ CFU/g**, with the 10⁶ CFU/g choice in the USDA work explicitly justified as "approximately 2 logs higher than the natural microflora."

Hamburg, p. 1294–1309, citing Holzapfel 1997; Vermote PhD thesis, VUB, p. 399–402, citing Vinicius de Melo Pereira et al. 2020) but **both are narrative reviews citing reviews — no measured data.**
2. **No numeric time-to-pH-4.0 or time-to-pH-3.5, and no lag-phase-in-hours, for sauerkraut under any inoculation regime.** Every sauerkraut head-to-head I retrieved (A1, A3, A5, A11, A12) reports pH as a figure, not a value, or reports only endpoints.
3. **No "days saved" figure for sauerkraut.** The only quantified days-saved value in this entire search is A4 (5–7 days, Chinese cabbage suancai/radish kimchi with *L. mesenteroides* AA001).
4. **Tolonen et al. 2004 (Food Microbiology) and Wiander & Ryhänen 2005 (Eur Food Res Technol) full texts not obtained** — both paywalled; abstracts contain no pH-time numbers. These are the two most-cited Finnish sauerkraut starter papers and the most likely place a "days saved" figure exists.
5. **Hu et al. 2021 (Food Res Int 148:110605) and Food Control 41:122–127 (2014) not obtained** — paywalled; likely contain quantitative acidification curves for Chinese sauerkraut.
6. **No dose–response study on backslopped brine proportion** (1 % vs 5 % vs 10 % v/v) measuring lag phase or fermentation time, for any vegetable.
7. **No dose–response study on starter CFU/mL (10⁵ vs 10⁶ vs 10⁷) reporting lag phase** for a whole fermented vegetable. The 10⁵/10⁶/10⁷ comparison literature exists for milk and model broth, not for cabbage/kimchi/cucumber.
8. **No head-to-head starter-vs-wild trial retrieved for beetroot or carrot (whole vegetable).** Carrot is covered only as juice (B1) and carrot juice is used as a starter-resuscitation medium in the 2026 cucumber methods paper (Santos et al., J Food Sci, doi:10.1111/1750-3841.71363), which is a methods paper, not a comparison.
9. **Di Cagno et al. 2008** ("Selection and use of autochthonous mixed starter for lactic acid fermentation of carrots, French beans or marrows", Food Microbiology) — identified via AGRIS/citation, full text not retrieved; would be the best carrot head-to-head if obtainable.
10. **No published head-to-head for cucumber pickles comparing a commercial defined starter against spontaneous fermentation with reported days-to-pH.** The 1964 Etchells work (A9) used sterilised cucumbers (no spontaneous arm). Recent USDA cucumber starter work (Anekella & Pérez-Díaz 2020, J Food Sci 85(10):3487–3467; Zhai et al. 2018, J Appl Microbiol 124(1):197–208) characterises and applies starters but I found no acidification-speed comparison against a wild control in the retrievable text.
11. **Mixed evidence on direction of effect.** Two studies (A6 kimchi 10 °C; A10 1968 sauerkraut) found starter cultures *slowed* acidification — A6 by ~1.5×, deliberately, to extend shelf-life. Any claim that "starters always shorten fermentation" is contradicted by the retrieved literature.
12. **No industry-funding disclosure retrievable** for most items (abstracts only). A4 has a state research institute co-author (Sichuan Academy of Food and Fermentation Industries); B2 was produced by a commercial producer (Yunnan Hongbin Green Food Group) — neither is a starter-culture vendor.

### Evidence-quality key used above
- **STRONG** — retrieved full text, replicated design, real vegetable, explicit numeric outcome, no evident commercial conflict.
- **MODERATE** — retrieved full text or abstract with numbers, but with a design limitation (endpoint-only, model medium, single strain, commercial samples) or numbers available only in abstract form.
- **WEAK / contested** — thesis-level, historical, single unreplicated statement, or contradicted by another retrieved study.
- **Marketing claims / vendor material: none used.** No starter-supplier literature was relied on for any number in this document.
